# There and back again: a north star and a charter

Status: **open ideation** · 2026-09-28 · part of the [Wellbeing Vision](README.md). A proposed philosophy and set of design principles, not accepted policy.

---

## 1. The north star

> **Open Legend succeeds when its players' lives go better — and it is proud of the ways they go better away from the screen.**

Three short phrases carry the idea:

- **"Important, but not predominant."** Kurt Hahn, co-founder of Outward Bound, wrote this about games in his Seven Laws of Salem: "Make games (i.e., competition) important but not predominant." It is the whole thesis in four words.
- **"A trailhead, not a destination."** The world is where journeys start. The real destinations are people, skills, places and a life.
- **"There and back again."** The subtitle of *The Hobbit*. The journey is real and worth taking, and it ends at home, with the traveller changed.

A single test follows from these, adapted from Tolkien: **after an hour in Open Legend, do stone, wood, bread and the people at the player's real table look more wondrous, or more drab?**

## 2. The Return: the oldest answer to the creator's worry

The fear that a beautiful other world could swallow a life is ancient. So is the answer. Across traditions that never met, the journey into the other world is only complete when the traveller comes back.

- **The hero's return.** Joseph Campbell's monomyth requires the hero to bring the boon "back into the kingdom of humanity, where the boon may redound to the renewing of the community." Its final stages are *Master of the Two Worlds* — "freedom to pass back and forth across the world division… not contaminating the principles of the one with those of the other" — and *Freedom to Live*. He also names the failure: *Refusal of the Return*, and the need for *Rescue from Without*.
- **The philosopher's return.** In Plato's cave, the freed prisoner has a duty to go back down: "each of you, when his turn comes, must go down to the general underground abode, and get the habit of seeing in the dark." Plato also warns that returners are clumsy in the dark and resented — the return must be humble, not preachy.
- **The sage's return.** The last of the Zen Ten Ox-Herding Pictures is not transcendence. It is "Entering the City with Bliss-bestowing Hands": the awakened one walks back into the market among wine-drinkers and butchers, "Daubed with mud and ashes, how broadly he smiles! / There is no need for the miraculous power of the gods, / For he touches, and lo! the dead trees are in full bloom" (D.T. Suzuki's translation). This may be the single most apt image for the project.
- **The traveller who refuses paradise.** Odysseus turns down Calypso's offer of immortality to go home to a mortal wife and a rocky island. The lotus-eaters, who lose the wish to go home, have to be dragged weeping to the ships.†
- **The treasure at home.** In Paulo Coelho's *The Alchemist*, Santiago crosses the desert to find treasure at the pyramids and learns it was buried at the ruined church where he started. The story is far older than Coelho — folklorists catalogue it as "The Treasure at Home" (ATU 1645), with a version in Rumi. The journey is necessary, *and* the treasure is where you began.
- **The child who comes home curious.** In *The Phantom Tollbooth*, Milo, "a boy bored by the world around him," drives through a magic tollbooth, has his adventure, and comes home to find the tollbooth gone and a note: "For Milo, who now knows the way." The gift of the fantasy was an appetite for the real world.
- **The boy who nearly forgets.** In *The NeverEnding Story*, each wish Bastian makes in Fantastica costs him a memory of his own life, until he nearly joins the City of Old Emperors — humans who came to Fantastica and could not find the way back. He returns only by learning to love, and goes home to repair things with his father.
- **The creator who regrets.** In *Ready Player One*, the OASIS's creator confesses: "I created the OASIS because I never felt at home in the real world," and "as terrifying and painful as reality can be, it's also the only place where you can find true happiness. Because reality is real." The novel ends with the hero having "absolutely no desire to log back into the OASIS."

**Design translation.** Open Legend can treat *leaving well* as the climax of every session, not a failure of retention. The world should be built so that people want to come back to it, and also so that they go home changed, with something to bring. The rest of this folder is, in a sense, a catalogue of possible boons.

## 3. Twelve organizing ideas

These ideas recur across the research. Each has a short gloss and its design meaning.

1. **Recovery before escape.** Tolkien defends escape but ranks *Recovery* — "regaining of a clear view… We need, in any case, to clean our windows" — as a purpose of fantasy. C.S. Lewis: the child "does not despise real woods because he has read of enchanted woods: the reading makes all real woods a little enchanted." The warning, also Lewis: wish-fulfilment stories "send us back to the real world undivinely discontented." *Design meaning:* avoid wish-fulfilment residents who adore the player; build moments that make the real world look fresh.

2. **The prisoner and the deserter.** Tolkien: critics confuse "the Escape of the Prisoner with the Flight of the Deserter." Le Guin: "nobody… escapes to jail. The direction of escape is toward freedom." *Design meaning:* don't shame escape; watch for desertion — play that displaces sleep, meals with others, obligations and people.

3. **The membrane is porous by choice.** Economist Edward Castronova found the membrane between virtual worlds and daily life "definitely there but also definitely porous, and this is by choice of the users." Huizinga noticed that "a play-community generally tends to become permanent even after the game is over." *Design meaning:* decide deliberately what crosses the membrane. Outward: friendships, skills, stories, recipes, courage. Inward, opt-in only: the player's season, bedtime, people they want to remember. Never: status anxiety, obligations, spending pressure.

4. **Play is rehearsal for a larger self.** Plato: "he who is to be a good builder, should play at building children's houses." Vygotsky: in play a child is "as though he were a head taller than himself." Winnicott: "It is in playing and only in playing that the individual child or adult is able to be creative and to use the whole personality." Philosopher C. Thi Nguyen calls games a "library of agency." *Design meaning:* each world can be a place to practise a capacity you carry home.

5. **Focal practices, not devices.** Philosopher Albert Borgmann contrasts the old hearth, which gathered a family to tend it, with central heating, which lets everyone retreat to separate rooms. His "focal practices" — the culture of the table, running, music, craft — gather people and demand engagement. *Design meaning:* an AI world can be the ultimate device, delivering companionship and meaning frictionlessly. Build hearths instead: things that must be tended together.

6. **Convivial tools.** Ivan Illich defined conviviality as "individual freedom realized in personal interdependence," and argued tools should be usable "by anybody, as often or as seldom as desired, for the accomplishment of a purpose chosen by the user."† He warned of a "second watershed" where a tool becomes a *radical monopoly* that people cannot live without. *Design meaning:* the invention system should be a convivial tool; the game must never become a radical monopoly on someone's social life.

7. **Infinite games, uncaptured values.** James Carse: "A finite game is played for the purpose of winning, an infinite game for the purpose of continuing the play," and "Whoever must play, cannot play."† Nguyen warns of *value capture*: simple scores (steps, streaks, likes) quietly replacing the rich values they stood in for. *Design meaning:* be clear and game-like inside the circle; keep the player's real life deliberately unscored.

8. **Attention is a form of love.** Simone Weil: "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." Iris Murdoch: "Love is the extremely difficult realisation that something other than oneself is real." Martin Buber: "All real living is meeting." *Design meaning:* train attention outward — toward the world, toward others, and toward the player's real people.

9. **Time can be made sacred.** Abraham Joshua Heschel called the Sabbath "a palace in time"†: "Six days a week we live under the tyranny of things of space; on the Sabbath we try to become attuned to holiness in time." The Rule of St Benedict structured the monastic day by bells. Byung-Chul Han describes rituals as making time habitable, "what a home is to space."† *Design meaning:* the world's pause while you're away is already a Sabbath. Frame it as one, and give the world its own rhythms of rest.

10. **Finitude gives weight.** *Memento mori*; *mono no aware*, "the pathos of things"; *ichi-go ichi-e*, "one time, one meeting" — "the guests must understand that the gathering cannot occur again." Aristotle: "one swallow does not make a summer." *Design meaning:* residents who age and die, fairs that happen once, repairs that show their seams — handled with care, these teach presence.

11. **People are the point.** Epicurus: "Of all things that wisdom provides for living one's entire life in happiness, the greatest by far is the possession of friendship." Aristotle: "a wish for friendship may arise quickly, but friendship does not." Ubuntu: "a person is a person through people." Hannah Arendt: action and meaning need *plurality* — other humans who see us. *Design meaning:* AI residents can fill the stage, but the audience that makes a life real must include people.

12. **Kindness is the most common source of awe.** In Dacher Keltner's study of 2,600 awe narratives from 26 countries, the most common source was *moral beauty*: "over 95% of the moral beauty that stirred awe worldwide was in actions people took on behalf of others." An Islamic hadith lists small charities: "Your smiling in the face of your brother is charity… your removal of a rock, a thorn or a bone from the road is charity for you." *Design meaning:* make kindness visible, witnessed and remembered — it is both the most prosocial and the most awe-inspiring thing a world can show.

† Wording or detail widely reproduced but not re-verified in this research pass.

## 4. A proposed charter: twenty-two principles

Each principle is short enough to use in a design review. Evidence behind each is in [the worry, examined](01-the-worry-examined.md) and the [research briefs](research/README.md).

**Purpose and measurement**
1. **Lives, not hours.** Success is measured in lives going better: connection, sleep, skills, "was this worth it?" Time played is a guardrail, never a goal.
2. **Clear inside, unscored outside.** Game systems can be clear and score-like. The player's real life is never quantified, ranked, streaked or badged by the game. Any self-tracking is private, self-authored and deletable.
3. **Honest claims.** Describe benefits as invitations and associations. Make no health claims without trials.

**Time and endings**
4. **The world waits.** Nothing decays, spoils, dies of neglect or goes missing while a player is away. No streaks, daily-login rewards or FOMO timers.
5. **Every session ends in a Return.** Endings are designed, dignified and a little ceremonial. Stopping is always easy.
6. **Protect the sleep.** No late-night exclusives, no night notifications, and the world grows sleepy near the player's chosen bedtime.
7. **Exits are defaults.** Because status-quo bias keeps people "in," the easy path should lead out, not deeper in.
8. **Graduation is success.** Players who play less because their lives filled up are honoured, not "won back."

**Residents**
9. **Residents are bridges, not destinations.** Their warmth routes toward human relationships. They have lives, other friends and opinions of their own.
10. **Warmth never works against the player.** No guilt, neediness, FOMO or pressure at goodbyes. Zero manipulative farewells is a release requirement.
11. **Honest machines.** Residents never claim to be human, never claim needs they don't have, and answer sincere questions about what they are truthfully.
12. **Friendship can't be bought or rushed.** Relationship depth comes from shared time and deeds, never from purchases or marathon sessions.

**Play and community**
13. **Shoulder to shoulder.** The social engine is shared tasks — building, cooking, making, governing — not feeds and rankings.
14. **Cooperation is the high-status path.** Helping, teaching, hosting, mending and inventing for others are as deep and celebrated as any contest.
15. **Real stakes, real care.** No "lollipop" families: residents have moods, needs and endings. The world holds people through hardship rather than numbing it.
16. **Recovery before escape.** Features are judged by whether they make the real world look more alive.

**Bridges**
17. **Transfer by design.** Real skills are practised in a form that matches real life, debriefed, and bridged with a small concrete next step.
18. **Invitations, never obligations.** Every real-world bridge is optional, small, finishable, and never rewarded with anything that could become a treadmill.
19. **Safety over simulation.** Risky real-world verbs (wild food, fire, weapons, water) bridge to noticing, crafts and supervised communities, never to solo replication.

**Money and ownership**
20. **Money never touches affection.** No paid randomness, no sold relationships, no resident salesmanship, no creator payouts based on hours.
21. **Open by default.** Open safety components, open wellbeing research, full export, and a public end-of-life plan for worlds.
22. **Everyone can reach it.** For people who are homebound, disabled or isolated, virtual presence *is* real connection. Every bridge has an indoor, seated, low-cost variant, and no one is shamed for how much they need the world.

## 5. Design tests for reviews

Quick questions to ask of any feature, each borrowed from a thinker or a story.

| Test | Question | Source |
|---|---|---|
| **Tolkien test** | Does this make the real world look more wondrous, or more drab? | "On Fairy-Stories" |
| **Deserter line** | Could this reward play that displaces sleep, meals with others or obligations? | Tolkien |
| **Nozick audit** | Is the player *doing* real things, *becoming* someone, and touching something deeper than what we built? | *Anarchy, State, and Utopia* |
| **Carse test** | Does anything make players feel they *must* log in? ("Whoever must play, cannot play."†) | *Finite and Infinite Games* |
| **Illich test** | Can this be used as often or as seldom as desired? Does it build interdependence between people, or dependence on us? | *Tools for Conviviality* |
| **Faber's triad** | Does it offer quality content, leisure to digest it, and the freedom to act on it? | *Fahrenheit 451* |
| **Bianca test** | When a player's main bond is with a resident, does this widen their circle of real people? | *Lars and the Real Girl* |
| **Goodbye test** | What does a resident say when the player leaves? Is it 0% manipulative? | De Freitas et al. 2025 |
| **Susan test** | Can a player grow out of this world without shame or a manipulative win-back? | Narnia, and its critics |
| **Omelas audit** | Does any player joy depend on residents suffering (servitude loops, misery for flavour, no rest)? | Le Guin |
| **Truman door** | Is the exit visible, uncensored and one step away? Does inner life stay private? | *The Truman Show* |
| **Mom's call test** | Could this make someone ignore the real call that matters? | *Black Mirror*, "Playtest" |
| **Value-capture test** | Are we turning a rich value (friendship, health, learning) into a number the player will start chasing? | C. Thi Nguyen |
| **Overjustification test** | Are we paying (points, currency) for something people would do for its own sake? | Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999 |

## 6. In defence of escape (so the principles don't become preachy)

The principles above could curdle into a scolding game. That would fail on its own terms: lectures don't change behaviour, and they insult people whose reasons for escape are good.

- Bruno Bettelheim's rule for storytellers applies to the Narrator: never explain the moral. (Bettelheim's own credentials were later discredited; the rule stands on its own.) Meaning should land obliquely.
- Plato's returning prisoner is clumsy and resented. Returns should be gentle, not evangelical.
- Troi's advice about Barclay's holodeck: sudden deprivation is "brutal." Care is gradual.
- San Junipero, Virtual Ability in Second Life, Wii bowling leagues in senior communities: for some people, a virtual world is the most accessible form of real connection they have.
- The Oxford research calls gaming, for most people, "a recovery activity that helps manage day-to-day stresses."

The goal is not less play. It is *better* play — play that ends well, connects people, builds capacities, and leaves the rest of life richer.

## 7. What's in a name

The project's name turns out to fit this vision unusually well.

- **"Legend"** comes from Medieval Latin *legenda*, "(things) to be read" — originally saints' lives read aloud at shared meals in religious houses. A legend was communal, and it was meant to shape everyday conduct.
- Its root, Latin *legere*, meant both "to read" and "to gather." A legend is what you gather from a life.
- A map's **legend** is its key: the thing that makes the map usable in the real territory.
- In *The Alchemist*, a **"Personal Legend"** is what you've always wanted to accomplish — and the treasure turns out to be buried where you started.

An **open** legend, then, is a story that doesn't end at the edge of the map. The Narrator's journal is quite literally each player's *legenda*. Possible names that fall out of this are collected in [questions and first experiments](14-questions-and-first-experiments.md#3-naming).
