# Ideas: growth, learning and real goals

Status: **open ideation** · 2026-09-28 · part of the [Wellbeing Vision](README.md). Tags: **Now / Next / Later / Moonshot**; evidence **[Strong] [Moderate] [Weak] [Wisdom] [Idea]**.

Can a game help people succeed at their real goals, learn real skills and grow wiser? The honest answer from the research: yes, but only when it is designed for it. Games teach what they make you do, transfer is narrow unless it is deliberately bridged, and debriefing is where transfer happens ([learning brief](research/08-learning-transfer-skills.md)). General "brain training" doesn't work, and Lumosity settled Federal Trade Commission charges over claiming it did. So this catalogue focuses on **specific, named capacities** that Open Legend's residents, worlds and invention system are unusually good at building.

---

## A. Worlds as a library of agencies

### 1. Each world practises one capacity you bring home
*Every world is designed around a way of acting in the world, and the Narrator names it at the end of an arc.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- Philosopher C. Thi Nguyen describes games as a "library of agency": each one lets us try on a different way of acting. A proposed mapping for Open Legend's worlds:

| World | Capacity practised | Carried home as |
|---|---|---|
| Primitive wilderness | Resourcefulness; calm when afraid | "I can figure things out"; steadiness in a crisis |
| Threewater March | Neighbourliness, craft, shared governance | Hosting, making, showing up for a commons |
| Linden Reach (contemporary city) | Adult life: work, money, relationships, civic action | Rehearsed conversations, real plans, local involvement |
| Living fantasy | Wonder and courage | Seeing the ordinary freshly (Tolkien's Recovery) |
| Planetary sci-fi | Imagining futures | Long-term thinking; "it doesn't have to be the way it is" (Le Guin) |

- At the end of an arc, the Narrator reflects in one sentence: *"Across these weeks, you became someone the camp asked for help."* Never a score, never a badge.

### 2. A head taller
*Mentor residents scaffold skills just beyond the player's current reach.* **Next · [Wisdom] + [Moderate]**
- Lev Vygotsky: in play a child is "as though he were a head taller than himself." Residents invite players to speak at the moot, mediate a quarrel, teach a child resident, lead a raising — the capacities people most want in real life and rarely get to practise safely. The Narrator then offers one small real-world analogue.

### 3. Roles that shape us well
*The most attractive roles in every world are roles of competence and care.* **Now · [Moderate]**
- Healer, teacher, host, maker, mediator, guide. The "Proteus effect" — behaviour shifting to match one's avatar — is small but consistent (r ≈ .22–.26 across 46 studies). Don't build the core loop around dominance or villainy; make care the high-status path.

## B. The Rehearsal Hall

### 4. The Rehearsal Hall
*Practise a real, difficult conversation with a resident configured to play the other person.* **Next · [Moderate] + [Strong] for the pattern**
- **What it looks like:** in Linden Reach (or any world, as a "dream" or play), a player sets up a scene: *asking my boss for a raise; telling my parents I'm changing careers; apologising to my sister; setting a boundary with a roommate; asking someone out; talking to my doctor about something I've been avoiding.* A resident plays the counterpart with realistic unpredictability and emotion. The player can pause, rewind, try again, swap roles, and get specific feedback. It ends with an explicit bridge: *"Want to schedule the real one?"* — and the Narrator asks, next time, how it went.
- **Why:**
  - Autistic adults who practised job interviews in a simulator had 7.82× the odds of landing a competitive job six months later ([Smith et al. 2015](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10803-015-2470-1); small sample).
  - In a small study, after practising a simulated conflict with a language model, people used competitive tactics 67% less and cooperative tactics twice as often as a lecture-trained group ([Shaikh et al., CHI 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.12309)).
  - VR exposure for public-speaking anxiety is roughly comparable to in-person exposure in meta-analyses.
  - Augusto Boal's Theatre of the Oppressed treats theatre as rehearsal for real life.
  - Common Sense Media found 39% of teens who used AI companions had carried skills practised with the AI into real life — starting conversations, giving advice, expressing emotions.
- **Design rules:**
  - Match the modality: spoken and timed builds performance; multiple-choice dialogue builds only recognition.
  - Always end with a real-world bridge and a later debrief.
  - Never clinical: no trauma re-enactment. Hard cases route to real help.
  - Role reversal: let the player voice the *other* person — whose resident memories make the perspective rich (a technique borrowed from psychodrama).

### 5. Counsel yourself as the elder
*The player steps into a wise elder resident's body to advise themselves on a real dilemma, then steps back.* **Later · [Weak] + [Moderate]**
- In a small VR study, men who alternated between their own avatar and Freud's body to counsel themselves improved their mood more than those who counselled themselves as themselves ([Osimo et al. 2015](https://www.nature.com/articles/srep13899)). People reason more wisely about others' problems than their own ("Solomon's paradox"), and taking a distanced third-person view removes the gap ([Grossmann & Kross 2014](https://doi.org/10.1177/0956797614535400)).

### 6. Wise-counsel quests
*Residents bring the player their dilemmas.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- A resident asks what to do about a feud, a debt, a love, a failing harvest. Advising others triggers wiser reasoning. An optional private prompt afterwards: *"Is there something like this in your own life?"*

### 7. Walk in their shoes
*Live a day as another resident — an elder, a newcomer, a struggling apprentice — with their memories and needs.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- In a VR study of becoming homeless, participants' self-reported empathy rose no more than a control group's, but significantly more of them signed an affordable-housing petition, and their attitudes lasted longer ([Herrera et al. 2018](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0204494)). Offer optional real-world actions afterwards; don't market "empathy gains."

## C. Goals, habits and follow-through

### 8. WOOP at the Wishing Well
*A mentor resident guides the player through a real goal: Wish, Outcome, Obstacle, Plan.* **Next · [Strong]**
- Mental contrasting with implementation intentions (WOOP) has an effect of about g = .34 across 21 articles and 15,907 people, and **guided versions beat written worksheets** (.47 vs .28) ([Wang et al. 2021](https://www.frontiersin.org/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2021.565202/full)) — a natural job for a conversational mentor. The plan ends as an "if-then" sentence: *"When I get home from work, I will put on my running shoes."* Implementation intentions have one of the most replicated effects in behaviour-change research.
- Check-ins happen in the journal, gently and only if the player wants.

### 9. The fresh-start calendar
*Festivals and "new chapter" ceremonies line up with real fresh starts.* **Now · [Moderate]**
- Mondays, the first of the month, the player's birthday, the anniversary of joining. People are more likely to start new goals at these "notional boundaries," which put distance between them and past failures ([Dai, Milkman & Riis 2014](https://faculty.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2014/06/Dai_Fresh_Start_2014_Mgmt_Sci.pdf)). Offer the ceremony; never force it.

### 10. The habit garden
*A small garden in the world grows as the player logs one tiny real habit — and a missed day wilts nothing.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Anchored to an existing routine ("After I pour my coffee, I will stretch for one minute"), followed by a small celebration (BJ Fogg's Tiny Habits). Habit formation took a median of about two months, with a wide range, and **"missing one opportunity… did not materially affect"** it ([Lally et al. 2010](https://api.openalex.org/works/doi:10.1002/ejsp.674)). In a megastudy of 54 gym nudges, the best performer was a small reward for *returning after a missed workout* ([Milkman et al. 2021](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04128-4)) — so the garden celebrates coming back, not unbroken streaks.
- **Watch out:** only 8% of those nudges still worked after four weeks, and experts couldn't predict which ones would. Test everything; rotate novelty; never tie habits to anything that could become a treadmill.

### 11. The day's work (behavioural activation, gently)
*A loop borrowed from a proven depression treatment, framed as ordinary life in the village.* **Later · [Strong] clinically; [Idea] as a game feature**
- Behavioural activation — scheduling valued activities, noticing their effect on mood, scheduling the next — was as effective as cognitive behavioural therapy at 12 months when delivered by junior workers (the COBRA trial). A non-clinical version: at the day's end, the Narrator asks what you'd like tomorrow to hold, in-world and out. **Never** marketed as treatment without trials.

### 12. Franklin's little book
*A private, self-authored virtue ledger.* **Now · [Wisdom]**
- Benjamin Franklin kept a small book with a column for each day, marking his lapses against each of 13 virtues and focusing on one virtue a week. "I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish."† It worked because it was **private, self-authored and cyclical**. The journal can offer the same, and nothing else — no scores, badges or sharing.

### 13. Playify, don't gamify
*Invitations to bring a playful attitude to ordinary life, with no points attached.* **Now · [Wisdom]**
- Bernard Suits defined playing a game as "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles." Ian Bogost's *Play Anything* argues that play is careful engagement with the limits of ordinary situations. Occasional suggestions: walk to the shop by a route you've never taken; cook with five ingredients; write a letter by hand; eat one meal without a screen. The opposite of gamification: no metrics, just a lighter way of seeing.

### 14. The play history
*Onboarding includes a conversation with an elder: "What did you love to do as a child?"* **Now · [Wisdom]**
- Psychiatrist Stuart Brown collected "play histories" and found play deprivation where lives had gone wrong. The answer seeds in-world affordances — and, much later and gently, real ones: someone who loved climbing trees might love a climbing wall; someone who loved building forts, a Men's Shed.

### 15. What lights you up (hobby discovery)
*The world notices what a player loves doing and occasionally names a real-world cousin.* **Next · [Idea]**
- "You've spent more evenings at the kiln than anywhere else. Have you ever tried a pottery class?" Based on *Designing Your Life*'s "Good Time Journal," which logs what gives you energy and engagement. Opt-in, rare, and never pushy.

## D. Self-knowledge and meaning

### 16. The Chronicle (your life as a story)
*The Narrator co-authors the player's story in chapters, and can hold real-life chapters too.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Psychologist Dan McAdams describes narrative identity as an "internalized, evolving story of the self"; redemptive stories (bad turning to good) go with wellbeing, and agency and connection are among the themes he traces. The Narrator can gently offer redemptive and agentic framings, without toxic positivity. A private page, "letters to the river," supports expressive writing about hard things — small, variable effects across 146 trials — with crisis resources always one tap away.
- **The legend is open:** the journal can be exported, printed or bound (see [moonshots](08-moonshots.md#the-bound-legend)).

### 17. The Seer's tent (best possible self)
*A short writing ritual: imagine your life a few years from now, having gone well.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- "Best possible self" exercises raised positive feelings and optimism in a meta-analysis (wellbeing d ≈ .33; shorter practice worked better) ([Carrillo et al. 2019](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0222386)).

### 18. Odyssey plans and prototype conversations
*Sketch three different possible lives, try each briefly in the world, then talk to a real person living one.* **Later · [Weak]**
- From Stanford's *Designing Your Life*: three alternative five-year "Odyssey Plans," then cheap tests — "prototype conversations" with people already living those lives. In Linden Reach, "virtual internships" with resident professionals can model how a profession thinks (David Williamson Shaffer's "epistemic games"). The quest completes only with a *real* conversation.

### 19. A letter from your future self
*A resident representing the player's older self, or a letter from them.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- One conversation with an AI-aged "future self" reduced anxiety and increased people's sense of continuity with their future selves ([MIT "Future You," 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.12514)); meeting age-progressed avatars made people allocate more to retirement in lab studies (Hershfield et al. 2011). The authors warn about over-reliance; keep it rare and reflective.

### 20. The values compass
*The player's recurring choices in the world are reflected back, and they decide whether those match what they value.* **Later · [Moderate] + [Wisdom]**
- Acceptance and Commitment Therapy treats values as "chosen life directions, not goals."† Aristotle: "we become just by doing just acts." The Narrator might observe, once in a long while, "You always share food first." The player decides what that means. Never scored.

### 21. The evening review and the view from above
*A Stoic spiritual exercise, offered in fiction.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- A bell tower or ridge from which a player sees the whole valley and its brief lives (Marcus Aurelius's "view from above"); an optional evening review in the journal: *What went well? What would I do differently?* Pierre Hadot called ancient philosophy "a series of spiritual exercises intended to transform the perception, and therefore the being, of those who practice it."

### 22. The gratitude post
*Letters written at the in-world post office can be exported or printed, and the Narrator encourages delivering them in person.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- In Martin Seligman's trial, a "gratitude visit" — writing and delivering a letter of thanks — gave the biggest short-term boost of several interventions. Letter writers underestimate how good recipients will feel (Kumar & Epley 2018†).

## E. Learning by design

### 23. Invention as constructionism: predict, test, explain, share
*The invention system becomes a way of thinking, not just a way of getting things.* **Now · [Moderate]**
- Before generating a new recipe, a mentor asks for a prediction and a reason ("Why will a longer cord throw farther?"). The engine validates and reports where reality disagreed. Good explanations are celebrated, not just working artifacts. This also keeps invention deliberate rather than a slot machine of re-rolls.
- **Lineage:** Seymour Papert's microworlds ("objects to think with," "hard fun"), Mitchel Resnick's Scratch and its design rule — **low floor, high ceiling, wide walls** — and Montessori's real tools and self-correcting materials. Plain-language invention is the floor; parametric tuning and scripting the ceiling; inventions in music, cooking, law, architecture and medicine make the walls wide.
- **Evidence caution:** learning to program transfers moderately, mostly to related skills (g ≈ .49); early Logo studies found no automatic gain in general planning. Transfer needs reflection.

### 24. Productive failure
*For key breakthroughs, players try and fail first; a mentor then consolidates.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Food preservation, water purification, irrigation: attempting a problem before instruction beats instruction-first for conceptual understanding (roughly g ≈ .36 in a meta-analysis of Manu Kapur's "productive failure").

### 25. Campfire debriefs
*After significant episodes, an elder offers a three-minute debrief.* **Next · [Strong] for debriefing**
- A failed hunt, a lost negotiation, a winter shortage. Four questions: What happened? Why? What would you try next? Where does this show up in your life? Feedback is the top-ranked feature of effective medical simulation; in the MIT Beer Game, players misread causes badly unless debriefed.

### 26. Two cases, one principle
*To teach a principle, show two surface-different cases and ask what they share.* **Next · [Strong] for analogical encoding**
- A grain-trade dispute in Threewater next to a roommate-utilities dispute in Linden Reach. Students who *compared* two negotiation cases were far more likely to use the underlying strategy in a later face-to-face negotiation ([Gentner, Loewenstein & Thompson 2003](https://doi.org/10.1037/0022-0663.95.2.393)).

### 27. One deep skill, many worlds
*Skills rhyme across worlds so they generalize.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- The commons is a fishing river, then a common pasture, then a shared apartment. Planning under delay is winter stores, then a harvest-to-market chain, then paycheck-to-rent timing. Varied surface forms plus explicit mapping is the known recipe for farther transfer.

### 28. Mentors who fade
*Mentor residents climb a hint ladder and step back as the player grows.* **Next · [Strong]**
- Question → nudge → worked example → partial solution. Mentors track *unassisted* success and fade (the "cognitive apprenticeship" model: modelling, coaching, scaffolding, articulation, reflection, exploration). Getting "just the answer" is allowed but visibly forgoes mastery.
- **Why this matters:** in a study of about 1,000 Turkish high-schoolers, unrestricted ChatGPT access raised practice scores 48% but *lowered* exam scores 17%; a teacher-designed tutor that withheld answers did no harm ([Bastani et al. 2025](https://hechingerreport.org/kids-chatgpt-worse-on-tests/)). A Harvard physics tutor engineered not to give answers away more than doubled learning gains versus an active-learning class ([Kestin et al. 2025](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-97652-6)).

### 29. Residents who quiz you
*Memory and spacing are built into ordinary conversation.* **Next · [Strong]**
- "Remind me which berries you said were safe?" Retrieval practice beats rereading; spacing deepens memory (Robert Bjork's "desirable difficulties"). Skills fade gently without use, so review becomes maintenance, not a chore.

### 30. Teach a resident
*Because residents' knowledge is separate from the engine, players can take apprentices.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- The apprentice asks "why?", voices plausible misconceptions and succeeds only as well as it was taught. Students worked harder learning *for* a teachable agent than for themselves, especially lower achievers (the "protégé effect," Chase et al. 2009†). Teaching becomes a core loop, and a world's knowledge spreads through its players.

### 31. The educator's kit
*Templates for teachers, with evaluation built in.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- Learning objectives, debrief scripts, research-backed defaults (multiple sessions, used alongside instruction, group play), configurable mentor styles ("Socratic," "worked examples first," "no answers," "immersion only"), a teacher dashboard that flags who's stuck (AI coaching for human tutors raised student mastery at about $20 per tutor per year), and opt-in pre/post measures. Eco, a game about ecology and law, began as a US Department of Education–funded tool.

## F. Language and heritage villages

### 32. Language villages
*Whole settlements where residents speak only the language you're learning, adapting to your level.* **Next · [Moderate] + [Strong] for the mechanism**
- Modelled on Concordia Language Villages (since 1961): a customs gate on arrival ("leave your language at the border"), passports, local currency, signs, and a skit before meals naming each food. Residents adjust speed, gesture and vocabulary to just beyond the learner's level (Krashen's "comprehensible input").
- **Quests that force output:** bargaining, asking directions, reporting a theft, courting — because *producing* language and negotiating meaning drive acquisition (Swain; Long). Residents give recasts and clarification requests, with optional explicit feedback afterwards.
- **Human tandem partners:** native speakers of each other's target languages share villages, with residents facilitating. Interest in chatbot conversation partners fades faster than in human partners (Fryer et al. 2017†), and real friendships can form.
- **Honest progress:** track input hours and "can-do" statements verified by tasks; flag the gap between understanding and speaking so players don't over-read their progress (Duolingo's own research found reading and listening gains but little grammar or speaking gain).
- **Precedent:** the DARPA-funded *Tactical Iraqi* had service members speak Iraqi Arabic with AI-driven characters in a 3D village in the mid-2000s.

### 33. Heritage villages
*Diaspora families build villages in their heritage language for their children.* **Later · [Idea]**
- Children of immigrants often lose their parents' language. A community-owned village — the grandmother's language, food, festivals and songs — lets a child live in it for an hour a week, with a real grandparent in a [Miranda Seat](04-ideas-residents-who-point-outward.md#18-a-seat-for-a-real-person-inside-someone-elses-story). Communities lead; the platform supplies tools. For endangered and Indigenous languages, only with the community's direction and consent.

## G. Life skills in the city world

### 34. Adulting with real numbers, just in time
*Budgets, leases, taxes, insurance and credit, with realistic figures, when the player faces the real decision.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- Financial education raises knowledge and behaviour modestly across 76 randomized trials ([Kaiser et al.](https://www.nber.org/papers/w27057)); it works best delivered close to the real decision. "Trial month" simulations for moving out, changing jobs, or taking a loan.

### 35. Drills that end in a real plan
*Occasional flood, fire or outage scenarios where players coordinate, then leave with a real household plan.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- Borrowed from emergency-management tabletop exercises, which always end in an after-action review and improvement plan. The bridge artifact is a checklist for the player's own home.

## H. Anti-patterns to avoid

- Brain-training claims or any "cognitive enhancement" marketing.
- Answer-machine mentors.
- Streaks that reward showing up rather than learning.
- Narrative wrappers bolted onto rote drills (a discovery game with a story wrapper produced *worse* learning than a slideshow in one study).
- A sequestered "Primer" that replaces real people.
- Overconfidence in dangerous skills: Norway's skid-training for novice drivers was linked to *more* slippery-road crashes. Skill without calibrated risk perception breeds overconfidence.
