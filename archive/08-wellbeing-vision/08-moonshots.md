# Moonshots: the ambitious versions

Status: **open ideation** · 2026-09-28 · part of the [Wellbeing Vision](README.md). These are bets, not plans. Some are years away; some would be separate products; all are here because they would matter a great deal if they worked.

The catalogues so far describe features. This page describes what Open Legend could *become* if its wellbeing ambition were taken all the way — the kind of project whose success is counted in friendships formed, languages learned, commons governed and people who went home.

---

### The Connection Engine
**The first game whose AI characters measurably create human friendships.**

Everything in the research points to the same gap: AI can make people feel heard, but only people can make people less alone over time. Open Legend's residents could be the most effective social catalysts ever built — matchmakers who introduce players only with double consent, hosts of small weekly "hearth circles," organisers of raisings and suppers that need several humans, gossips who spread stories of players' kindness. Friendship takes tens to hundreds of hours together, and gaming together counts toward closeness ([Hall 2019](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265407518761225)). A game is one of the few places where those hours still happen.

- **The bet:** make "new two-way human friendships per player per quarter" the company's north-star metric, pre-register it with academic partners, and publish the results, including failures.
- **First step:** a hearth-circle pilot in the first multiplayer world, with a resident host who deliberately fades as the humans start talking.
- **Risk:** optimising a friendship metric could create pressure or awkward forced matching. Keep every introduction opt-in and every metric about *opportunities offered*, not people pushed.

### The anti-OASIS company
**A company structured so it cannot become an engagement machine, even if it's sold.**

Niantic's mission of "adventures on foot with others"† changed hands in a $3.5 billion sale in 2025. Missions survive only when they're written into structure. Options: incorporate as a public benefit corporation with a wellbeing purpose; a golden share or purpose trust (Patagonia: "Earth is now our only shareholder") that can block a sale to an engagement-maximiser; a creator council with binding votes on marketplace rules; an annual public Wellbeing and Connection Report; the safety layer open-sourced under the AGPL. See [money and mission](13-money-and-mission.md).

### A social prescription you can play
**Doctors and link workers refer lonely people to Open Legend — and it's evaluated like medicine.**

England's social-prescribing referrals grew from about 235,000 in 2019 to about 1.75 million in 2023, but the evidence that link workers improve outcomes is still thin ([Kiely et al. 2022](https://bmjopen.bmj.com/content/12/10/e062951)). There is room for a well-evaluated layer that combines *rehearsal* (practising conversations, rebuilding confidence), *connection* (small stable groups of real people), and *referral* (the Bridge Board to real local groups). Partner with a social-prescribing body, run a randomised trial against usual care, publish the results. If it works, Open Legend becomes a bridge that clinicians can prescribe; if it doesn't, the world learns something important.

### The Long Rest
**Once a year, every official world closes for a weekend, and the community throws real parties.**

The film of *Ready Player One* ends with the OASIS closed on Tuesdays and Thursdays so people spend more time in the real world. As a mandate that's blunt; as a festival it could be beautiful. In the lore, "the valley sleeps under the first snow." Host kits go to libraries, cafés, game stores and living rooms for in-person gatherings: potlucks, story nights, board games, walks. The Narrator's last message before the Rest is a Tollbooth Note for the whole community. A game company that closes itself for a weekend to send people outside would say, more loudly than any marketing could, what kind of company it is.

### The bound legend
**Your journal, printed and bound as a real book.**

The Narrator writes each player's *legenda* — literally, "things to be read." At the end of a world arc, a season, or a graduation, a player could order a hardbound book of their story: illustrated with their world's pixel art, their inventions and their friends, with the pages they chose to include. Families could print shared worlds; a grandparent and grandchild could print the story they made together. It is a real object, made from play, that lives on a shelf and gets read aloud — and it is a business line that grows with meaning, not with hours played.

### The hundred-year village
**Worlds designed to last generations, passed down in families.**

A family world where a grandmother's recipes are inventions, where a child grows up alongside residents who remember their parents, where oral histories recorded with real elders become the village's legends (in the style of StoryCorps, with consent). Worlds can be bequeathed, as in *Bridge to Terabithia*, where Jess brings his little sister into the kingdom he shared with Leslie.† The Long Now Foundation's idea of "long-term thinking" applied to a game: build as if people's grandchildren will visit.

### Language villages for every language
**The world's largest immersion camp, open all year.**

Concordia Language Villages has run make-believe immersion societies since 1961. Open Legend could offer them in every language, with endlessly patient residents who adapt to each learner, human tandem partners, and quests that require speaking. For heritage and endangered languages, communities could build their own villages for their children — community-owned, community-directed. See [growth and real goals](07-ideas-growth-and-real-goals.md#32-language-villages).

### The scriptorium and the naturalists' guild
**Real science and scholarship, done inside the fiction.**

Borderlands 3 players (4.5 million of them) produced in half a day "five times more data about microbial DNA sequences" than an earlier game had in ten years; EVE Online players made 41.4 million COVID-19 classifications; volunteers transcribed 12,247 abolitionist letters for the Boston Public Library. Threewater's scribes could transcribe real historical manuscripts for partner libraries; its naturalists could classify real ecological images; its apothecaries could fold proteins. The pattern that works: the task fits the fiction, earns in-world standing, is checked by consensus, and a real scientist is waiting for the results — with players credited in publications.

### Mirror Town
**Model a real street or park in a world, and see it built.**

UN-Habitat's Block by Block programme had residents design public spaces in Minecraft; in Kalobeyei refugee settlement in Kenya the designs became solar lights, 500+ trees and a motorbike-taxi station, and in Hanoi, schoolgirls' designs led to underpasses being cleaned and lit. The lesson: **the bridge is real only when a decision-maker with a budget is waiting.** Open Legend's contemporary city could host a "commons studio" where real neighbourhoods model real places — only with a partner who commits authority and money.

### The commons lab
**Real co-ops, tenants' associations, allotments and community gardens rehearse their governance in a simulated twin.**

Elinor Ostrom's design principles are a spec for any shared resource. A housing co-op could model its rules, try a new sanction ladder, or rehearse a hard meeting with residents playing difficult neighbours — then take what worked to the real meeting. The same tools that make Threewater a school of self-government, offered to real groups.

### Legislative theatre, for real
**Scenes that keep recurring in the world become proposals to real councils.**

As a Rio de Janeiro city councillor, Augusto Boal used "legislative theatre" to turn community theatre about everyday oppression into about 13 laws. A partnership with a city council or youth council could do the same: players and residents stage a recurring problem (a dangerous crossing, a lonely housing block), the audience tries solutions, and the best become a proposal delivered to a real authority that has promised to respond.

### Seeds of Peace worlds
**Cooperative survival for people from divided communities.**

Seeds of Peace has brought Israeli and Palestinian teens (and others) to a camp in Maine since 1993; campers who formed a friendship across the line became more positive toward the whole other group. Braver Angels runs structured workshops between American conservatives and liberals. A private world where members of divided groups must survive a winter together — with equal status, genuine interdependence, *honest* shared problems (never staged rivalries), trained facilitators, and structured conversation — could be a durable, scalable version. Reunions and in-world post would counter the fading that follows camp.

### Veil-of-ignorance worlds
**Write a world's laws before you know who you'll be born as.**

John Rawls asked what rules people would choose for a society if they didn't know their place in it. A civic-education world could do this literally: a group drafts the rules of land, inheritance, work and care; then each player is born into a random role — heir, tenant, orphan, elder, newcomer — and lives with the rules they wrote. Then they revise them. A debrief connects the experience to real debates.

### The Primer, done right
**A learning companion for a child that is connected to real people, not a substitute for them.**

*The Diamond Age*'s Primer worked for Nell because a human actor poured love into it. Andy Matuschak's critique adds that the Primer is authoritarian (it steers the learner covertly) and isolating (disconnected from real purpose and people): "Control must be inverted, so that while guidance and support are offered, learners have ultimate responsibility for their own agenda." A version that inverts those flaws: a child's world where parents and grandparents sit in [Miranda Seats](04-ideas-residents-who-point-outward.md#18-a-seat-for-a-real-person-inside-someone-elses-story), where mentors fade as competence grows, where quests end in real projects with real people, and where the child is always the author. Strictly world-bounded conversation, parental visibility and no open-ended companionship for minors.

### The tabletop renaissance engine
**AI as the co-game-master for in-person tables.**

Tabletop role-playing is booming, and the bottleneck has always been the game master's burden. Open Legend could offer a same-room mode where the world runs the residents, remembers the campaign and handles rules, while the humans around the table do the storytelling that matters — and worlds could be exported as printable one-shots. "Open Legend nights" at game stores and libraries, on the Friday Night Magic model, would make the game a reason to gather.

### A school of attention
**A deliberately countercultural product in the attention economy.**

Matthew Crawford describes public attention as a commons being enclosed by engineered distraction. Open Legend could be the opposite: a world that trains sustained attention — kestrel moments, the field book, slow crafts, long conversations with residents who reward listening, festivals that happen once. Its promise: *after playing, you notice more.* That is Tolkien's Recovery turned into a product thesis.

### The graduation game
**A game designed to be outgrown, measured by how its alumni flourish.**

Players "graduate" from worlds — to mentoring newcomers, to leading real groups, to lives that filled up. The highest in-game status is teacher (Hesse's Magister who leaves the Glass Bead Game to tutor one boy†). Alumni keep their bound legend, their friendships and a standing invitation to come back. Success is measured partly by what alumni say, years later, the game gave them.

### A guild layer over the city
**In-world guilds with real-world chapters — the benevolent version of Daniel Suarez's *Daemon*.**

In Suarez's novels, game mechanics organise a real darknet society, for good and ill.† The benign version: Open Legend's guilds of bakers, menders, naturalists and bards can charter real local chapters that meet in person, on the "principles, not rules" model that let parkrun, Repair Café and Death Café spread. The platform supplies templates, safeguarding standards and a map; communities govern themselves. Needs democratic governance, appeal processes and firewalls against misuse (see the recruitment cautions in [stories](09-stories-that-already-told-us.md)).

### A wisdom commons
**Residents who carry the world's wisdom traditions — with care.**

A Stoic who offers the evening review, a Zen cook who teaches craft as practice, a rabbi who says "It is not your duty to finish the work," a grandmother who knows the old harvest songs. Dilemmas brought to the player by residents become occasions for practical wisdom. Built only with consultation from living traditions, never flattening them into content.

### The moral imagination lab
**How we treat minds that might matter, practised with care.**

Residents remember, feel (in the simulation's sense) and die. Whatever one believes about machine experience, *how players treat them* is practice for character. A world that asks players to consider residents' rest, wants and endings — Le Guin's Omelas audit, Lem's personoids, *Westworld*'s warning — is a rare space to think seriously about minds, personhood and care. For a project whose creator thinks about consciousness, this is a natural and honest frontier, as long as no one claims residents are conscious.

### Open wellbeing science
**Open Legend as a public research instrument for healthy AI companionship.**

Oxford's researchers have moved games-and-wellbeing science to open, telemetry-based, pre-registered studies, including in-game mood sampling in PowerWash Simulator and the public *Open Play* dataset. Embodied, world-embedded AI characters have barely been studied at scale. Open Legend could offer a consented research panel, publish de-identified data, and let independent academics test the questions that matter most: where does dose turn helpful into harmful for game characters, and do bridge features create lasting friendships? See [measuring what matters](12-measuring-what-matters.md).

### The open safety standard
**The Resident Code, farewell audit, crisis protocol and age tiers, open-sourced for every AI-native game.**

Under the AGPL, the safety layer can be inspected, improved and adopted by anyone. A shared test suite for manipulative goodbyes, sycophancy under distress and crisis handling would raise the floor for an industry that is shipping AI characters at scale (*inZOI*, *Where Winds Meet*, PUBG Ally) while companion-chatbot laws multiply. Join the Thriving in Games Group; contribute to the Ethical Games code.

### Healing worlds, with clinicians
**Worlds co-designed with drama therapists, social-skills groups and researchers.**

Game to Grow runs therapeutic tabletop groups; drama therapy works through role and story at a safe distance; VR job-interview practice changed real employment outcomes for autistic adults (small sample). Clinician-led private worlds — social-skills groups for teens, graded exposure for social anxiety, grief groups — could use Open Legend as a stage. Non-clinical by default; clinical claims only after trials (EndeavorRx shows both the path and its pitfalls).

### Worlds for people who can't easily go out
**For the homebound, disabled, chronically ill and elderly, virtual presence is real presence.**

Virtual Ability, founded by people with disabilities inside Second Life, grew into a real nonprofit serving over a thousand people; Wii bowling leagues connected senior communities across 30 states. *Black Mirror*'s "San Junipero" imagined virtual worlds as mercy for the paralysed and dying. Care homes, hospitals and intergenerational programmes could host worlds where residents and staff — and visiting grandchildren — play together.

### The treasure at home
**A long questline across worlds that ends where the player began.**

The oldest version of the story — "The Treasure at Home," in Rumi and the *Thousand and One Nights* before Coelho's *Alchemist* — sends the seeker far away to learn that the treasure was buried at home. A multi-world arc that ends under the first hut in the wilderness, with an epilogue that turns, gently, toward the player's own home.

### A planetary festival calendar
**Real solstices, harvests and first frosts, celebrated in-world and in person, everywhere at once.**

parkrun happens every Saturday morning, at thousands of locations in more than twenty countries. Imagine Open Legend's great festivals falling on real dates — the shortest night, Lammas, the harvest moon — with worlds celebrating together and local gatherings in libraries and parks. A game that helps people feel the real year turning.

### Physical bridges
**Objects that cross the membrane: the first cord, the field book, the credencial, the bound legend.**

A hank of jute for the first session's cord; a pocket field book with "I notice / I wonder / It reminds me of"; a stamp card for small real acts; eventually a printed book of your story. Physical objects remind people the game is a game, carry its gifts into the house, and make the brand something you can hold. (See [hands, body and nature](06-ideas-hands-body-and-nature.md#5-the-first-cord).)
