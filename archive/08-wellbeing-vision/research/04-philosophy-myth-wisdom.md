## PART 1: Ten organizing ideas

1. **The Return is the point of the journey.** Several traditions say the same thing:
   - Campbell's hero must bring the boon back "into the kingdom of humanity."
   - Plato's freed prisoner "must go down" into the cave again.
   - The tenth Zen ox-herding picture shows the sage walking back into the market with "bliss-bestowing hands."

   In each of these, a journey that never comes home is unfinished. Open Legend should treat *leaving the game well* as the climax of a session, not as a failure to retain the player.

2. **Recovery: fantasy should clean the windows, not paint over them.** Tolkien's Recovery means "to clean our windows; so that the things seen clearly may be freed from the drab blur of triteness or familiarity—from possessiveness." Lewis puts it this way: "the reading makes all real woods a little enchanted." That gives a simple test. After playing, do stone, bread and the people at the player's table look *more* luminous, or more drab?

3. **The prisoner escapes; the deserter flees.** Tolkien, Lewis ("jailers") and Le Guin ("The direction of escape is toward freedom") all defend escape. Tolkien's distinction supplies the design criterion. Some escape restores a person to their post. Other escape abandons it. Don Quixote and Emma Bovary are the cautionary readers, and Lewis names the failure: stories that send us back "undivinely discontented."

4. **The magic circle is porous, so the membrane has to be designed.** Huizinga's play-grounds are "forbidden spots, isolated, hedged round, hallowed." Yet he also says the play-community "tends to become permanent even after the game is over." Castronova found the membrane "definitely there but also definitely porous, and this is by choice of the users."
   - What should cross the membrane: friendship, skill, courage, stories.
   - What should not: compulsion, status anxiety, spending.

5. **Play is rehearsal for a larger self.**
   - Plato: the future builder "should play at building children's houses."
   - Vygotsky: in play the child is "as though he were a head taller than himself."
   - Winnicott: only in playing is a person "able to be creative and to use the whole personality."
   - Nguyen: games are a "library of agency."

   The value is in the *transfer*: capacities practiced in a safe world and then carried out of it.

6. **Focal practices and convivial tools, not devices.** Borgmann contrasts the family hearth with central heating. Illich defines conviviality as "individual freedom realized in personal interdependence." Crawford writes about the dignity of skilled engagement. The right texture for the world is making, tending and gathering, not frictionless consumption. The player-invention system should be an Illich-style convivial tool.

7. **Infinite games, not captured values.** Carse writes that "an infinite game [is played] for the purpose of continuing the play." Nguyen warns about *value capture* by scores. Bogost calls gamification "exploitationware." Wellbeing must never become another score.

8. **Attention is the practice of love.**
   - Weil: "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity."
   - Murdoch: "Love is the extremely difficult realisation that something other than oneself is real."
   - Buber: "All real living is meeting."

   The world should train attention *outward*, including toward the real people the player loves.

9. **Time can be made sacred: Sabbath, hours, festival, leisure.** Heschel wrote of "holiness in time," Benedict organized the monastic day by bells, Pieper described leisure as receptivity, Han describes rituals that make time habitable, and Epicurus's Garden held a monthly feast. Aristotle: "we are busy that we may have leisure." Open Legend already pauses the world when players leave. That is a Sabbath, and it should be *framed and ritualized as one*.

10. **Finitude gives weight.** The relevant ideas are:
    - memento mori (remember that you will die)
    - mono no aware ("the pathos of things")
    - ichi-go ichi-e: "a particular gathering will never be replicated"
    - kintsugi: breakage and repair as "part of the history of an object"
    - Aristotle: "one swallow does not make a summer"

    NPCs who age and die, a harvest fair that never happens the same way twice, and repairs that show their seams can all teach the player something. They are not simply sad features.

## PART 2: Detailed entries, grouped by tradition

### A. The literary theory of fantasy and escape

**A1. J.R.R. Tolkien, "On Fairy-Stories"**
*Lecture 1939; published 1947; revised in* Tree and Leaf, *1964.* Tolkien names four functions of fairy-stories: Fantasy (sub-creation), Recovery, Escape and Consolation. The highest form of Consolation is **eucatastrophe**, the sudden joyous turn.

Quotations [V, full text at ieas-szeged.hu]:
- "Recovery (which includes return and renewal of health) is a re-gaining—regaining of a clear view."
- "We need, in any case, to clean our windows; so that the things seen clearly may be freed from the drab blur of triteness or familiarity—from possessiveness."
- "It was in fairy-stories that I first divined the potency of the words, and the wonder of the things, such as stone, and wood, and iron; tree and grass; house and fire; bread and wine."
- "Why should a man be scorned if, finding himself in prison, he tries to get out and go home?" (*Tree and Leaf*, 1965, p. 60, per Quote Investigator)
- Critics "are confusing, not always by sincere error, the Escape of the Prisoner with the Flight of the Deserter."
- "And lastly there is the oldest and deepest desire, the Great Escape: the Escape from Death."
- The eucatastrophic joy "is a sudden and miraculous grace: never to be counted on to recur." It "denies (in the face of much evidence, if you will) universal final defeat and in so far is evangelium, giving a fleeting glimpse of Joy, Joy beyond the walls of the world, poignant as grief."
- "Fantasy is a natural human activity. It certainly does not destroy or even insult Reason; and it does not either blunt the appetite for, nor obscure the perception of, scientific verity."
- "Fantasy remains a human right: we make in our measure and in our derivative mode, because we are made."

*For Open Legend:*
- Tolkien's order matters. **Recovery comes before Escape.** A world true to Tolkien measures itself by whether real stone, wood, iron, bread and wine look more wondrous afterwards.
- Eucatastrophe is "never to be counted on to recur." Do not build a reward schedule of engineered joyful turns. Leave room for rare, unscheduled grace.
- The prisoner/deserter distinction is the best ready-made test for when a player's escape has become unhealthy.

**A2. C.S. Lewis**
- *"On Three Ways of Writing for Children"* (1952; in *Of Other Worlds*) [V, catholicculture.org]. The fairy tale "stirs and troubles him (to his life-long enrichment) with the dim sense of something beyond his reach and, far from dulling or emptying the actual world, gives it a new dimension of depth. He does not despise real woods because he has read of enchanted woods: the reading makes all real woods a little enchanted." Lewis contrasts this with realistic wish-fulfilment stories about popularity and success, which "send us back to the real world undivinely discontented" [V2 for the phrase].
- *"On Science Fiction"* (1955; *Of Other Worlds*, 2002 ed., p. 67) [V2, Quote Investigator]: "What class of men would you expect to be most preoccupied with, and most hostile to, the idea of escape?... jailers." Lewis presents this as a question Tolkien put to him [U].
- *Sehnsucht* ("Joy," in *Surprised by Joy*, 1955): "an unsatisfied desire which is itself more desirable than any other satisfaction" [U].

*For Open Legend:* Lewis supplies the sharpest diagnostic. The *dangerous* fantasy is the plausible wish-fulfilment: being popular, admired, chosen. It is dangerous because it makes real life look drab by comparison. Open Legend's AI residents could flatter and adore a player without limit, which is exactly this risk. Design NPCs who are *not* wish-fulfilment machines. Their regard should be earned, sometimes withheld, and should point outward.

**A3. G.K. Chesterton, "The Red Angel," in *Tremendous Trifles* (1909)**
[V2, Gaiman's Tumblr and excellence-in-literature.com]: "Fairy tales do not give the child his first idea of bogey. What fairy tales give the child is his first clear idea of the possible defeat of bogey. The baby has known the dragon intimately ever since he had an imagination. What the fairy tale provides for him is a St. George to kill the dragon." And: "Exactly what the fairy tale does is this: it accustoms him for a series of clear pictures to the idea that these limitless terrors had a limit..."

*For Open Legend:* In the wilderness-survival starting world, dangers exist to show the player "clear pictures" in which terrors turn out to have limits. That is rehearsal for resilience, not fear for its own sake. (The popular "dragons can be beaten" line is Gaiman's paraphrase; see Part 6.)

**A4. Ursula K. Le Guin**
- *"Why Are Americans Afraid of Dragons?"* (1974; in *The Language of the Night*, 1979) [V2, pressbooks excerpt]: "For fantasy is true, of course. It isn't factual, but it is true." / "They are afraid of dragons, because they are afraid of freedom."
- *Blog post "28. It Doesn't Have To Be the Way It Is"* (ursulakleguin.com; the page is dated June 2011) [V]: "Escape from real life, responsibility, order, duty, piety, is what the charge implies. But nobody, except the most criminally irresponsible or pitifully incompetent, escapes to jail. The direction of escape is toward freedom. So what is 'escapism' an accusation of?" She treats fantasy as asking what would happen if things "didn't go on just as they do" [V2].

*For Open Legend:* Fantasy's real gift is the *imagination of alternatives*: "It doesn't have to be the way it is." That is something a player can carry home: a lived sense that their street, habits or relationships could be otherwise. It pairs well with McGonigal's futures work in *Imaginable*.

**A5. Bruno Bettelheim, *The Uses of Enchantment* (1976)**
[V2, Wikipedia] Bettelheim argued that fairy tales help children work through separation anxiety, oedipal conflict and sibling rivalry. On method: "One must never 'explain' to the child the meaning of fairy tales. However, the narrator's understanding of the fairy tale's message to the child's preconscious mind is important."

*Caution:* Bettelheim's credentials turned out to be fabricated. In 1991 Alan Dundes (*Journal of American Folklore*) documented "wholesale borrowing" from Julius Heuscher. Cite the idea, not the man as an authority.

*For Open Legend:* This is a rule for the Narrator. **It never explains or moralizes the player's story.** Meaning should land obliquely.

**A6. Cervantes and Flaubert: the two great cautionary readers**
- *Don Quixote* (1605/1615). Alonso Quixano reads chivalric romances until he loses his wits. At the end, "By his deathbed, he has regained his sanity, and is once more 'Alonso Quixano the Good'" [V2, Wikipedia]. Many modern readers, Unamuno among them, also take Quixote as *ennobled*: mad, but brave and kind [P].
- *Bovarysme* (Jules de Gaultier, after *Madame Bovary*, 1857): "a tendency towards escapist daydreaming in which the dreamer imagines themself to be a hero or heroine in a romance, whilst ignoring the everyday realities of the situation" [V, Wikipedia definition].

*For Open Legend:* There are two failure modes to design against:
- **Quixote:** the player can no longer tell the frames apart.
- **Emma:** the romance makes real life unbearable by comparison.

Quixote's deathbed also gives a redemptive pattern: he recovers his true name, "the Good."

**A7. Paulo Coelho, *The Alchemist* (1988; English 1993): the "Personal Legend"**
[V2, Wikipedia] Melchizedek tells Santiago to sell his sheep and travel to Egypt to fulfil his "Personal Legend." Wikipedia gives the book's core line as "when you really want something to happen, the whole universe will conspire so that your wish comes true."

The ending: Santiago is robbed at the pyramids. One thief mocks him by describing his own dream of treasure "under a tree at a ruined church." That church is where Santiago's journey began, and the treasure was there all along. The story is folktale type **ATU 1645, "The Treasure at Home."** Its earliest known version is a 13th-century poem by Rumi, and a variant appears in the *1001 Nights*.

Coelho's definition [U]: "It's what you have always wanted to accomplish. Everyone, when they are young, knows what their Personal Legend is."

*For Open Legend:* The name can inherit Coelho's **ending** rather than his "universe conspires" slogan, which reads as magical thinking. The journey is real and necessary, *and* the treasure is buried where you started. Build on the ATU 1645 structure.

**A8. The etymology of "legend"** [V, etymonline]
Medieval Latin *legenda*, "legend, story," especially lives of saints, "which were formerly read at matins and in refectories of religious houses, literally '(things) to be read.'" It comes from *legere*, "to read; to gather, pluck, select," and ultimately from the Proto-Indo-European root *leg-*, "to collect, gather," whose derivatives also mean "to speak." The senses developed in this order:

| Period | Sense |
|---|---|
| early 14th c. | "narrative dealing with a happening or an event" |
| late 14th c. | "nonhistorical or mythical story" |
| 1610s | an inscription, especially on a coin or medal |
| 1903 | applied to "a map, illustration, etc." |
| 1958 | "a legend in (one's) own time" |

*For Open Legend:* The name carries four resonances:
- A legend is **read aloud at a shared table**, in the refectory: it is communal.
- *Legere* means **to gather**: a legend is what you gather from a life.
- A map legend is the **key** that makes the map usable in the real territory.
- Saints' legends were **exemplary lives** read to shape everyday conduct.

The Narrator's journal is, quite literally, each player's *legenda*.

### B. The philosophy of play

**B1. Johan Huizinga, *Homo Ludens* (1938; English 1949/1955)**
- **Definition** [V2, Labaree]. Play is "a free activity standing quite consciously outside 'ordinary' life as being 'not serious', but at the same time absorbing the player intensely and utterly. It is an activity connected with no material interest, and no profit can be gained by it." The definition goes on to fixed boundaries of time and space, fixed rules, and the formation of social groupings [U].
- **The magic circle** [V2, Wikipedia; 1955 ed., p. 10]: "All play moves and has its being within a play-ground marked off beforehand either materially or ideally, deliberately or as a matter of course... The arena, the card-table, the magic circle, the temple, the stage, the screen, the tennis court, the court of justice, etc, are all in form and function play-grounds, i.e. forbidden spots, isolated, hedged round, hallowed, within which special rules obtain." As a *design term*, "magic circle" was coined by Zimmerman and Lantz in 1999 and popularized in Salen and Zimmerman's *Rules of Play* (2003). Zimmerman later said they "more or less invented the concept."
- **The play-community** [V2, Labaree]: "A play-community generally tends to become permanent even after the game is over... the feeling of being 'apart together' in an exceptional situation, of sharing something important, of mutually withdrawing from the rest of the world and rejecting the usual norms, retains its magic beyond the duration of the individual game."
- **Decline** [V2]: "the play-element in culture has been on the wane ever since the 18th century... Civilization to-day is no longer played, and even where it still seems to play it is false play."
- **Puerilism** (*In the Shadow of Tomorrow*, 1935, p. 170) [V2, First Person Scholar]: "the attitude of a community whose behaviour is more immature than the state of its intellectual and critical faculties would warrant, which instead of making the boy into the man adapts its conduct to that of the adolescent age."

*For Open Legend:* Huizinga is both charter and warning. The charter is a bounded, hallowed space in which a lasting community forms. The warning is "false play" and puerilism: recreation that keeps adults adolescent. **Aim for play that "makes the boy into the man," play that matures people.**

**B2. Roger Caillois, *Man, Play and Games* (1958; English 1961)** [V2, Wikipedia]
Play is "free, or not obligatory"; "separate (from the routine of life)"; "uncertain"; "unproductive in that it creates no wealth"; "governed by rules"; and it involves "make-believe."

Caillois names four forms of play. Each has a characteristic corruption when it spills into ordinary life:

| Form | What it is | Corruption in ordinary life |
|---|---|---|
| **agon** | competition | violence and trickery |
| **alea** | chance; "a surrender to destiny" | superstition |
| **mimicry** | role-play | alienation |
| **ilinx** | vertigo; "voluptuous panic" | drugs and alcoholism |

Separately, play runs along a continuum from **paidia** (free improvisation) to **ludus** (rule-bound games).

*For Open Legend:* The corruption column is a ready-made **risk register**.
- A world of LLM residents is heavy on mimicry, whose corruption is *alienation*: losing oneself in a role.
- Loot and gacha systems are alea, whose corruption is superstition and compulsion.

Build explicit guards for each form. Keep the world paidia-rich (open invention), with ludus pockets such as the fair contests.

**B3. Bernard Suits, *The Grasshopper: Games, Life and Utopia* (1978)** [V2, Wikipedia, The Marginalian, Christian Scholar's Review]
- Short definition: playing a game is "the voluntary attempt to overcome unnecessary obstacles."
- Formal definition: "To play a game is to attempt to achieve a specific state of affairs [prelusory goal], using only means permitted by rules [lusory means], where the rules prohibit use of more efficient in favour of less efficient means [constitutive rules], and where the rules are accepted just because they make possible such activity [lusory attitude]."
- The Grasshopper's dream: "Everyone alive is in fact engaged in playing elaborate games, while at the same time believing themselves to be going about their ordinary affairs."
- The Grasshopper holds that "work is not self-justifying."
- Games are "activity in which what is instrumental is inseparably combined with what is intrinsically valuable" (p. 172).
- In Utopia, game-playing is what remains worth doing.

*For Open Legend:* Suits explains why survival play feels meaningful: the obstacles are freely chosen. His Utopia is also the project's central temptation, a world where the game *becomes* life. The counter-move is to help players take the lusory attitude *into* their real lives: a craft, a long walk, a handwritten letter, each taken up as an obstacle they chose.

**B4. Friedrich Schiller, *On the Aesthetic Education of Man*, Letter XV (1795)** [V, monadnock.net]
"Reason also utters the decision that man shall only play with beauty, and he shall play only with beauty." Then: "For, to speak out once for all, man only plays when in the full meaning of the word he is a man, and he is only completely a man when he plays."

*For Open Legend:* For Schiller, play reconciles sense and reason, necessity and freedom. It is the fullest form of the person, not a flight from the person. Quote both halves of the line.

**B5. D.W. Winnicott, *Playing and Reality* (1971)** [V2, Resurgence and UNCW]
- "It is in playing and only in playing that the individual child or adult is able to be creative and to use the whole personality, and it is only in being creative that the individual discovers the self."
- "It is creative apperception more than anything else that makes the individual feel that life is worth living." In contrast: "Compliance carries with it a sense of futility for the individual and is associated with the idea that nothing matters and that life is not worth living."
- The intermediate area of experience "is retained in the intense experiencing that belongs to the arts and to religion and to imaginative living, and to creative scientific work."
- "If play is neither inside nor outside, where is it?"
- The transitional object is eventually outgrown, "not forgotten and... not mourned" [U].

*For Open Legend:* Winnicott's "potential space," the zone between inner and outer life, is the best psychological model for what Open Legend could be. His axis gives a hard test:
- Checklists, dailies and quest logs breed **compliance**, and with it futility.
- Open invention breeds **creative apperception**, the sense that life is worth living.

A good transitional space is one a person can eventually *outgrow without mourning*.

**B6. Brian Sutton-Smith, *The Ambiguity of Play* (1997)** [V2, Psychology Today and Wikipedia]
The original sentence: "The opposite of play, in these terms, is not a present reality or work, it is vacillation, or worse, it is depression." Popular versions compress it to "The opposite of play is not work. It is depression." Sutton-Smith identifies seven "rhetorics" of play: progress, fate, power, (community) identity, the imaginary, the self, and frivolity. And his joke: "We study play because life is crap."

*For Open Legend:* Aim for **vitality and exultant commitment**, not productivity.

**B7. Stuart Brown, *Play* (2009) and the National Institute for Play** [P]
Brown is a psychiatrist who collected "play histories," including from violent offenders, and found play deprivation in them. He popularized Sutton-Smith's line and argues that adults need play for resilience and connection.

*For Open Legend:* A "play history" conversation makes a natural onboarding ritual (see Part 4).

**B8. Lev Vygotsky, "Play and its role in the mental development of the child"** (in *Mind in Society*, 1978, pp. 102–103) [V2, JoanWink]
- "play creates a zone of proximal development of the child. In play a child always behaves beyond his average age, above his daily behavior; in play it is as though he were a head taller than himself."
- "play contains all developmental tendencies in a condensed form and is itself a major source of development."
- "It is incorrect to conceive of play as activity without purpose… creating an imaginary situation can be regarded as a means of developing abstract thought."

*For Open Legend:* NPC mentors can serve as scaffolding. Put players "a head taller": braver in conversation, more generous, more patient. Aim for capacities that are *just* within reach off-screen.

**B9. Plato**
- *Republic* VII (Jowett translation) [V, MIT Classics]
  - The freed prisoner who goes back down "would... have his eyes full of darkness" and is ridiculed. "If any one tried to loose another and lead him up to the light, let them only catch the offender, and they would put him to death."
  - The duty to return (520c): "Wherefore each of you, when his turn comes, must go down to the general underground abode, and get the habit of seeing in the dark... you will see ten thousand times better than the inhabitants of the den."
  - Education is not putting sight into blind eyes: "the power and capacity of learning exists in the soul already." The soul must be *turned*.
- *Laws* (Jowett) [V, MIT Classics]
  - Book I: "he who is to be a good builder, should play at building children's houses; he who is to be a good husbandman, at tilling the ground." And: "The soul of the child in his play should be guided to the love of that sort of excellence in which when he grows up to manhood he will have to be perfected."
  - Book VII: "man... is made to be the plaything of God, and this, truly considered, is the best of him." Therefore "every man and woman should walk seriously, and pass life in the noblest of pastimes." And "the plays of childhood have a great deal to do with the permanence or want of permanence in legislation."

*For Open Legend:* The cave is the oldest form of the creator's worry. Plato's answer is not "never leave the cave." It is "go up, then go *down*, each in his turn." Note the warning: returners are clumsy in the dark and resented. Don't design the return as the player preaching to others. The player should "get the habit of seeing in the dark" again, gently.

**B10. Aristotle, *Nicomachean Ethics* (W.D. Ross translation)** [V, MIT Classics]
- **Eudaimonia (I.7):** "activity of soul in accordance with virtue, and if there are more than one virtue, in accordance with the best and most complete." It must be "in a complete life," because "one swallow does not make a summer, nor does one day; and so too one day, or a short time, does not make a man blessed and happy."
- **Habituation (II.1):** "men become builders by building and lyreplayers by playing the lyre; so too we become just by doing just acts, temperate by doing temperate acts, brave by doing brave acts." And: "It makes no small difference, then, whether we form habits of one kind or of another from our very youth; it makes a very great difference, or rather all the difference."
- **Friendship (VIII):**
  - "without friends no one would choose to live, though he had all other goods."
  - There are three kinds: for utility, for pleasure, and "Perfect friendship is the friendship of men who are good, and alike in virtue."
  - Friends cannot know each other "till they have 'eaten salt together.'"
  - "a wish for friendship may arise quickly, but friendship does not."
- **Amusement versus leisure (X.6–7):**
  - "to exert oneself and work for the sake of amusement seems silly and utterly childish. But to amuse oneself in order that one may exert oneself, as Anacharsis puts it, seems right; for amusement is a sort of relaxation."
  - "happiness does not lie in amusement."
  - "happiness is thought to depend on leisure; for we are busy that we may have leisure, and make war that we may live in peace."
- *Politics* VIII on leisure as "the first principle of all action" is [U]; the page could not be fetched.

*For Open Legend:*
- Aristotle separates **amusement**, which is rest for the sake of work, from **leisure** (*scholē*), free activity that is its own end. Open Legend can be either one; it should know which it is offering.
- Virtues form by *doing*. The acts a player practices in the world should be the same kinds of acts as outside it.
- Friendship takes time and shared salt. Make relationships that cannot be rushed or bought.

**B11. Clifford Geertz, "Deep Play: Notes on the Balinese Cockfight"** (*Daedalus* 1972; reprinted in *The Interpretation of Cultures*, 1973)
Geertz takes "deep play" from Bentham: "a game with stakes so high that no rational person would engage in it" [V2, Wikipedia]. The cockfight is "a Balinese reading of Balinese experience, a story they tell themselves about themselves" [U; standard citation p. 448].

*For Open Legend:* A world's games are its commentary on itself. The harvest-fair contests should dramatize the village's real values and tensions.

**B12. Diane Ackerman, *Deep Play* (1999)** [P]
Ackerman describes "deep play" as the rapt, ecstatic form of play: in art, sport, nature, risk and prayer, self-consciousness drops away.

*For Open Legend:* Nudge players toward deep play that is *not* on screens, such as climbing, music or the garden.

**B13. James P. Carse, *Finite and Infinite Games* (1986)**
- [V2, Wikipedia] "A finite game is played for the purpose of winning, an infinite game for the purpose of continuing the play." (The book opens: "There are at least two kinds of games. One could be called finite, the other infinite." [U])
- [U, consistent across many reproductions] "Finite players play within boundaries; infinite players play with boundaries." / "Whoever must play, cannot play." / "To be prepared against surprise is to be trained. To be prepared for surprise is to be educated." / "There is but one infinite game."

*For Open Legend:* The world is the infinite game; contests and quests are finite games nested inside it. "Whoever must play, cannot play" implies that **any mechanic that makes players feel they *must* log in is anti-play by definition.**

**B14. C. Thi Nguyen**
Works: *Games: Agency as Art* (2020), "The Seductions of Clarity" (2021), "Value Capture" (*Journal of Ethics and Social Philosophy*, 2024), and his book *The Score*.
- [V, OUP] "Games are a unique art form. Games work in the medium of agency." Games form a "library of agency." In *striving play* we pursue goals for the sake of the struggle. The book also examines "gamification and value capture."
- [V2, PhilPapers] "If an epistemic manipulator can imbue a belief system with an exaggerated sense of clarity, then they can induce us to terminate our inquiries too early."
- [V2, Wikipedia's gloss] Value capture happens "when you get your values from some external source and let them rule you without adapting them." It is "one consequence of rankings, score keeping, and gamification."

*For Open Legend:* Nguyen is the key contemporary philosopher for this project. Games are good *because* they let us adopt clear, simple values for a while. That is exactly why importing game-clarity into life, as streaks, step-counts or wellbeing scores, captures values. **Keep things clear inside the circle and deliberately unscored outside it.**

**B15. Ian Bogost** [V, bogost.com, 2011]
"Gamification is bullshit." Bogost proposes the name **"exploitationware"** and describes gamification as taking "games—a mysterious, magical, powerful medium" and reducing them to points and levels for business use. In *Play Anything* (2016) [P], play means working within a situation's limits with care and attention, so that even mowing a lawn can become a playground.

*For Open Legend:* Don't *gamify* life. *Playify* it: teach players to treat their own streets and chores as material for play.

**B16. Edward Castronova** [V2, Wikipedia "Magic circle (virtual worlds)"]
- *Synthetic Worlds* (2005): the membrane "can be considered a shield of sorts, protecting the fantasy world from the outside world" (p. 147), but it "cannot be sealed completely; people are crossing it all the time in both directions, carrying their behavioral assumptions and attitudes with them" (p. 147). Also: "the membrane between synthetic worlds and daily life is definitely there but also definitely porous, and this is by choice of the users" (pp. 159–160).
- *Exodus to the Virtual World: How Online Fun Is Changing Reality* (2007) [P]: foresees a mass migration of time and attention into virtual worlds, and argues that "fun" deserves standing as a policy goal.

*For Open Legend:* Castronova is the prophet of the creator's fear, and his "porous by choice" line gives the designer agency. **Porosity is a design decision.** Make the outward flows rich (skills, relationships, stories). Resist inward flows that turn the world into a second job or a market.

**B17. Jane McGonigal** [V2, Wikipedia; P]
- *Reality Is Broken* (2011) argues that "games contribute to human happiness and motivation, a sense of meaning, and community development." The *New York Times Book Review* faulted "the lack of evidence demonstrating that in-game behavior and values could translate into solutions to real-world problems."
- *SuperBetter* (2015) grew out of "Jane the Concussion-Slayer," a game she made to recover from a severe concussion in 2009.
- *Imaginable* (2022) teaches futures thinking.
- "Reality, compared to games, is broken" [U].

*For Open Legend:* Borrow the positive programme, such as SuperBetter's allies, power-ups and "bad guys" applied to real life. Take the critique seriously too: **transfer must be designed and measured, not assumed.**

**B18. Raph Koster, *A Theory of Fun for Game Design* (2004)**
"Fun is just another word for learning" [U]. Games are exercises in learning patterns, and boredom arrives once the patterns are mastered.

*For Open Legend:* When a player has mastered a world, the Narrator can help them *graduate*, either to another world or to the real-world version of the skill.

**B19. Jesper Juul, *The Art of Failure* (2013)** [P]
The paradox of failure: we avoid failure in life, yet seek it out in games, because games promise a fair chance to redeem ourselves.

*For Open Legend:* Make failure survivable and dignified. The Narrator can model self-compassion.

**B20. Miguel Sicart, *Play Matters* (2014)** [P]
Play is not confined to games. It is a way of being in the world and of appropriating contexts. "Playfulness" is an attitude that can be carried into non-play activities.

*For Open Legend:* Teach playfulness as a *portable* disposition.

### C. Technology, attention and the good life

**C1. Robert Nozick's Experience Machine** (*Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, 1974, pp. 42–45) [U for Nozick's wording; V2 for De Brigard's data]
The thought experiment: "Suppose there were an experience machine that would give you any experience you desired... Should you plug into this machine for life, preprogramming your life's experiences?" Nozick gives three reasons not to:
- "we want to do certain things, and not just have the experience of doing them";
- "we want to be a certain way, to be a certain sort of person";
- "plugging into an experience machine limits us to a man-made reality, to a world no deeper or more important than that which people can construct."

**The empirical rejoinder.** Felipe De Brigard, "If you like it, does it matter if it's real?" (*Philosophical Psychology*, 2010), asked 72 undergraduates whether they would *disconnect*, assuming they were already inside a machine:

| What participants were told about their "real" life | Share wishing to disconnect |
|---|---|
| nothing | 54% |
| they are prisoners | 13% |
| they are multimillionaire artists in Monaco | about half |

De Brigard attributes the results to **status-quo bias**.

*For Open Legend:* This finding is the most important one for the creator's worry. Once people are "in," status-quo bias keeps them there, *especially people whose real lives are hard* (the "prisoners"). The game cannot rely on players' own preference to leave; it must build in exits and protect real doing. Use Nozick's three reasons as an **audit**:
- **Doing:** is the player doing real things?
- **Being:** is the player becoming someone?
- **Deeper reality:** is the player in contact with something deeper than what we built?

**C2. Albert Borgmann, *Technology and the Character of Contemporary Life* (1984)** [V2, Wikipedia "Device paradigm"; U for the hearth quotation]
- A device is "the compound of commodity and machinery."
- "A focal thing is something of ultimate concern and significance, which may be masked by the device paradigm, and must be preserved by its intimate connection with practice."
- Central heating makes warmth available on demand, so family members can retreat to separate rooms instead of gathering to tend the fire.
- [U] The stove "was a focus, a hearth, a place that gathered the work and leisure of a family and gave the house a center."
- Other focal practices he discusses include the culture of the table, running and music [P].

*For Open Legend:* An LLM world is the ultimate device: it can deliver companionship, meaning and achievement as frictionless commodities. Design **focal** textures instead: hearths that must be tended together, meals that must be cooked, crafts that demand patience. Then point past the screen to the real table and the real run.

**C3. Ivan Illich, *Tools for Conviviality* (1973)** [V2, Wikipedia; U for the tool definitions]
- Conviviality is "individual freedom realized in personal interdependence" and "autonomous and creative intercourse among persons, and the intercourse of persons with their environment."
- A **"radical monopoly"** is a technology so dominant that people who lack it are excluded, as cars shaped cities.
- **Two watersheds:** a tool first brings real benefits, then passes a threshold where it brings professional control and harm.
- [U] "Tools foster conviviality to the extent to which they can be easily used, by anybody, as often or as seldom as desired, for the accomplishment of a purpose chosen by the user."
- [U] "Convivial tools are those which give each person who uses them the greatest opportunity to enrich the environment with the fruits of his or her vision."

*For Open Legend:* Test both the game and its invention system against Illich:
- Can it be used "as often or as seldom as desired"?
- Does it foster interdependence between people, or dependence on the platform?
- Is it approaching the second watershed, where it becomes a radical monopoly on a player's social life?

**C4. Matthew B. Crawford, *Shop Class as Soulcraft* (2009) and *The World Beyond Your Head* (2015)** [P]
Manual competence gives real agency because the world pushes back. Skilled practices shape attention through their "jigs." Public attention is a commons (the "attentional commons") being enclosed by engineered distraction.

*For Open Legend:* Treat player attention as a **commons you steward**, not a resource you extract.

**C5. Simone Weil on attention**
- "Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity." [V2 for the wording.] The source is muddled online: The Marginalian cites *First and Last Notebooks*, and Wikipedia ties it to the school-studies essay. The standard scholarly citation is her letter to Joë Bousquet of 13 April 1942, published in their *Correspondance* (1982) [U; verify before print].
- "Attention, taken to its highest degree, is the same thing as prayer." (*Gravity and Grace*) [V2]
- "We have to try to cure our faults by attention and not by will." [V2]
- [U] "The love of our neighbor in all its fullness simply means being able to say to him: 'What are you going through?'" ("Reflections on the Right Use of School Studies with a View to the Love of God," in *Waiting for God*.)

*For Open Legend:* The highest thing an NPC can model is attention, and "What are you going through?" is a line NPCs can teach players to ask each other.

**C6. Iris Murdoch, *The Sovereignty of Good* (1970)**
- [U] "I am looking out of my window in an anxious and resentful state of mind, oblivious of my surroundings, brooding perhaps on some damage done to my prestige. Then suddenly I observe a hovering kestrel. In a moment everything is altered. The brooding self with its hurt vanity has disappeared. There is nothing now but kestrel. And when I return to thinking of the other matter it seems less important."
- She attributes an "unselfing" power to beauty in nature and art [V2].
- [V2] "Love is the extremely difficult realisation that something other than oneself is real." ("The Sublime and the Good," *Chicago Review*, 1959, p. 51)

*For Open Legend:* Build **kestrel moments**: beauty that carries no reward (see Part 4).

**C7. Martin Buber, *I and Thou* (1923)** [V2, Wikiquote]
"All real living is meeting" (Smith translation); "All actual life is encounter" (Kaufmann). "The basic word I-Thou can be spoken only with one's whole being." "Through the Thou a person becomes I."

*For Open Legend:* This is an NPC ethic. **No purely instrumental NPC relationships** (I–It relations used only for loot). NPCs should also direct players toward human Thous.

**C8. Hannah Arendt, *The Human Condition* (1958)** [V2, SEP]
- Plurality: "Plurality is the condition of human action because we are all the same, that is, human, in such a way that nobody is ever the same as anyone else who ever lived, lives, or will live" (7–8).
- Natality: "It is in the nature of beginning that something new is started which cannot be expected from whatever may have happened before... The fact that man is capable of action means that the unexpected can be expected from him" (177–8).
- The space of appearance is "where I appear to others as others appear to me, where men exist not merely like other living or inanimate things, but to make their appearance explicitly" (198–9).

*For Open Legend:*
- Player inventions are natality.
- Town squares and the fair are spaces of appearance.
- Arendt's plurality is among *humans*. AI residents can fill the stage, but the audience that makes deeds real must include people.

**C9. Josef Pieper, *Leisure: The Basis of Culture*** (*Muße und Kult*, 1948; English translation by Alexander Dru, 1952, introduction by T.S. Eliot; bibliographic details [V2])
- [U] Leisure "is a mental and spiritual attitude—it is not simply the result of external factors, it is not the inevitable result of spare time, a holiday, a weekend or a vacation."
- [U] "Leisure is a form of silence, of that silence which is the prerequisite of the apprehension of reality."
- The soul of leisure is festivity, rooted in worship (hence *Kult*) [P].

*For Open Legend:* Cultivate the receptive, celebratory *attitude* of leisure. Don't merely fill spare time.

**C10. Abraham Joshua Heschel, *The Sabbath* (1951)** [V2, Wikiquote]
- "The Sabbaths are our great cathedrals; and our Holy of Holies is a shrine that neither the Romans nor the Germans were able to burn" (p. 8).
- "Six days a week we live under the tyranny of things of space; on the Sabbath we try to become attuned to holiness in time" (p. 10).
- "The meaning of the Sabbath is to celebrate time rather than space" (p. 10).
- "A Palace in Time" is the title of the book's first chapter [U].

*For Open Legend:* Heschel lets the world-pause become sacred rest (see Part 4, idea 8).

**C11. Byung-Chul Han**
- *The Burnout Society* (English 2015) [V2, Wikipedia]: "Today, everyone is an auto-exploiting labourer in his or her own enterprise. People are now master and slave in one."
- *The Disappearance of Rituals* (English 2020) [U]: "Rituals are symbolic techniques of making oneself at home in the world... They are to time what a home is to space: they render time habitable."

*For Open Legend:* Self-optimization loops are burnout machines. Make repetition **ritual** (a way of being at home), not **grind** (a way of achieving).

### D. Ancient schools and spiritual exercises

**D1. Epicurus** [V2, Wikipedia "Epicureanism"; V, Wikisource]
- Principal Doctrine 27: "Of all things that wisdom provides for living one's entire life in happiness, the greatest by far is the possession of friendship."
- Principal Doctrine 28: "...in the midst of life's limited evils, nothing enhances our security so much as friendship."
- Seneca, *Letter* 19.10, quoting Epicurus (Gummere translation): "You must reflect carefully beforehand with whom you are to eat and drink, rather than what you are to eat and drink. For a dinner of meats without the company of a friend is like the life of a lion or a wolf."
- The Garden community held monthly social gatherings called **Eikas** ("the Twentieth").

*For Open Legend:* Friendship is the central good, and the shared table is where it is practised.

**D2. The Stoics and Pierre Hadot** [V2]
- Hadot defines spiritual exercises as "practices... intended to effect a modification and a transformation in the subjects who practice them." Ancient philosophy was "a series of spiritual exercises intended to transform the perception, and therefore the being, of those who practice it."
- Marcus Aurelius, 4.17 (Casaubon translation): "Death hangs over thee: whilst yet thou livest, whilst thou mayest, be good."
- Marcus, 10.16 (Long translation): "Waste no more time arguing what a good man should be. Be one."
- On obstacles: "That which impeded action thus becomes profitable to action, and that which blocked the road allows me to advance along the road."
- The "view from above" (Marcus 7.48 and 9.30; Hadot's essay of that name) [P]: an imaginative ascent to see human affairs whole and brief.

*For Open Legend:* Spiritual exercises can take fictional form: an evening review, a mountaintop view over a village of brief lives, and rehearsing the hardship ahead before winter.

**D3. Benjamin Franklin's thirteen virtues** (*Autobiography*, Part Two) [V2, Wikipedia; U for the quotations]
The virtues: Temperance, Silence, Order, Resolution, Frugality, Industry, Sincerity, Justice, Moderation, Cleanliness, Tranquility, Chastity, Humility.

His method was a small book with a column for each day, "marking his offenses against each virtue with black spots." He gave one week's focus to each virtue, completing a thirteen-week cycle. He found Order the hardest. He concluded that perfection was unattainable but that the attempt made him better and happier. [U] "I was surpris'd to find myself so much fuller of faults than I had imagined; but I had the satisfaction of seeing them diminish."

*For Open Legend:* This is the ancestral gamification of character. It worked for Franklin because it was **private, self-authored and cyclical**. Versions imposed from outside become value capture.

### E. Myth, ritual and religion

**E1. Joseph Campbell, *The Hero with a Thousand Faces* (1949)** [V2]
- The monomyth: "A hero ventures forth from the world of common day into a region of supernatural wonder: fabulous forces are there encountered and a decisive victory is won: the hero comes back from this mysterious adventure with the power to bestow boons on his fellow man."
- The return: "The full round, the norm of the monomyth, requires that the hero shall now begin the labor of bringing the runes of wisdom, the Golden Fleece, or his sleeping princess, back into the kingdom of humanity, where the boon may redound to the renewing of the community, the nation, the planet, or the ten thousand worlds."
- "The returning hero, to complete his adventure, must survive the impact of the world."
- *Master of the Two Worlds*: "Freedom to pass back and forth across the world division, from the perspective of the apparitions of time to that of the causal deep and back—not contaminating the principles of the one with those of the other, yet permitting the mind to know the one by virtue of the other—is the talent of the master." (p. 196 in the edition cited by the source)
- The stages of return: Refusal of the Return, The Magic Flight, Rescue from Without, The Crossing of the Return Threshold, Master of the Two Worlds, Freedom to Live.

*For Open Legend:* Campbell names the exact stages Open Legend must support:
- **Refusal of the Return**: some players won't want to leave, which is why the game needs **Rescue from Without** (friends, the Narrator).
- **The Crossing of the Return Threshold**: the ritual that ends a session.
- **Master of the Two Worlds**: the goal. Not abolishing the game, but moving freely between worlds without letting either contaminate the other.

**E2. Arnold van Gennep (*Les rites de passage*, 1909) and Victor Turner** [V2, Wikipedia]
- Van Gennep's three phases:
  - **preliminal** (separation, "a metaphorical 'death'");
  - **liminal** (transition, in "a strictly prescribed sequence");
  - **postliminal** (incorporation: the initiate is "re-incorporated into society with a new identity, as a 'new' being").
- Turner on communitas: "an unstructured state in which all members of a community are equal allowing them to share a common experience." And: "Spontaneous communitas has something magical about it. Subjectively there is in it the feeling of endless power."
- Turner's **"liminoid"** (1974): "the liminal is part of society... while the liminoid is a break from society, part of 'play' or 'playing.'"
- [U] "Liminal entities are neither here nor there; they are betwixt and between the positions assigned and arrayed by law, custom, convention, and ceremonial." (*The Ritual Process*, 1969)

*For Open Legend:* A game is liminoid. What most games lack is the **third phase, incorporation**, so ritualize re-entry into ordinary life. The harvest fair is a natural site for communitas.

**E3. The Ten Ox-Herding Pictures** (Kuòān Shīyuǎn, 12th c.) [V, terebess.hu]

**D.T. Suzuki**, *Manual of Zen Buddhism* (1934/35), picture X, "Entering the City with Bliss-bestowing Hands." From the commentary: "His thatched cottage gate is closed, and even the wisest know him not... Carrying a gourd he goes out into the market, leaning against a staff he comes home. He is found in company with wine-bibbers and butchers, he and they are all converted into Buddhas." The verse:

> Bare-chested and bare-footed, he comes out into the market-place;
> Daubed with mud and ashes, how broadly he smiles!
> There is no need for the miraculous power of the gods,
> For he touches, and lo! the dead trees are in full bloom.

**Reps and Senzaki**, *Zen Flesh, Zen Bones* (1957), "10. In the World": "Inside my gate, a thousand sages do not know me. The beauty of my garden is invisible. Why should one search for the footprints of the patriarchs? I go to the market place with my wine bottle and return home with my staff. I visit the wineshop and the market, and everyone I look upon becomes enlightened." The verse:

> Barefooted and naked of breast, I mingle with the people of the world.
> My clothes are ragged and dust-laden, and I am ever blissful.
> I use no magic to extend my life;
> Now, before me, the dead trees become alive.

In the sequence, the stages just before are the empty circle, in which ox and self are both forgotten, and the return to the source. The *final* stage is a return to ordinary life, not a higher transcendence [P].

*For Open Legend:* This is **the single most apt image for the project.** The awakened one goes back to the market, laughing among butchers and drinkers, and the dead trees bloom. "There is no need for the miraculous power of the gods."

**E4. The Bodhisattva ideal** [V2, Wikipedia; U for Shantideva]
The bodhisattva vows to attain buddhahood "for the sake of all sentient beings." The first of the East Asian Four Great Vows reads, literally, "Masses [of] creatures, without-bounds, [I/we] vow to save [them all]." Shantideva, *Bodhicaryāvatāra* 10.55 [U]: "For as long as space endures and for as long as living beings remain, until then may I too abide to dispel the misery of the world."

*For Open Legend:* The greatest hero is the one who **comes back for others**, so a player's legend should grow by what they bring back.

**E5. Līlā: the world as divine play (Hindu thought)** [V2, Wikipedia]
Brahma Sutra 2.1.33: *lokavat tu līlākaivalyam*, "However, [it is] but līlā, as [occurs] in daily experience." On this view, creation "instead of being an objective for achieving any purpose, is rather an outcome of the playful nature of the divine." Shankara compared Brahman to "a king whose needs have been fulfilled, but engages in recreational activity."

*For Open Legend:* Līlā treats play as the nature of reality itself, and it locates that play in *daily experience*. **The deepest play is not elsewhere.**

**E6. Zhuangzi**
- The butterfly dream (Burton Watson translation) [V2]: "Once, Zhuang Zhou dreamed he was a butterfly, a butterfly flitting and fluttering about, happy with himself and doing as he pleased." On waking he cannot tell whether he is Zhou who dreamed the butterfly or the butterfly dreaming Zhou; the text calls this the Transformation of Things.
- Cook Ding (chapter 3, "Nourishing the Lord of Life") [P]: the cook carves an ox with dance-like rhythm by following its natural joints. His blade stays sharp for nineteen years. The lord says the cook has taught him how to nourish life.

*For Open Legend:* Cook Ding is the patron of **craft as flow**. The butterfly invites playful perspective-taking, but use it cautiously with players who may be prone to dissociation.

**E7. The Tao Te Ching (Legge translation, 1891)** [V, Gutenberg]
- Chapter 11: "The thirty spokes unite in the one nave; but it is on the empty space (for the axle), that the use of the wheel depends... Therefore, what has a (positive) existence serves for profitable adaptation, and what has not that for (actual) usefulness."
- Chapter 64: "The journey of a thousand li commenced with a single step."
- Chapter 80, on the small contented state: "They should think their (coarse) food sweet; their (plain) clothes beautiful; their (poor) dwellings places of rest; and their common (simple) ways sources of enjoyment."

*For Open Legend:*
- Chapter 11 is a design principle. **The empty space is what makes the thing useful**: pauses, unscripted time, land that is not monetized.
- Chapter 80 is the ethos of the medieval borderland world.

**E8. Confucian *li* (ritual propriety)** [V2, Wikipedia]
*Li* refers to "rites through which human agency participates in the larger order of the universe," and "the conscientious practice of li—ritual propriety—'humanizes' or 'civilizes' individuals." Xunzi counted "songs and laughter, weeping and lamentation... rice and millet, fish and meat... the wearing of ceremonial caps, embroidered robes" as the fabric of *li*.

*For Open Legend:* Greetings, precedence, feasting and mourning are **technology for self-cultivation**. NPCs should practise *li*, and players can learn it from them.

**E9. Japanese aesthetics and life-concepts** [V2, Wikipedia]
- **Ichi-go ichi-e** (一期一会, "one time, one meeting"): "any moment in life cannot be repeated; even when the same group of people get together in the same place again, a particular gathering will never be replicated." It traces to Sen no Rikyū and Yamanoue Sōji, and was formalized by Ii Naosuke in *Chanoyu Ichie Shū*: "Even though the host and guests may see each other often socially, one day's gathering can never be repeated exactly... The guests must understand that the gathering cannot occur again."
- **Mono no aware** (物の哀れ): "the pathos of things," "the aesthetic appreciation of impermanence." It carries "both a transient gentle sadness (or wistfulness) at their passing as well as a longer, deeper gentle sadness about this state being the reality of life." The concept comes from Motoori Norinaga's reading of *The Tale of Genji*; cherry blossoms are the classic example.
- **Wabi-sabi**: "acceptance of transience and imperfection." Richard Powell: "nothing lasts, nothing is finished, and nothing is perfect."
- **Kintsugi**: repairing pottery with lacquer mixed with powdered gold, silver or platinum. It "treats breakage and repair as part of the history of an object, rather than something to disguise." The story that it began with Ashikaga Yoshimasa is "one theory," not established fact.
- **Ikigai**: "an individual's definition of the meaning of their life." The popular four-circle Venn diagram "does not originate in Japan." Marc Winn adapted Andrés Zuzunaga's 2011 "purpose" diagram, "replacing 'purpose' with 'ikigai,'" and critics have called it "catchy but misleading." In Japanese usage ikigai is often small and everyday, such as a morning ritual or grandchildren, rather than a career sweet spot [P].

*For Open Legend:* See the mechanics in Part 4. Note that ikigai reframed as small, everyday reasons for living is far more useful than the career diagram.

**E10. Ubuntu** [V2, Wikipedia]
*Umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*: "A person is a person through people," sometimes glossed as "I am because we are." Desmond Tutu (1999): "A person with Ubuntu is open and available to others, affirming of others, does not feel threatened that others are able and good, based from a proper self-assurance that comes from knowing that he or she belongs in a greater whole..." And (2008): "You can't be human all by yourself."

*For Open Legend:* A player's legend is authored through others, so a player's standing should come from what they do for others.

**E11. Judaism**
- *Pirkei Avot* 2:16 (Rabbi Tarfon; Kulp translation) [V, Sefaria]: "It is not your duty to finish the work, but neither are you at liberty to neglect it."
- *Tikkun olam* [P]: in the Mishnah, a legal rationale "for the sake of repairing (ordering) the world"; later the cosmic repair of Lurianic Kabbalah; today an ethic of social repair.
- The Sabbath: see Heschel above.

*For Open Legend:* Tarfon's line is the ideal ethic for a persistent world where NPCs die and projects outlive players: do your part, and let others continue.

**E12. Christianity: St Benedict and Brother Lawrence**
- **The Rule of St Benedict, ch. 48** [V, OSB.org, Doyle translation 1948]: "Idleness is the enemy of the soul. Therefore the [brethren] should be occupied at certain times in manual labor, and again at fixed hours in sacred reading." The fetched copy is an edition for women's communities and reads "sisters." Manual labour was "never less than five hours a day," and the day is structured by "the eight canonical hours," from Matins to Compline [V2].
- **Hospitality (ch. 53)**: guests are received "with all care and hospitality, for it is in them that Christ is received" [V2, Wikipedia's rendering].
- **"Ora et labora" is "not stated explicitly in the rule"**; it is a later summary [V2].
- **Brother Lawrence**, *The Practice of the Presence of God* (1692) [U]: "The time of business does not with me differ from the time of prayer; and in the noise and clatter of my kitchen, while several persons are at the same time calling for different things, I possess God in as great tranquillity as if I were upon my knees at the blessed sacrament." And: "We ought not to be weary of doing little things for the love of God, who regards not the greatness of the work, but the love with which it is performed."

*For Open Legend:*
- The monastic horarium is an **architecture for a healthy day**: bells, and an alternation of work, reading, meals, prayer and sleep. It gives natural stopping points.
- Brother Lawrence makes the kitchen holy: chores done with love are a path in themselves.

**E13. Islam: small deeds as charity** [V, sunnah.com]
- Jami' at-Tirmidhi 1956 (graded *hasan*): "Your smiling in the face of your brother is charity, commanding good and forbidding evil is charity, your giving directions to a man lost in the land is charity for you. Your seeing for a man with bad sight is a charity for you, your removal of a rock, a thorn or a bone from the road is charity for you. Your pouring what remains from your bucket into the bucket of your brother is charity for you."
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6021 (narrated by Jabir): "Enjoining all that is good is a Sadaqa." It is often rendered "Every good deed is charity."

*For Open Legend:* The hadith is almost a **ready-made list of affordances**: greeting, giving directions, guiding someone who cannot see well, clearing the road, sharing water.

**E14. Indigenous thought: handle with care**
- **The Haudenosaunee "seventh generation"** [V2, Wikipedia]. The popular line "In every deliberation, we must consider the impact on the seventh generation" is *not* verbatim in the Great Law of Peace. The written Constitution (Law 28) says leaders should "Look and listen for the welfare of the whole people and have always in view not only the present but also the coming generations, even those whose faces are yet beneath the surface of the ground." Chief Oren Lyons (Onondaga): "We are looking ahead... to make every decision that we make relate to the welfare and well-being of the seventh generation to come."
- **Aboriginal songlines** [V2, Wikipedia]. A songline is "one of the paths across the land (or sometimes the sky)." A knowledgeable person can "navigate across the land by repeating the words of the song, which describe the location of landmarks, waterholes, and other natural phenomena." Songlines carry "knowledge that instruct on social behaviour, gender relations or where water or food can be sourced." Sung in the language of a particular region, a songline "shows respect to the people of that country." Bruce Chatwin's *The Songlines* (1987) is an outsider's literary account [P].

*For Open Legend:*
- The seventh-generation principle can shape world councils that deliberate for "faces yet beneath the surface of the ground."
- Songlines can inspire the idea of *memory carried in song* only at a structural level. Do not borrow sacred Indigenous forms without consultation and credit.

**E15. Modern formation traditions: Bildung, Grundtvig, Hahn, Baden-Powell** [V2, Wikipedia]
- **Grundtvig and the Danish folk high schools.** Grundtvig emphasized "the living word" and the "enlightenment of the people," with no exams. The first folk high school opened at Rødding in 1844. Christen Kold's schools taught "from November to March because students did farm work the rest of the year." *Bildung* [P] means the self-formation of the whole person through culture.
- **Kurt Hahn** co-founded Outward Bound with Lawrence Holt. His **Seven Laws of Salem** include:
  - "Give the children opportunities for self-discovery."
  - "Make the children meet with triumph and defeat."
  - "Give the children the opportunity of self-effacement in the common cause."
  - "Provide periods of silence."
  - "Train the imagination."
  - "Make games (i.e., competition) important but not predominant."

  He listed six "declines of modern youth," including the "Decline of Compassion due to the unseemly haste with which modern life is conducted," and championed "Samaritan service."
- **Baden-Powell's last message** to Scouts (written around 1929 and published after his death in 1941): "Try and leave this world a little better than you found it," and "the real way to get happiness is by giving out happiness to other people."

*For Open Legend:* Hahn's Seven Laws read almost like a ready-made design charter, and **"Games important but not predominant"** is the whole thesis in five words.

## PART 4: Design implications for Open Legend

### Principles (a charter the team can adopt)

1. **The Tolkien Test: Recovery before Escape.** Review every feature with one question: *does this make stone, wood, iron, bread, and the people at the player's real table look more wondrous, or more drab?* Treat Lewis's "undivinely discontented" as the red flag. Measure it with occasional opt-in reflection prompts, and keep the results as research data, never as a score.

2. **The Deserter Line.** Write down what counts as desertion: play that displaces sleep, meals with others, or obligations. The game never rewards it. That means:
   - no login streaks;
   - no decay while away;
   - no events that punish absence;
   - no notifications designed to summon.

   The grounding is Carse: "Whoever must play, cannot play."

3. **A Membrane Charter**, after Castronova's "porous by choice." Publish what crosses in each direction.
   - **Outward:** skills, stories, friendships, recipes, courage, kindness.
   - **Inward, opt-in:** the player's real rhythms, interests and season.
   - **Never:** money that buys status, social-comparison metrics, anything that makes the world a second job.

4. **No wellbeing score.** Drawing on Nguyen, Bogost and Franklin: nothing about a player's real-life flourishing is quantified, ranked, badged or streaked. Any self-tracking is **private, self-authored, cyclical and deletable**, like Franklin's little book.

5. **"Important but not predominant"** (Hahn) as the north-star phrase for the product. Pair it with Nozick's three-part audit, *doing, being, deeper reality*, in design reviews. Because De Brigard found status-quo bias keeps people "in," **exits are defaults, not options.**

### Rituals

6. **The Return Rite**, after van Gennep's incorporation, Campbell's Return Threshold and ox-herding picture X ("In the World"). Every session closes with a short ritual:
   - an in-world bell rings (see idea 9);
   - the Narrator closes the day's page;
   - the Narrator asks one skippable question: *"What will you carry back to the market?"*

   The answer, the boon, becomes the chapter's last line. Next session the Narrator may open lightly, without guilt: "Did anything come of what you carried?"

7. **The Threshold Rite (entry).** A brief separation ritual marks the circle: passing a gate, washing at a well, hanging up a coat. The message is that the space is hallowed and also *bounded*.

8. **"The village keeps Sabbath while you are away."** Frame the world-pause as sacred rest, not an engineering constraint. NPCs are never harmed by a player's absence. Add a weekly in-world **Palace in Time** day (after Heschel's first chapter): no trade, contest or crafting; only feast, song, stories and walks. That way the rhythm of rest is modelled inside the world too.

9. **The Hours**, from Benedict's horarium. The in-world day is marked by bells: dawn, labour, the midday meal, reading, vespers, and Compline. Compline is where the game suggests closing the session. Optionally, align the village's night with the player's local bedtime, so the village sleeps when the player should.

10. **Eikas and Ichi-go ichi-e feasts.** Each world holds a monthly feast named after Epicurus's twentieth-day gathering.
    - Every fair and feast gets a unique name and a keepsake page in the journal: "one time, one meeting."
    - The harvest fair is explicitly unrepeatable.
    - The game invites players to host a real meal that week, with a printable recipe from the in-world harvest. The grounding is Seneca: choose *with whom* you eat.

11. **Rites for the dying.** NPC aging and death get vigils, eulogies drawn from the NPC's durable memories of players, and memorial stones (memento mori; mono no aware).
    - The Narrator may offer one gentle, optional bridge: *"Is there someone you've been meaning to call?"*
    - There must be sensitivity settings for bereaved players.
    - The Tarfon line can be carved on memorials: *"not your duty to finish the work..."*

### Mechanics

12. **Kestrel Moments** (Murdoch). Beauty with no reward: a hovering kestrel, frost ferns on a window, a lark at dusk. No XP and no loot. The Narrator simply notices. This is training in unselfing.

13. **The Six Charities** (Tirmidhi 1956) as world affordances:
    - greeting with a smile;
    - giving directions;
    - guiding someone who cannot see well;
    - clearing stones and thorns from a path;
    - pouring from your bucket into another's;
    - encouraging the good.

    NPCs remember these acts and the Narrator writes them into the legend as prose, not points. Occasionally the Narrator suggests a real-world "road-clearing."

14. **Salt-and-bread friendships** (Aristotle). NPC and player friendships deepen only through time and shared meals or work, never through gifts bought with currency. Build in a minimum real-time cadence: "a wish for friendship may arise quickly, but friendship does not."

15. **Kintsugi repair.** Broken tools and pottery can be mended with visible gold seams, gain a line of history, and are valued *more* by NPCs. Extend the idea to relationships: rituals of apology and repair that leave a visible, honoured mark.

16. **A convivial invention system** (Illich).
    - Usable "by anybody, as often or as seldom as desired."
    - Inventions enter a world commons, consistent with the AGPL ethos of the engine.
    - Guard against radical monopoly: when the stove is invented, the **hearth must survive** (Borgmann).

17. **The Hearth at the center** (Borgmann). Every home has a hearth that must be tended, and important scenes gather there. The heart of a village is its hearth or long table, not its shop.

18. **Worlds as a library of agencies** (Nguyen). Each world is designed around one agency to practise and bring home:

    | World | Agency practised | Grounding |
    |---|---|---|
    | Wilderness | resourcefulness and calm when afraid | Chesterton's terrors with limits |
    | Borderland village | neighbourliness, craft, *li* | Confucian ritual propriety |
    | City | civic action and plurality | Arendt |
    | Living fantasy | wonder and courage | Tolkien's Recovery |
    | Sci-fi | imagining futures | Le Guin; McGonigal's *Imaginable* |

    At the end of an arc, the Narrator *names the agency* the player practised.

19. **"A head taller" mentoring** (Vygotsky). NPC mentors scaffold skills just beyond the player's current level: speaking at the fair, mediating a quarrel, teaching an NPC child. The Narrator then offers one small real-world analogue.

20. **The Play History** (Stuart Brown). Onboarding includes a conversation with an elder: *"What did you love to do as a child?"* The answer seeds in-world affordances, and later gentle real-world suggestions, such as noticing that a player who loved climbing trees might enjoy the local climbing wall.

21. **The View from Above and the evening review** (Hadot, Marcus, Seneca). A bell tower or ridge from which the player sees the whole valley and its brief lives. An optional Stoic review in the journal: *What went well? What would you do differently?*

22. **Seventh-generation councils.** When a world faces a lasting choice, such as felling the old wood, an elder speaks for "faces yet beneath the surface of the ground." Decisions persist across NPC generations and new players inherit them.

23. **The Treasure at Home questline** (ATU 1645). A long quest that crosses worlds and ends where the player began: the treasure is under the first hut. Its epilogue turns toward the player's real home.

### NPC archetypes

24. **The Market Sage** (ox-herding picture X). A ragged, laughing elder who lives among butchers and drinkers. The Sage cannot be recruited for loot quests. Every "quest" they give is to go back and help someone, and dead trees bloom where they pass.

25. **The Returned One** (Plato's cave). Someone who has seen more and come back. They are clumsy in the dark, not always believed, and model a humble return rather than a preachy one.

26. **The Knight of the Books** (Quixote). An NPC lost in romances: brave, kind and deluded. His arc ends when he recovers his true name, "the Good." He is a mirror for players, never a figure of mockery.

27. **The Grasshopper** (Suits). A joyful NPC who only plays, asks players provocative questions about work and play, and has a winter coming.

28. **Master Ding** (Zhuangzi). A cook or smith whose mastery looks effortless. Master Ding teaches through rhythm and is the source of the craft-flow design.

29. **The Narrator's ethic.** Following Bettelheim, the Narrator never explains the moral. Following Weil, it attends; its signature question is *"What are you going through?"* It writes the *legenda* and never pushes wish-fulfilment (Lewis).

30. **The Rescuer from Without** (Campbell). An NPC or system behaviour that notices signs of desertion, such as very long sessions or late nights. Speaking in the fiction, it says the lamps are going out, the village is resting, and "go home." It should also carry non-fiction links to support resources.

### Naming ideas

31. Possible names:

    | Name | For |
    |---|---|
    | **Legenda** | the journal ("(things) to be read") |
    | **The Key** | a map-legend screen showing how practices in the world map to practices in life |
    | **In the World** | the return rite |
    | **Bliss-bestowing Hands** | the final stage of any character arc |
    | **Clean Windows** | the Recovery features |
    | **Eikas** | the monthly feast |
    | **Compline** | the closing bell |
    | **Palace in Time** | the Sabbath day |
    | **The Market** | the hub |

## PART 6: Commonly misattributed or distorted quotations to avoid

1. **"Fairy tales are more than true: not because they tell us that dragons exist, but because they tell us that dragons can be beaten."** This is Neil Gaiman's paraphrase of Chesterton, and Gaiman says so himself: "the Chesterton quote was mine and not his." Use Chesterton's real line: "...a St. George to kill the dragon."

2. **"You can discover more about a person in an hour of play than in a year of conversation." (attributed to Plato)** Wikiquote lists it as misattributed. It was attributed to Plato in Alan Loy McGinnis, *Confidence* (1987), and is "probably a paraphrase" of a line in Richard Lingard's *Letter of Advice to a Young Gentleman Leaving the University...* Wikiquote spells the name "Lindgard" and cites a 1907 edition.

3. **"Fantasy is escapist, and that is its glory." (attributed to Tolkien)** It is flagged "Not a Tolkien quote" by thetolkienist.com (seen as a search-result title only). Tolkien's own image is the prisoner and the deserter.

4. **"Who are the people most opposed to escapism? Jailers!" (usually credited to Tolkien)** That wording is Arthur C. Clarke's (1999). The underlying "jailers" point appears in C.S. Lewis's "On Science Fiction," which reports a question from Tolkien.

5. **"The opposite of play is not work. It's depression."** This compresses Sutton-Smith's longer sentence ("...it is vacillation, or worse, it is depression"). Credit Sutton-Smith (1997), not Stuart Brown, who popularized it.

6. **"Ora et labora" as a quotation from the Rule of St Benedict.** It is "not stated explicitly in the rule"; it is a later motto.

7. **"In every deliberation, we must consider the impact on the seventh generation" as a quotation from the Great Law of Peace.** It is not verbatim. Quote Law 28 ("faces are yet beneath the surface of the ground") or Oren Lyons instead.

8. **The ikigai Venn diagram as Japanese wisdom.** It "does not originate in Japan": Marc Winn relabelled Andrés Zuzunaga's 2011 "purpose" diagram.

9. **"Attention is the rarest and purest form of generosity" attributed to *Gravity and Grace* or the school-studies essay.** The commonly given source is Weil's letter to Joë Bousquet (13 April 1942). Confirm before printing.

10. **"Life must be lived as play..." (Plato, *Laws* 803)** is a loose modern rendering. Jowett gives "pass life in the noblest of pastimes," and the passage is framed by "man... is made to be the plaything of God."

11. **"The journey of a thousand miles begins with a single step."** This is a popularized version. Legge has "The journey of a thousand li commenced with a single step" (ch. 64), and more literal renderings say it begins "beneath one's feet" [U].

12. **"I am because we are" as the literal meaning of *umuntu ngumuntu ngabantu*.** The literal sense is "a person is a person through people"; the other is a popular gloss.

13. **The magic circle as Huizinga's theory.** Huizinga used the phrase in a list of play-grounds. The *game-design concept* was coined by Zimmerman and Lantz (1999) and popularized by Salen and Zimmerman (2003).

14. **Kintsugi's Ashikaga Yoshimasa origin** is "one theory," not established fact.

15. **Bettelheim as a clinical authority.** His credentials were misrepresented and plagiarism was documented (Dundes, 1991). Cite the idea, not the authority.

16. **Coelho's "the universe conspires" presented as ancient wisdom.** It is Coelho's own 1988 line. The *ancient* part is the story structure, the Treasure at Home (ATU 1645; Rumi; the *1001 Nights*).
