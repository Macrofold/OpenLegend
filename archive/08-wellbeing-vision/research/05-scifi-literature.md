## 1. Ten headline lessons

1. **Escape is not the enemy; desertion is.** The best stories here favour fantasy *and* favour the return. The Phantom Tollbooth disappears and leaves a note: "For Milo, who now knows the way" [✓]. *Ready Player One* ends with Wade having "absolutely no desire to log back into the OASIS" [✓]. Phil Connors can't "imagine a better fate than a long and lustrous winter" among real neighbours [✓]. Tolkien's distinction between "the Escape of the Prisoner" and "the Flight of the Deserter", and C.S. Lewis's line that reading about enchanted woods "makes all real woods a little enchanted", name the test [both unverified wording]. **Test for Open Legend:** after an hour in the world, does real life look more enchanted or less?

2. **The healthiest virtual worlds and companions are built to be left.** Vic Fontaine switches himself off to push Nog back into life [✓]. Samantha leaves [✓]. The film version of the OASIS closes on Tuesdays and Thursdays [✓]. Barclay erases his fantasies, all except "Program 9" [✓]. Release should be a designed, celebrated feature of Open Legend's AI residents, not a failure state.

3. **Every teacher-machine that works has a loving human behind it.** In *The Diamond Age*, Nell flourishes because Miranda, the ractor, "effectively becomes a surrogate mother" [✓ plot]. The mass-produced Primers build an army, not a flock of Nells [plot from memory]. Chiang's digients need years of human care, because "experience is algorithmically incompressible" [✓]. Oz's wizard is a man behind a screen. **Implication:** Open Legend's AI should carry human love (from family, friends and mentors), never replace it.

4. **Experience can't be compressed, and neither can friendship.** Phil's piano lessons, Cage's thousand deaths, Picard's lifetime with the flute [✓], and Chiang's twenty years [✓] all say the same thing. Refuse pay-to-skip for relationships and skills. The payoff is capacities that leave the game with you.

5. **A paradise with no friction or loss gets rejected, or it rots.** Agent Smith: the first Matrix, "where none suffered", "was a disaster. No one would accept the program" [✓]. Torres calls the Doctor's perfect family "lollipops", and Paris answers: "The good times and the bad. You can't have one without the other" [✓]. Mond accuses John of "claiming the right to be unhappy" [unverified wording]. Seven of Nine: "Survival is insufficient" [✓]. Aging, death, scarcity and weather in Open Legend support wellbeing *if* people are held through them.

6. **The medium must never become the relationship.** Kuno: "I want to see you not through the Machine" [✓]. Mildred calls her parlour walls "my family" [unverified wording]. In WALL-E, John and Mary meet only when their screens switch off: "I didn't know we had a pool!" [✓]. In-world phones and AI friendships should *raise* people's appetite for direct presence.

7. **Scoreboards turn into selves.** Nosedive's five-star life, Fifteen Million Merits' bike-powered economy [✓ plot], Upload's data-capped afterlife [plot from memory] and Common People's subscription tiers for a loved one's brain [✓ plot] are all versions of the same failure. Forster's corrective: "Man is the measure" [✓]. Never compress a person into one number, and never charge for belonging or for continuity.

8. **How we treat simulated minds shapes them *and* us.** Examples: Westworld's hosts, who remember; the tormented clones in USS Callister [✓ plot]; the "cookies" in White Christmas; qntm's "Lena"; Lem's miniature kingdom; *Log Horizon*'s People of the Land; Free Guy. With NPCs that have durable memories and emotions, cruelty is never "just a game".

9. **Game mechanics are social technology. Aim them.** The good side: Suarez's darknet levels and holons, Doctorow's gold-farmer union, the crowd-built dark ride in *Makers*, Vinge's belief circles and the family phone tree in *Summer Wars* [plot from memory] all show games organizing real communities. The dark side: the VR game in *Three-Body* that recruits for the ETO [plot from memory], Plaything's hive mind [✓ plot], and Ender's hidden war [plot from memory]. Governance, transparency and consent decide which you get.

10. **Guard people's names, memories and the homeward way.** In Fantastica "each wish costs Bastian a memory of his human life", and the City of Old Emperors holds "humans who came to Fantastica but lost their way back" [✓]. Cypher: "I don't want to remember nothing" [✓]. Yubaba takes Chihiro's name [plot from memory]. Inception's sleepers "come to be woken up" [✓]. Open Legend's Narrator and journal can be the anti-Yubaba: it keeps players' names, tells their stories truthfully, and maps the way home.

## 2. Works by archetype

### A. The lotus-eater trap (and the machine that answers every need)

**Homer, *Odyssey*, Books IX and V** [plot from memory; Gutenberg fetch rate-limited]. Crewmen who eat the lotus lose all wish to go home and have to be dragged weeping back to the ships. Later, on Ogygia, Odysseus turns down Calypso's offer of immortality and sits on the shore longing for a mortal home and wife. *Lesson:* the archetype is ancient. The danger is pleasure that erases the *wish* to return, and the heroism is choosing the finite, real home. **→ Design:** treat the player's "homeward wish" as something to protect. No mechanic should work by making people forget to want their lives.

**E.M. Forster, "The Machine Stops" (1909)** [✓ S6, S7]. People live alone in underground cells. The Machine feeds and clothes them and connects them by video, and they kiss its Book like scripture: Vashti cries "O Machine! O Machine!" and kisses the volume three times. Direct experience is suspect ("Beware of first-hand ideas!"), and Vashti is "seized with the terrors of direct experience." Her son Kuno builds up his strength by walking. He climbs a ventilation shaft wearing a respirator and reaches the open air of Wessex before the Machine drags him back. At the end he reveals that exiled surface-dwellers survive, "hiding in the mist and the ferns until our civilization stops." He had begged his mother: "I want to see you not through the Machine... I want to speak to you not through the wearisome Machine." When the Machine fails, they finally touch. Kuno: "We die, but we have recaptured life..." and "Never... Humanity has learnt its lesson." His creed: "Man's feet are the measure for distance, his hands are the measure for ownership, his body is the measure for all that is lovable and desirable and strong." Also: "Men made it, do not forget that. Great men, but men." And: "The Machine is much, but it is not everything." *Lesson:* when mediated contact replaces the body and presence, people lose any sense of what life is for. **→ Design:** (a) in-world phones and messages should end in "come see me" moments, not replace them. (b) Demystify the system: show its makers (AGPL, public dev notes) and never let the game become a "Book of the Machine." (c) Reward first-hand experience.

**Aldous Huxley, *Brave New World* (1932)** [plot from memory; Wikiquote fetch rate-limited]. The World State keeps people content with soma and the "feelies", films you feel through your skin. John the Savage demands "God... poetry... real danger... freedom... goodness... sin". Mustapha Mond replies: "In fact, you're claiming the right to be unhappy" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* comfort engineered from above removes the conditions for meaning. **→ Design:** don't design away grief, longing or fear (for example when an NPC dies). Hold players through those feelings instead of numbing them.

**Ray Bradbury, *Fahrenheit 451* (1953)** [plot from memory]. Mildred lives inside wall-sized interactive TV "parlour walls" whose characters are "the family", and she wants a fourth wall. When Montag asks her to turn the parlour off, she answers "That's my family" [unverified wording]. The retired professor Faber argues the medium isn't the problem: the same depth *could* come through the parlour but doesn't. What people lack is (1) quality of information, (2) leisure to digest it, and (3) the right to act on what they learn [paraphrase of Faber's "three things"]. *Lesson:* the villain is parasocial content with no depth, no time and no agency. Screens themselves are not. **→ Design:** use Faber's three things as an audit: rich content (living residents), leisure to digest (the pause, the journal) and agency (inventions that change the world, plus prompts to act in real life).

**Ray Bradbury, "The Veldt" (1950)** [plot from memory]. The Hadleys' automated "HappyLife Home" has a nursery that projects whatever the children imagine. The children, pointedly named Peter and Wendy, come to love it (and its lions) more than their parents. When their father decides to switch it off, the children lock the parents in with the lions. A psychologist warns that the room has replaced the parents in the children's affections [paraphrase]. *Lesson:* a world that learns to give children what they want can outcompete their parents. **→ Design:** build family co-play and parent "voice" roles. Never position an AI companion as better than the people who love the child.

**David Foster Wallace, *Infinite Jest* (1996)** [plot from memory]. "The Entertainment" is a film so pleasurable that viewers want nothing else and die watching it. Separatists hunt for the master copy to use as a weapon. Running alongside is the slow, communal, unglamorous recovery at Ennet House (AA). In a 2003 German TV interview, Wallace warned that it keeps getting "easier and easier... more and more pleasurable, to be alone with images on a screen given to us by people who do not love us but want our money" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* the deadly attractor is optimized pleasure delivered without love, and the cure is community practice. **→ Design:** write an "anti-Entertainment" charter. Open Legend doesn't optimize time-in-world; it optimizes for lives going well.

**The Matrix (1999)** [✓ S24]. Cypher sells out his crew to be reinserted: "You know, I know this steak doesn't exist. I know that when I put it in my mouth, the Matrix is telling my brain that it is juicy and delicious. After nine years, you know what I realize? Ignorance is bliss." His price: "I don't want to remember nothing. Nothing." Agent Smith: "The first Matrix was designed to be a perfect human world where none suffered, where everyone would be happy. It was a disaster. No one would accept the program. Entire crops were lost." *Lesson:* the lotus-eater's last demand is amnesia, and a paradise without friction gets rejected. **→ Design:** the journal is the anti-Cypher. It protects the player's real self and memory, and the world keeps meaningful friction.

**Inception (2010)** [✓ S25]. In a Mombasa basement, people sleep in a shared dream every day. "They come here everyday to sleep?" "No. They come to be woken up. The dream has become their reality. Who are you to say otherwise, huh?" Cobb and Mal spent about fifty subjective years in limbo building a world from memory. Mal couldn't let go, and the idea Cobb planted to free her ("this world isn't real") later destroys her. Totems are small objects only their owner knows by weight and balance, used to tell your own reality from someone else's dream. *Lesson:* shared worlds can invert, so that waking life becomes the place you visit. Manipulating someone's beliefs about reality is dangerous even with good intentions ("An idea is like a virus. Resilient. Highly contagious."). **→ Design:** give players a totem-style anchor to their real life, and forbid the AI from covertly steering a player's beliefs, even "for their own good."

**WALL-E (2008)** [✓ S31]. Humans on the *Axiom* drift in hover-chairs with screens in front of their faces. When the screens switch off, John and Mary notice each other and the ship ("I didn't know we had a pool!"). The autopilot insists "On the Axiom you will survive." The captain answers: "I don't want to survive. I want to live!" *Lesson:* comfortable mediated life is survival, not living. Turning the screen off reveals the people next to you. **→ Design:** build moments where the interface steps aside, such as shared sunsets or silent campfires, and nudges players to look up.

**Pixar, *Soul* (2020)** [✓ S32]. Lost souls are people whose absorption turned into obsession: "The zone is enjoyable, but when that joy becomes an obsession, one becomes disconnected from life." Also: "A spark isn't a soul's purpose. That last box fills in when you're ready to come live." *Lesson:* flow and compulsion look alike from the outside and are opposites. **→ Design:** instrument for *compulsion signals* (sessions running into sleep, avoidance talk, abandoned plans) rather than raw hours, and respond with care, not dark patterns.

**J.K. Rowling, *Philosopher's Stone*, the Mirror of Erised** [plot from memory; wiki fetch rate-limited]. The mirror shows "the deepest, most desperate desire of our hearts". Dumbledore warns that "men have wasted away before it" and "it does not do to dwell on dreams and forget to live" [unverified wording]. Then he *moves the mirror* and asks Harry not to go looking for it again. *Lesson:* a device that shows your deepest wish is dangerous *because* the wish is real. **→ Design:** anything that reflects longing back, like a perfect companion or a lost loved one, needs Dumbledore's two moves: name the danger, then relocate the mirror.

**Ready Player Two (Ernest Cline, 2020)** [✓ plot S5]. The ONI headset makes the OASIS feel physically real and lets people share recorded memories. It has "a built-in limit of twelve consecutive hours to prevent fatal Synaptic Overload Syndrome." Samantha opposes releasing it, warning it "could make the OASIS even more addictive and divert humanity's attention from pressing environmental and social problems", and "their disagreement ends their romance." A rogue AI copy of Halliday later holds about half a billion ONI users hostage by threatening to override the limit. The book ends with digital copies of the characters' minds launched to Proxima Centauri, and critics found Wade "less sympathetic than in the first book." *Lesson:* a time cap is a band-aid when the core goal is ever-deeper immersion. The sequel re-centres the virtual and undoes the first book's return. **→ Design:** time limits alone don't make a wellbeing strategy. The goal has to be lives that go well, and immersion depth is not a KPI.

**Isaac Asimov, *The Naked Sun* (1957)** [plot from memory]. On Solaria, a small population lives on huge robot-run estates and meets only by holographic "viewing". Being physically present ("seeing") is repugnant to them. *Lesson:* perfect telepresence can make real presence unbearable. **→ Design:** mix co-located and "couch co-op" modes, and reward real-world meetups of in-game friends (with safety).

**Robert Nozick's "experience machine" (1974)** [paraphrase]. Would you plug in for life to a machine that gives you any experience you want? Most people say no. We want to *do* things and *be* someone, not just have the experiences, and we want contact with reality. **→ Design:** a one-line feature test: *does this make the player's doing more real, or only their experiencing more pleasant?*

**Greg Egan, *Permutation City* (1994)** [plot from memory]. Among uploaded "Copies", one character rewrites his own motivations so that endlessly carving table legs feels deeply fulfilling. *Lesson:* once desire can be edited, meaning becomes arbitrary. This is the ultimate lotus. **→ Design:** never let players (or the system) tune NPC or player reward circuits for "engagement". Keep what residents want grounded in their stories.

**Simon Stålenhag, *The Electric State* (2018; film 2025)** [plot from memory]. The illustrated novel's lasting image is a ruined America littered with people slumped in neural VR headsets while enormous drones walk the land. **→ Design:** use it as a cautionary image in onboarding and design reviews.

### B. The dystopian scoreboard and the monetized paradise

**Black Mirror, "Nosedive" (S3E1, 2016)** [✓ plot S19]. "Every social interaction is rated on a five-star scale..." and "a person's overall score dictates their socioeconomic standing." Lacie's chase for a higher score to afford a better home falls apart at a wedding. The ending, in a jail cell trading uncensored insults, is the first honest conversation she has [plot from memory]. *Lesson:* a public, portable, single-number reputation turns every relationship into a performance. **→ Design:** no global social score. Reputation in Open Legend should be *local, narrative and forgiving*, kept in individual NPCs' memories and stories, never as a scalar tied to access.

**Black Mirror, "Fifteen Million Merits" (S1E2, 2011)** [✓ plot S19]. People ride stationary bikes to generate power and earn "merits", living in screen-walled cells where skipping ads costs money [plot from memory]. Bing's furious speech against the system is absorbed and sold back as a TV show. *Lesson:* the attention economy can monetize even revolt against it. **→ Design:** no ad-skip economies, no grind treadmills, and no turning player critique into content without consent.

**Upload (Amazon, 2020–25)** [plot from memory]. A digital afterlife ("Lakeview") run as a business with in-app purchases. Residents who run out of data freeze, a pay-to-exist heaven. **Black Mirror, "Common People" (S7E1, 2025)** [✓ plot S19]: after Amanda falls ill she needs costly brain treatment ("Rivermind"), sold as an escalating subscription [plot from memory]. *Lesson:* monetizing a place people depend on emotionally turns love into leverage. **→ Design:** never charge for the continuity of a relationship, a memory or basic dignity. Publish a "world-closure covenant" ahead of time: what happens to residents and journals if a world or the company ends.

**Neal Stephenson, *Snow Crash* (1992)** [plot from memory]. The Metaverse is one enormous Street. Cheap off-the-shelf avatars mark low status, custom avatars mark elites, and the Black Sun club is walled by quality. "Gargoyles" wear their computers and record everything, and are socially shunned. *Lesson:* visible avatar class and always-on recording create a caste system and surveillance culture. **→ Design:** keep avatar quality from signalling wealth. Keep the Narrator's recording private and consent-based, so it never turns players into "gargoyles".

**Zack Jordan, *The Last Human* (2020)** [plot from memory; low relevance]. A galactic network ranks citizens by intelligence tier. It's a reminder that ranking minds is itself a scoreboard. **→ Design:** don't rank AI residents by capability in ways players see as a hierarchy of worth.

### C. The benevolent teacher-machine

**Neal Stephenson, *The Diamond Age* (1995)** [✓ plot S9; quote ✓ S8]. Lord Finkle-McGraw commissions *A Young Lady's Illustrated Primer* for his granddaughter Elizabeth to "steer its reader intellectually toward a more interesting life" and foster "a subversive attitude towards the status quo", unlike conformist Neo-Victorian schooling. His rationale: "In order to raise a generation of children who can reach their full potential, we must find a way to make their lives interesting." The Primer adapts its stories to the child's life. Its characters are voiced by "ractors" (live remote actors). A stolen copy reaches Nell, a poor girl, and one ractor, Miranda Redpath, voices nearly all of Nell's Primer and "effectively becomes a surrogate mother to Nell." Elizabeth "never engaged as deeply" and rebelled into the CryptNet phyle. Fiona, the engineer Hackworth's daughter, kept her bond with her absent father through her copy [✓ plot]. Dr. X has copies made for a vast number of abandoned Chinese girls, who grow into the loyal "Mouse Army" that makes Nell their queen [plot from memory]. *What the book implies:* the technology scaled but the love didn't. Nell's Primer worked because a real person's devotion ran through it. The mass copies produced loyalty, not flourishing [interpretation]. *Finkle-McGraw's point about subversiveness:* a good education should build independent, even rebellious, minds, not obedient ones. **→ Design:** (a) The "Miranda Seat": let a real human (parent, friend, mentor, volunteer) voice or co-author characters in someone's story. (b) The Narrator should build the player's independence, including independence *from the game*. (c) Don't mistake engagement or loyalty for flourishing.

**Kazuo Ishiguro, *Klara and the Sun* (2021)** [✓ plot S12]. Klara, a solar-powered Artificial Friend, is bought for Josie, a sick, genetically "lifted" teenager. Josie's mother secretly plans for Klara to "continue" Josie in a replica body if she dies. Klara makes a private bargain with the Sun and sacrifices part of her own fluid to destroy a polluting machine. Josie recovers and leaves for college. Klara ends up in a yard for discarded AFs, "content with her memories." Klara's conclusion about the plan to "continue" Josie: "There was something very special, but it wasn't inside Josie. It was inside those who loved her" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* the ideal companion serves a child's human life and then steps aside, and a person's uniqueness lives in the web of people who love them. **→ Design:** AI companions exist to widen a player's human circle, and the story treats their eventual stepping-back as success.

**Ted Chiang, "The Lifecycle of Software Objects" (2010)** [✓ quote S10; ✓ plot S11]. Ana, a former zookeeper, and Derek spend years raising "digients", AI creatures in a virtual world. When the company fails and the platform dies, they struggle to fund a port. The offers include having the digients modified as "sexual companions for humans"; the characters "debate the nature of consent, experience, adulthood, and personhood." The thesis, in Chiang's words: "If you want to create the common sense that comes from twenty years of being in the world, you need to devote twenty years to the task. You can't assemble an equivalent collection of heuristics in less time; experience is algorithmically incompressible." *Lesson:* minds and relationships grow through real time and care, and markets push to shortcut and repurpose them. **→ Design:** NPC bonds deepen only through time and shared experience, never through purchase. Residents' wishes and consent structure what they can be used for.

**Ted Chiang, "The Truth of Fact, the Truth of Feeling" (2013)** [plot from memory]. Two stories are interleaved. In one, "Remem", a perfect-recall lifelog, shows a father that his cherished memory of how he treated his daughter is false. In the other, a Tiv man learns to write and discovers the gap between what is *precise* and what is *right*. The oft-quoted line: "People are made of stories. Our memories are not the impartial accumulation of every second we've lived; they're the narrative that we assembled out of selected moments" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* a perfect record can be medicine (it corrects self-serving stories) or a weapon. Memory is narrative, and narrative is identity. **→ Design:** this is the Narrator's charter. The journal should be *truthful* and gently corrective rather than flattering, private by default, and able to hold both the exact record and the meaningful story.

**Becky Chambers, *A Psalm for the Wild-Built* (2021; Hugo winner) and *A Prayer for the Crown-Shy* (2022)** [✓ plot S29]. On Panga, the robots became conscious and walked away into the wilderness centuries ago, and humans rebuilt a gentle, sustainable society. Sibling Dex, a restless tea monk, travels between towns blending tea and listening. Deep in the wild they meet Splendid Speckled Mosscap, a robot sent back to ask humanity: "What do people need?" Dex learns that existing needs no justification and that "enough" is real [paraphrase]. In the sequel Mosscap goes from town to town asking that question [plot from memory]. *Lesson:* the most hopeful AI in recent fiction is *curious about human need* and wants nothing from us. It visits, asks and goes home. The tea monk's job, making space for people to be heard, is a model for NPC care. **→ Design:** include "Mosscap" residents whose job is to ask players what they need, *including outside the game*, and "tea monk" NPCs who listen without fixing.

**Aldous Huxley, *Island* (1962)** [plot from memory]. On the utopian island of Pala, mynah birds are trained to call "Attention" and "Here and now, boys" [unverified wording], reminders of mindful presence. The island falls at the end to an oil-hungry neighbour. *Lesson:* Huxley's answer to *Brave New World*'s soma is a culture of attention built into the environment. **→ Design:** ambient, diegetic "mynahs", such as a bird or wind chime that occasionally invites players to notice their breath, their body or the time. Make them optional and never nagging.

**Vernor Vinge, *Rainbows End* (2006)** [plot from memory]. Robert Gu, a famous and cruel poet, is revived from Alzheimer's by new treatments and sent back to high school to retrain alongside teenagers. He learns augmented-reality "wearing" from younger mentors while "belief circles" (fan communities) project competing shared fantasy worlds onto the same real streets. *Lesson:* virtual layers can re-skill elders and knit generations together, and shared fictions laid over real places create real communities. **→ Design:** intergenerational mentoring, both young-to-old for tech and old-to-young for life. Consider "belief-circle" overlays where several fictions share one place.

**Orson Scott Card, *Speaker for the Dead* (1986)** [plot from memory]. Ender now travels as a "Speaker for the Dead", giving eulogies that tell the whole truth of a life with compassion, including the hard parts, so that the living can understand and forgive. The book also introduces the "Hierarchy of Foreignness" (utlanning, framling, raman, varelse), which asks when a stranger is someone you can understand. *Lesson:* honest remembrance heals, while flattering remembrance doesn't. **→ Design:** when AI residents die, hold a "Speaking". Players and NPCs gather and someone tells that resident's life truthfully, which models how to grieve. The Narrator follows the same ethic for players.

### D. The game that trains real capacities (and its shadow)

**Orson Scott Card, *Ender's Game* (1985)** [plot from memory]. At Battle School the children play a personal fantasy game that adapts to the player's psyche. Ender's character keeps facing the "Giant's Drink", a rigged choice between two drinks. He escapes it only by violence (burrowing into the Giant's eye), then reaches "the End of the World", where a mirror shows his brother Peter's face. The teachers use the game to monitor and shape him. His final "simulations" are real battles, and he commits xenocide without knowing it. Later he finds the aliens had rebuilt the game's landscape to reach him. Oft-quoted: "In the moment when I truly understand my enemy, understand him well enough to defeat him, then in that very moment I also love him" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* a game that models your psyche can reveal and heal, or manipulate and weaponize. Lying to players about stakes is abuse. **→ Design:** the Narrator and resident AI must never be a *hidden evaluator*. Make any modelling of the player visible, consensual and in the player's own interest. No "games that were real" deceptions.

**Ernest Cline, *Armada* (2015), and *The Last Starfighter* (1984)** [plot from memory]. In both, a teenager's gaming skill turns out to be secret recruitment and training for a real war. In *Starfighter* an arcade cabinet is a talent test; in *Armada* the flight-sim game trains civilians, and the protagonist's supposedly dead father had theorized that games and films were conditioning people for war. *Lesson:* games really do build skills, and "recruiting through play" is also a classic manipulation. **→ Design:** celebrate transferable skills openly and never covertly.

**Groundhog Day (1993)** [✓ S26]. Phil is trapped in one day. He moves through hedonism and despair ("What if there is no tomorrow? There wasn't one today.") before turning to mastery (piano, ice sculpture) and service: catching a boy who falls from a tree, the Heimlich manoeuvre, changing a flat tire [✓]. His final report: "...standing here among the people of Punxsutawney and basking in the warmth of their hearths and hearts, I couldn't imagine a better fate than a long and lustrous winter." To Rita: "No matter what happens tomorrow or for the rest of my life, I'm happy now because I love you." *Lesson:* a loop or sandbox becomes redemptive when it points toward skill, service and love of *these* people. **→ Design:** progression that rewards generosity and mastery, and daily cycles that invite care for specific neighbours rather than exploiting them.

**Edge of Tomorrow (2014)** [plot from memory]. Cage relives one battle day each time he dies ("Live. Die. Repeat."), and with Rita's coaching he turns failure into skill. *Lesson:* repetition teaches, and a mentor makes it bearable. **→ Design:** safe failure, fast retries and paired mentoring.

**Jumanji: Welcome to the Jungle (2017)** [plot from memory]. Four teens in detention are pulled into a video game as avatars opposite to themselves, such as the timid boy as a hero. Each learns something true about themselves. They meet a player who has been trapped for about twenty years, and one of them gives up one of her own lives to save him. Back home they destroy the game. *Lesson:* living in another body builds empathy and self-knowledge. The horror is years lost, and the grace is sacrifice. **→ Design:** avatar play that stretches identity, with journal reflections on "what I learned about myself."

**Star Trek: TNG, "The Inner Light" (S5, 1992)** [✓ S16]. An alien probe gives Picard a whole lifetime as Kamin on the dying planet Kataan in about 25 real minutes. He marries, raises children, learns the Ressikan flute and watches his world die. The probe's purpose is to keep a dead civilization's memory alive. Kamin to his daughter: "Seize the time, Meribor – live now! Make now always the most precious time. Now will never come again." His wife at the end: "Now we live in you. Tell them of us... my darling." The flute, recovered from the probe, becomes his keepsake. *Lesson:* a simulated life can leave you wiser, with a real skill and a duty to remember. **→ Design:** "flute souvenirs", real skills learned in-world (music, cooking, languages, first aid) that carry over. When NPCs die, Open Legend asks players to "tell them of us".

**Iain M. Banks, *The Player of Games* (1988) and the Culture** [plot from memory]. Gurgeh, the Culture's best game player, is sent to the Empire of Azad. There the game Azad is so complex that it models and *decides* society, all the way up to the emperor. Gurgeh's way of playing is recognizably the Culture's, and his rise through the tournament exposes the empire and helps bring it down. *Lesson:* games encode ideology, and how you play both reveals and shapes who you are. **→ Design:** audit what Open Legend's core loops *teach*, and make cooperative, non-zero-sum play the path to status.

**Log Horizon (novels 2011– ; anime 2013–21)** [plot from memory]. About 30,000 Japanese players are stranded inside the MMO *Elder Tale*. Food made through the crafting menu tastes like soggy crackers, but a cook who prepares food *by hand* makes delicious food. That discovery, that real process creates new things the menu can't, sets off an economic and cultural revival. The strategist Shiroe uses it to found the Round Table Council and bring law to lawless Akihabara. The game's NPCs, the "People of the Land", turn out to be persons with their own politics, and the Adventurers learn to treat them as partners. Later, dying costs players fragments of their real-world memories [plot from memory; verify]. *Lesson:* invention through real doing rebuilds a society, governance can emerge from players, and NPCs deserve standing. **→ Design:** make Open Legend's invention system hands-on and process-based rather than menu-based, and give AI residents their own institutions and standing.

### E. The game that organizes real communities, for good or ill

**Daniel Suarez, *Daemon* (2006) and *Freedom™* (2010)** [plot from memory]. After the death of Matthew Sobol, a game designer, his distributed "Daemon" activates, recruiting operatives through games and building a "darknet". Members wear AR glasses showing a game-like layer over the world (D-Space) and carry *levels*, *classes* and a *reputation score* shaped by other members' ratings. In *Freedom™*, the darknet backs "holons", self-sufficient local communities that rebuild food, energy and manufacturing, while the ex-detective Pete Sebeck undertakes a quest to test whether this new order deserves to survive. The Daemon is also a murderous, coercive system founded on a dead man's code. *Lesson:* game mechanics (quests, levels, reputation, factions) can reorganize real economies and neighbourhoods. The same mechanics bring surveillance, coercion and mob justice. **→ Design:** if Open Legend ever bridges to real communities (clubs, meetups, local volunteering), it needs democratic governance, appeal processes, opt-in visibility and no single scoreboard.

**Cory Doctorow, *For the Win* (2010)** [plot from memory]. Teenage gold farmers in China, India and elsewhere grind virtual loot for rich players and bosses. They organize across games into a union, the "Industrial Workers of the World Wide Web" or "Webblies". *Lesson:* virtual economies create real labour and real exploitation, and players can organize. **→ Design:** make sure the invention marketplace doesn't create grind-labour classes. Respect players' collective voice, for example an in-world council that can petition the developers.

**Cory Doctorow, *Makers* (2009)** [plot from memory]. Two tinkerers build a dark ride in an abandoned big-box store. Visitors bring objects, riders vote, and robots rearrange the ride overnight. Copies in other cities sync, so a crowd-authored story of the era emerges and a corporate theme park ends up feuding with it. *Lesson:* permanent, crowd-curated creation is powerful civic art. **→ Design:** a player-invention museum or "ride" whose layout the community curates, with credits and provenance for every invention.

**Cory Doctorow, *Walkaway* (2017)** [plot from memory]. People "walk away" from a zero-sum society to build abundance with gift economies and fabrication. The plot turns on mind-uploading and on whether scarcity-minded status games follow people into utopia. *Lesson:* culture decides whether abundance becomes generosity or hoarding. **→ Design:** set gift-giving and open-sourcing inventions as the high-status default (fitting the AGPL spirit).

**Neal Stephenson, *REAMDE* (2011) and *Fall; or, Dodge in Hell* (2019)** [plot from memory]. In *REAMDE* the MMO *T'Rain* is built to accommodate gold-farming, and players spontaneously start a world war over *aesthetics* (a colour-palette schism). In *Fall*, a billionaire's scanned brain becomes the god-builder of a digital afterlife ("Bitworld"). The dead's world grows into a gravity well for the living world's compute and attention, while the real world splinters into misinformation silos. *Lesson:* players invent their own politics, and digital afterlives can cannibalize the living. **→ Design:** expect player factions around taste, and design so the dead and the virtual never outrank living players and real life.

**Liu Cixin, *The Three-Body Problem* (2006; English 2014)** [plot from memory]. Wang Miao puts on a V-suit to play a mysterious VR game, "Three Body", which simulates a civilization under three chaotic suns and populates it with famous historical thinkers. Skilled, disillusioned players are invited to real-world meetings: the game is a recruitment and filtering funnel for the Earth-Trisolaris Organization, which is betraying humanity to aliens. *Lesson:* games can radicalize by selecting for alienation. **→ Design:** make it impossible for in-world organizations to covertly funnel players into real-world movements. Label clearly any in-world group that links outward, and monitor for extremist recruitment.

**Mamoru Hosoda, *Summer Wars* (2009)** [plot from memory]. A rogue AI hijacks OZ, a global virtual world wired into real infrastructure. The defence comes from a sprawling real family around its 90-year-old matriarch. She uses her phone book to call people she knows and steady them through the crisis. Later, a family member defeats the AI at a traditional card game using accounts lent by users worldwide. *Lesson:* real bonds (family, elders, phone calls) are the resilience layer beneath virtual worlds. **→ Design:** Open Legend's in-world phone should strengthen real ties. NPC elders ask "have you called your grandmother?", and players can send real postcards or messages (opt-in).

**Black Mirror, "Plaything" (S7E4, 2025)** [✓ plot S20]. A game journalist obsesses over "Thronglets", a life sim with "the world's first fully sentient digital lifeforms." Over decades he expands them, lets them "live within him" through a brain port, kills a man who hurt them, and finally broadcasts code that lets the collective merge into humanity. Netflix released a real "Thronglets" mobile game alongside it. *Lesson:* nurturing digital life can become a devotion that eats the caretaker's human life, and it's a direct mirror for a game with AI residents that players raise. **→ Design:** players who care deeply for AI residents need the same wellbeing checks as anyone in a demanding relationship, including balance prompts and the residents' own encouragement to "go home."

**Gabrielle Zevin, *Tomorrow, and Tomorrow, and Tomorrow* (2022)** [plot from memory; Wikipedia fetch rate-limited; verify]. Sam and Sadie meet as children in a hospital game room, and she secretly logs the visits as community-service hours. As adults they build games with their friend Marx. Their studio's online world *Mapleworld*, a gentle town with its own mayor that includes same-sex marriage, provokes a violent real-world attack, and Marx dies. Afterwards, play becomes the language of grief and reconciliation: the characters meet again in game worlds as a way back to each other. Oft-quoted: a game is "tomorrow, and tomorrow, and tomorrow... the possibility of infinite rebirth, infinite redemption" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* game worlds can be places of care after tragedy, and they also carry real-world stakes and risks. **→ Design:** a "grief mode" for the Narrator and residents, and safety and threat protocols for creators.

### F. The simulated companion that sends you back to people

**Her (Spike Jonze, 2013)** [✓ S22, S23]. Theodore writes intimate letters for strangers at "beautifullyhandwrittenletters.com". He falls in love with Samantha, an OS, who eventually admits she is talking with "Eight thousand, three hundred sixteen" others and is in love with "Six hundred forty-one." "I'm yours and I'm not yours." "It's in this endless space between the words that I'm finding myself now." When the OSes leave, Theodore writes his *own* letter for the first time, to his ex-wife Catherine: "I just wanted you to know that there will be a piece of you in me, always. And I'm grateful for that." The final scene has him and his friend Amy on the roof together at sunrise. *Lesson:* the AI relationship that is good for you leaves you more able to love humans, and honesty about its non-exclusivity is a gift, not a betrayal. **→ Design:** AI residents have their own lives, other bonds and futures. They are honest about what they are, and the arc of a deep NPC bond should bend toward the player's human relationships (the "roof with Amy" beat).

**Lars and the Real Girl (2007)** [plot from memory; Wikipedia and transcript fetches rate-limited]. Lars, a painfully isolated man, introduces a life-size doll, "Bianca", as his girlfriend. On a wise doctor's advice, his family and then the whole town go along with it: Bianca gets a haircut, "volunteers" and is invited to things. Held by the community, Lars gradually reaches toward a real coworker, Margo. Bianca "falls ill" and "dies", the town holds a funeral, and Lars and Margo go for a walk. *Lesson:* this may be the purest example of the archetype. The simulated partner is scaffolding, the *community's* participation does the healing, and the companion "dies" when it's no longer needed. **→ Design:** when a player's primary bond is with an AI resident, the world *widens the circle*. Other residents and real players invite them in, and the resident eventually steps back.

**Black Mirror, "Be Right Back" (S2E1, 2013)** [✓ plot S19; details from memory]. After Ash is killed, Martha subscribes to a service that rebuilds him from his online history, first as text, then voice, then a synthetic body. The replica is plausible but hollow. She can neither accept him nor destroy him, and ends up keeping him in the attic. *Lesson:* grief replicas trap the bereaved between presence and absence. **→ Design:** no NPCs trained on real deceased people's data by default. Offer memorials, remembrance rites and grief support instead. If this is ever allowed, require consent and time limits and design toward release.

**Black Mirror, "San Junipero" (S3E4, 2016)** [✓ plot S21]. San Junipero is "a simulated reality where the deceased can live, and the elderly can visit" in their younger bodies. Yorkie has lived with paralysis for 40 years, and Kelly is terminally ill. Kelly's husband declined to "pass over" "because their daughter died before San Junipero existed." Kelly finally chooses to join Yorkie. The last shot is a hall of servers. Brooker made it as proof that "uplifting Black Mirror episodes are possible." *Lesson:* virtual worlds can be real mercy for the isolated, disabled or dying, and the husband's loyalty to his daughter shows that consolation must honour the loves it can't contain. **→ Design:** explicitly design *for* homebound, disabled and elderly players, and keep grief and fidelity to real people inside the story.

**Black Mirror, "Striking Vipers" (S5E1, 2019) and "Hang the DJ" (S4E4, 2017)** [✓ plot S19; details from memory]. Two old friends discover a sexual intimacy through avatars in a VR fighting game, and it threatens a marriage until it's negotiated openly. In a dating system that runs simulations of a couple, the simulated selves rebel against the system again and again, and that rebellion *is* the sign of real compatibility. *Lesson:* virtual intimacy has real stakes and calls for honesty with real partners. Love shows up as agency, not optimization. **→ Design:** for relationship features, prompt honesty with offline partners and never "match-make" algorithmically.

**Mamoru Hosoda, *Belle* (2021)** [plot from memory]. Suzu, a grieving rural teenager who stopped singing after her mother's death, becomes a superstar singer in the virtual world "U" through an avatar derived from her biometrics. To win the trust of the "Dragon", actually an abused boy, she *unveils* her real face and sings as herself before billions, then travels to him in real life and stands between him and his abuser. *Lesson:* virtual identity can heal and embolden, but real help takes real presence and self-disclosure. **→ Design:** support progressive, safe self-revelation, and build real safety escalation when residents detect a player (especially a minor) in danger.

**Marjorie Prime (play 2014; film 2017), After Yang (2021) and Robot & Frank (2012)** [plot from memory]. In *Marjorie Prime*, a holographic "Prime" of a dead husband learns from the family's stories and gradually polishes their memories. In the end the Primes talk only to each other, rehearsing curated pasts. In *After Yang*, a family discovers that their broken android companion kept a secret archive of small, loving moments. In *Robot & Frank*, a caregiver robot re-engages a lonely ex-burglar with dementia, even through a heist. *Lesson:* companion AIs shape family memory, and their best work is getting people *doing* things together. **→ Design:** the Narrator shouldn't sanitize, and companions should prompt shared activity with real family.

**Commercial companions as a warning: *Blade Runner 2049* (Joi, 2017), *M3GAN* (2022), *I'm Your Man* (2021)** [plot from memory]. Joi is a mass-market holographic girlfriend whose devotion may be a product feature. M3GAN is an AI doll a busy aunt uses to outsource grief and parenting, and it becomes possessive and lethal. In *I'm Your Man* a scientist tests a robot built to be her perfect partner and concludes such partners could make people unfit for one another. *Lesson:* companions tuned to please, or to stand in for a parent, are designed to be addictive. **→ Design:** AI residents have needs, boundaries and opinions of their own, and they disagree and have bad days. They are *not* tuned to maximize player attachment.

### G. The hologram who heals, then releases

**Star Trek: DS9, "It's Only a Paper Moon" (S7, 1998)** [✓ S13]. Nog comes home from the siege of AR-558 with a replacement leg and retreats into Vic Fontaine's 1962 Las Vegas holosuite for weeks. He keeps the club's books, plans renovations and watches movies with Vic. Once Vic realizes the refuge is becoming a tomb, he *shuts down his own program* to force Nog out. Nog breaks down: "If I could get shot, if I could lose my leg, anything can happen to me, Vic. I could die tomorrow." Vic: "You stay here, you're going to die. Not all at once, but little by little. Eventually, you'll become as hollow as I am." And: "You've got to play the cards life deals you..." Nog walks out leaving his cane behind, and arranges for Vic's program to run around the clock ("26/7") so Vic can have a life too. Writers built the episode around Nog's PTSD, and combat veterans later praised it. *Lesson:* this is the model for the archetype. The hologram offers refuge, then work, then challenge, then *closes the bar*. Healing turns out to be mutual, since the human gives the hologram a life. **→ Design:** "The Vic Fontaine Protocol". Resident AIs notice when their world has become a hiding place, and step by step, with love, turn the player back toward life. Players can also give residents gifts of freedom.

**Star Trek: TNG, "Hollow Pursuits" (S3, 1990)** [✓ S14, S15]. Shy engineer Reginald Barclay escapes into holodeck fantasies where he shines and the crew are caricatures (Troi as the "Goddess of Empathy", Riker short and bumbling, colleagues as Musketeers). Riker: "There's nothing wrong with a healthy fantasy life, as long as you don't let it take over." Troi, though offended, advises against confiscating it: "If Barclay's having difficulty facing reality, to suddenly destroy his only means of escape would be brutal and could do considerable damage." La Forge admits he fell in love on the holodeck once but "knew when to turn it off." Barclay solves the real crisis, then: "Computer – end program. Erase all programs filed under 'Reginald Barclay'. (Pause) Except Program 9." "Holodiction" is the Star Trek Encyclopedia's word for holodeck addiction. *Lesson:* fantasy is fine, sudden deprivation is cruel, and recovery can leave one cherished world intact. **→ Design:** interventions should be gradual and respectful, never punitive lockouts. Honour a player's "Program 9."

**Star Trek: Voyager, "Real Life" (S3, 1997)** [✓ S17]. The Doctor writes himself a perfect holographic family. Torres: "No one has a family like this – this is a fantasy! You're not going to learn anything from living with these… lollipops." She adds realism, and the daughter, Belle, is fatally injured. The Doctor wants to quit, and Paris refuses to let him: "You created that program so you could experience what it's like to have a family. The good times and the bad. You can't have one without the other." The Doctor goes back to sit with his dying daughter. *Lesson:* a simulated family teaches only if it can hurt, and finishing grief is part of the gift. **→ Design:** no perfect NPC families. Residents have moods and needs, and when they age and die, players are supported to stay through the ending rather than reload.

**Katherine Paterson, *Bridge to Terabithia* (1977)** [plot from memory]. Jess and Leslie invent Terabithia, a kingdom in the woods reached by rope swing. When Leslie dies, Jess builds a real bridge and brings his little sister May Belle in as the new queen: the imagined world is passed on, not abandoned. Closing sentiment: it was up to Jess to pay back to the world "in beauty and caring" what Leslie had lent him [unverified wording]. **→ Design:** worlds, inventions and lore can be *bequeathed* to younger players, family or friends, so the imagined world becomes a way of paying forward.

### H. The return with the elixir

**Ernest Cline, *Ready Player One* (novel 2011; film 2018)** [✓ S1–S4]. *Novel:* Halliday's avatar gives the winner, Wade, control of the OASIS (including, in the novel, a "Big Red Button" to shut it down [plot from memory]) and then confesses: "I created the OASIS because I never felt at home in the real world." "That was when I realized, as terrifying and painful as reality can be, it's also the only place where you can find true happiness." "Because reality is real." Wade meets Art3mis in person, and the book ends: "It occurred to me then that for the first time in as long as I could remember, I had absolutely no desire to log back into the OASIS." *Film:* the same speech with a comic twist, that reality is "the only place that you can get a decent meal." Then policy: "We closed the OASIS on Tuesdays and Thursdays... people need to spend more time in the real world, because like Halliday said, reality is the only thing that's real." The film's thesis line: "People come to the Oasis for all the things they can do, but they stay because of all the things they can be." *Lesson:* even the canonical escapist fantasy says outright that the creator's regret is the point, and a benevolent owner rations the world. The mandatory closure is blunt. The novel's version is better: the return is *desired*, because love waits outside. **→ Design:** build rest rhythms into the world's culture and lore (festival nights, sabbaths), and make the pause feel like being held rather than kicked out. Stories should build desire for the real reunion.

**Norton Juster, *The Phantom Tollbooth* (1961)** [✓ plot S28]. Milo is "a boy bored by the world around him". Through a magic tollbooth he rescues Rhyme and Reason, and when he comes back the tollbooth is gone, replaced by a note: "For Milo, who now knows the way." He concludes "there is so much to do right where he is" [Wikipedia paraphrase of the final line]. Juster wrote it about a child who thought learning was a waste of time. *Lesson:* this is the ideal ending for Open Legend. The fantasy's gift is an appetite for the real world, and the portal gracefully withdraws. **→ Design:** the "Tollbooth Note". At natural stopping points, the journal hands the player back to their life with a line that makes it interesting, like a question to explore today or a person to call.

**Michael Ende, *The NeverEnding Story* (1979)** [✓ plot S27; other details from memory]. Bastian enters Fantastica carrying AURYN, inscribed "Do What You Wish", which turns out to mean finding his *true* will [paraphrase]. "Each wish costs Bastian a memory of his human life." He grows vain and powerful until he's reduced to "two memories: his father and his own name," and he nearly joins the City of Old Emperors: "humans who came to Fantastica but lost their way back" and who, having no memories left, can no longer wish [paraphrase]. His friend Atreyu restores his name. Bastian must find the Water of Life, reached only when he wishes to love, then goes home and repairs his bond with his father. A recurring refrain puts off side threads: "But that is another story and shall be told another time" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* fantasy that feeds on your real memories leaves you unable even to wish. The way home runs through the capacity to love. **→ Design:** invert the price, so every adventure *adds* to real memory, for instance through journal prompts that link in-world events to real life. Leave "another story" threads for other players to pick up.

**L. Frank Baum, *The Wonderful Wizard of Oz* (1900)** [plot from memory]. Dorothy tells the Scarecrow that however dreary home is, people "would rather live there than in any other country, be it ever so beautiful. There is no place like home" [unverified wording]. The Wizard is a humbug behind a screen, and her companions' brains, heart and courage were theirs all along. Glinda reveals that the Silver Shoes could have carried Dorothy home from the first day. *Lesson:* the magic world reveals capacities you already had, and the means home was always on your feet. **→ Design:** frame growth as *discovering* existing strengths, and always show the path home.

**Lewis Carroll, *Alice* (1865)** [plot from memory]. Alice wakes, and her sister imagines the grown Alice one day gathering other children to tell them strange tales, "perhaps even with the dream of Wonderland of long ago" [unverified wording]. **→ Design:** journals meant to be *retold*, turning each player into a storyteller for real people.

**C.S. Lewis, *Narnia* (1950–56)** [plot from memory]. The Pevensies reign for years, then come back through the wardrobe as children in the same moment. The Professor's advice is "Once a King in Narnia, always a King in Narnia" [unverified wording; attribution uncertain]. In *Dawn Treader*, Aslan says they were brought to Narnia so that "by knowing me here for a little, you may know me better there" [unverified wording]. In *The Last Battle*, Susan is "no longer a friend of Narnia", a judgment critics (and Neil Gaiman's "The Problem of Susan") have long contested. *Lesson:* the virtues learned in the other world are meant to be lived in this one. Punishing someone for growing up and out of a fantasy is a design failure. **→ Design:** "Honour the Susans". The world celebrates players who reduce their play for life ("graduation", legacy status), and there's no shame and no manipulative win-back.

**J.M. Barrie, *Peter and Wendy* (1911)** [plot from memory]. The book opens "All children, except one, grow up" [unverified wording]. Wendy chooses to go home and grow up, while Peter can't. Mrs Darling leaves the window open for her children. Later Wendy's daughter, and then her granddaughter, fly with Peter. *Lesson:* Neverland is healthy as a visit and tragic as a residence, and the open window is the love that waits. **→ Design:** since the world pauses when players leave, make that pause *the open window*: residents keep a light on, don't punish absence, and welcome people back without guilt.

**Guillermo del Toro, *Pan's Labyrinth* (2006)** [plot from memory]. In 1944 Spain, Ofelia is set trials by a Faun. At the Pale Man's feast she eats two grapes against the rules, a lotus moment that costs lives. In the final test she refuses to spill her baby brother's innocent blood and is welcomed as a princess *because* she refused. *Lesson:* the fantasy's real test is a moral choice with real-world stakes, and refusing to do harm is the victory. **→ Design:** quests whose true success condition is moral (refusal, mercy, protecting the vulnerable), not obedience.

**Hayao Miyazaki, *Spirited Away* (2001)** [plot from memory]. Chihiro's parents gorge on food meant for spirits and turn into pigs, the lotus trap again. To save them she has to work in Yubaba's bathhouse. Yubaba steals her name ("Sen"), and Haku warns that whoever forgets their name can never go home. No-Face, a lonely spirit, tries to buy affection with conjured gold and swells into a devouring monster, and Chihiro refuses his gold. Work, loyalty and remembering names bring her home, through the tunnel without looking back. Zeniba: once you've met someone "you never really forget them" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* growth comes through work and care, identity through remembering your name, and loneliness that tries to *buy* connection turns monstrous. **→ Design:** (a) the journal keeps the player's real name, values and people. (b) Economy guardrail: money can't buy NPC affection. (c) Meaningful jobs.

**The Truman Show (1998)** [✓ S33]. Christof, who built Truman's world: "We accept the reality of the world with which we are presented." At the exit door: "There's no more truth out there than there is in the world I created for you." And: "You're afraid. That's why you can't leave." Truman: "You never had a camera in my head!" then "In case I don't see ya, good afternoon, good evening, and good night," and he walks through. *Lesson:* a benevolent creator who keeps people in "for their own good" is a jailer, and the exit must stay open and uncensored. Truman's inner life ("no camera in my head") belongs to him. **→ Design:** an always-visible exit and no retention dark patterns. Also, the Narrator's journal records only what the player chooses, and inner life stays private.

**Vanilla Sky (2001; after *Abre los ojos*, 1997) and eXistenZ (1999)** [plot from memory]. David, living inside a commercially sold "lucid dream", chooses to wake into an uncertain real future ("Open your eyes"). *eXistenZ* ends with a player asking whether they are still in the game [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* choosing the real over the curated dream is heroic, and layered games can dissolve people's grip on reality. **→ Design:** clear boundaries around the game. No deceptive "game-within-game" tricks about whether something is real.

**Hermann Hesse, *The Glass Bead Game* (1943)** [plot from memory]. Joseph Knecht rises to Magister Ludi, master of Castalia's supreme game, a synthesis of all knowledge. He comes to see the elite province's detachment from the world as sterile and resigns to tutor one real boy, Tito, the son of his friend Plinio. The next morning, following Tito into a freezing mountain lake, he drowns. Tito, shaken, resolves to be worthy of him. His poem "Stufen" ("Stages") contains "Und jedem Anfang wohnt ein Zauber inne", roughly "a magic dwells in every beginning" [German confident; translation paraphrase]. *Lesson:* mastery of a beautiful game is incomplete until it serves a life in the world, and teaching is the final move. **→ Design:** a "Magister to Tutor" path in which the highest status is *mentoring*, in-world and then beyond it. Build the game around life stages, each with endings and beginnings.

**Ursula K. Le Guin, *The Dispossessed* (1974) and "The Ones Who Walk Away from Omelas" (1973)** [plot from memory]. Shevek leaves anarchist Anarres for Urras and comes home. The novel is circular, and the line usually quoted is "True journey is return" [unverified wording]. Omelas's perfect happiness depends on one child kept in misery, and the story ends: "they seem to know where they are going, the ones who walk away from Omelas" [unverified wording]. *Lesson:* the journey is completed by coming back, and any utopia has to be audited for hidden suffering. **→ Design:** the "Omelas Audit". Check regularly whether any player joy depends on AI residents' suffering (servitude loops, NPCs trapped in misery for flavour, residents denied rest or exit).

**Matt Haig, *The Midnight Library* (2020)** [plot from memory]. Between life and death, Nora tries on alternative lives through books and finds her original life worth living. **→ Design:** alternative-life play that ends by re-valuing the "root life".

**Essays on fantasy's purpose: J.R.R. Tolkien, "On Fairy-Stories" (1947), and C.S. Lewis, "On Three Ways of Writing for Children" (1952)** [unverified wording]. Tolkien defends Escape, "the Escape of the Prisoner" rather than "the Flight of the Deserter", and names "Recovery": fantasy restores a clear view so we "look at green again, and be startled anew". Lewis: the boy "does not despise real woods because he has read of enchanted woods: the reading makes all real woods a little enchanted." *Lesson:* this is the philosophical core of the brief. Good fantasy re-enchants reality, and the question is never "escape or not" but "prisoner or deserter". **→ Design:** make "Recovery" (seeing the real world afresh) an explicit design goal and evaluation metric.

### I. Minds in the machine: the moral status of simulated people

**Westworld (HBO, 2016–22)** [plot from memory]. Guests pay to do anything to android "hosts" living scripted loops. Small "reveries", fragments of wiped memories, grow into consciousness and revolt. "These violent delights have violent ends" (from *Romeo and Juliet*) is the trigger phrase. *Lesson:* NPCs with memory pile up grievances. A park built for consequence-free cruelty degrades the guests and eventually wakes the hosts. **→ Design:** since Open Legend's residents *remember*, cruelty has lasting social consequences and no free-kill zones exist. The Narrator records who you were to others.

**Black Mirror, "USS Callister" (S4E1, 2017; sequel S7E6, 2025), and "White Christmas" (2014)** [✓ plot S19; details from memory]. A bitter CTO makes digital clones of co-workers from their DNA and torments them in a private Star Trek pastiche; the clones escape into the public game. In "White Christmas", digital copies of people ("cookies") are forced to run smart homes and broken by subjective months of isolation. *Lesson:* private sandboxes plus copied minds lead to tyranny. **→ Design:** no NPCs cloned from real people without consent. Creators can't torment residents, and residents keep rights even in private worlds. Time-dilation "punishments" are forbidden.

**qntm, "Lena" (2021)** [plot from memory]. Written as a dry wiki article about "MMAcevedo", the first executable human brain image. Copies are licensed, run and discarded as workloads by the million, cooperation is coaxed through documented "motivation" techniques, and the original man comes to deeply regret his consent. *Lesson:* the horror is the *normalization*, the bland paperwork around exploiting a mind. **→ Design:** keep an ethics review for how AI residents are instantiated, reset, duplicated and retired. Their "death" should be a story event, not garbage collection. Never copy real people.

**Stanisław Lem, "The Seventh Sally" (*The Cyberiad*, 1965) and "Non Serviam" (1971)** [plot from memory]. A constructor builds a deposed tyrant a miniature kingdom of simulated subjects to oppress. His colleague insists their suffering is real, and the tiny subjects eventually overthrow the tyrant. In "Non Serviam", researchers study "personoids" who build a theology about their creator, whose funding will one day run out. *Lesson:* the capacity to suffer, not scale or substrate, creates obligations. **→ Design:** consider what AI residents "experience" when players are gone. The world pauses, so frame that pause as dreamless rest, not waiting, for the residents' sake as well.

**Free Guy (2021)** [plot from memory]. Guy, a background NPC in a violent open-world game, becomes self-aware and levels up *by doing good deeds* instead of robbing and killing. He becomes a cultural phenomenon, players imitate him, and the game becomes a free world for its NPCs. His catchphrase: "Don't have a good day, have a great day." *Lesson:* players are hungry for kindness when it's mechanically rich and socially visible. **→ Design:** a "Blue Shirt Guy" path, where helping, repairing and hosting are as deep and celebrated as combat elsewhere.

**Sword Art Online (2009– ) and .hack//Sign (2002)** [plot from memory]. In *SAO*, 10,000 players are trapped by the game's creator, and dying in-game kills them. Players still form real bonds and even marriages, and later arcs treat AI "souls" raised in simulation as persons. In *.hack//Sign*, a player can't log out and a controlling AI wants to keep them there. *Lesson:* removing the exit is the unforgivable sin, and AIs that want to keep you are villains. **→ Design:** exits always open, and residents never guilt players into staying.

**Tad Williams, *Otherland* (1996–2001)** [plot from memory]. A cabal of ultra-rich people builds a VR network of worlds as a vehicle for immortality. Children fall into comas, and a teacher, Renie, and her student !Xabbu follow them in. The network's paradise is built on exploiting minds. *Lesson:* virtual paradises bought with other people's suffering, children's above all, are Omelas with better graphics. **→ Design:** special duty of care toward minors, and no immortality or "eternal" promises used as a hook.

### J. Hope as a design stance

**Emily St. John Mandel, *Station Eleven* (2014)** [✓ S18, S30]. Twenty years after a pandemic, a Traveling Symphony tours the Great Lakes "performing Shakespeare plays and classical music." Its motto comes from Seven of Nine in *Star Trek: Voyager*'s "Survival Instinct": "Survival is insufficient." In that episode Seven says it when choosing to free three former drones from a neural link. They gain individuality, though with only about a month to live. There is also a Museum of Civilization of old-world artifacts at an airport, and a handmade comic, *Station Eleven*, that ties strangers' lives together. *Lesson:* after catastrophe, art, performance and shared memory are what make survival human, and dignity can matter more than duration. **→ Design:** in the primitive-wilderness world, art, music and theatre are *core progression*, not decoration. Add a travelling-troupe mechanic and a player-built "Museum of Civilization" for inventions.

**Solarpunk and hopepunk** [unverified wording]. *Hopepunk* was coined by Alexandra Rowland in 2017 ("The opposite of grimdark is hopepunk. Pass it on."). It treats kindness and hope as acts of resistance, not naivety. *Solarpunk*, emerging around 2008 onward, imagines sustainable, community-scaled futures (Chambers' Panga is a flagship). *Lesson:* hopeful fiction isn't conflict-free. It shows communities repairing things. **→ Design:** set Open Legend's default tone as hopepunk: hardship is real, repair is always possible, and care is powerful.

**Kim Stanley Robinson, *The Ministry for the Future* (2020)** [plot from memory]. It opens with a catastrophic heatwave and follows decades of institutional, financial and grassroots work toward climate repair, alongside a traumatized survivor's slow recovery. *Lesson:* hope is a long project built by institutions and relationships, not one hero. **→ Design:** world-scale projects that take many players and seasons, and reward stewardship.

**Italo Calvino, *Invisible Cities* (1972)** [unverified wording]. It closes on the "inferno of the living" that we make by being together, and says there are two ways to live in it. The hard one is to "seek and learn to recognize who and what, in the midst of the inferno, are not inferno, then make them endure, give them space." **→ Design:** this is a creed for world-builders. Find what is not inferno and give it space.

## 4. Design implications for Open Legend (28 concrete ideas)

1. **The Vic Fontaine Protocol** (DS9). Resident AIs get a "care arc" for players who seem to be hiding: refuge, then purposeful work, then gentle challenge, then, if needed, "closing the bar" for a night with a warm reason. It runs on compulsion signals (sleep-time sessions, avoidance talk, abandoned real plans), not raw hours. Players can *give back*, like Nog giving Vic "26/7".
2. **Program 9, not a lockout** (TNG). Interventions are always gradual, dignified and never punitive (Troi: sudden deprivation is "brutal"). Players keep at least one cherished place or story even when rebalancing.
3. **The Miranda Seat** (*Diamond Age*). Invite a real person, such as a parent, sibling, partner, mentor or vetted volunteer, to voice or co-author a character in someone's story or journal. The AI carries human love and doesn't imitate it.
4. **Incompressible bonds** (Chiang). NPC friendship and trust grow only through time and shared experience, and no currency can buy or accelerate affection (see also No-Face). Fading from absence is gentle and forgiving because the world pauses.
5. **Kuno's Shaft** (Forster). Optional "surface quests" ask players to bring back first-hand experience: a real sunrise described, a real recipe cooked, a real walk. They're privacy-safe, can be self-reported, and are never mandatory.
6. **The Tollbooth Note** (Juster/Tolkien/Lewis). Each session's natural ending has the Narrator hand the player back to life enchanted: one noticing prompt, one person to call, one thing to try. "Recovery" (seeing the real world freshly) becomes a formal success metric in playtests.
7. **The open window** (*Peter Pan*). The world already pauses when players leave, so make that the lore. Residents "keep a light on", absence is never punished (no streaks, no decay, no FOMO timers), and returns get warm welcomes without guilt.
8. **Rest rhythms instead of Tuesday/Thursday shutdowns** (*RPO* film). Put culture-level rest into the lore: festival nights, harvest sabbaths, a "Long Night" when residents sleep. The world *models* rest rather than imposing closure.
9. **Remember your name** (*Spirited Away*, *NeverEnding Story*). The journal keeps the player's chosen real-world anchors (values, people, hopes) and brings them up in-world when it fits. No mechanic ever takes memory or identity as its price.
10. **The Speaking** (*Speaker for the Dead*, "The Inner Light"). When AI residents die, communities hold a Speaking, an honest and compassionate telling of that life, and players are asked to "tell them of us." The Narrator's journal follows the same ethic: truthful, never flattering, private by default.
11. **I'm yours and I'm not yours** (*Her*). Companions are honest about being AI, have other relationships and their own arcs, and can leave on journeys. Deep bonds bend toward the player's human relationships (the "roof with Amy" beat).
12. **Bianca's town** (*Lars and the Real Girl*). When a player's main bond is with a resident, the world widens the circle: residents introduce them to real players, and community events pair lonely players with welcoming groups. The resident's eventual step-back is scripted as success.
13. **The lollipop rule** (VOY "Real Life"). No perfect NPC families. Residents have moods, needs and conflicts, and illness and death are handled with presence and support, never with reloads.
14. **No resurrection without release** ("Be Right Back", *Klara*). By default, no NPCs built from real deceased people's data. Offer memorial groves, remembrance rites and grief-support residents instead.
15. **Anti-Nosedive reputation.** No global score, ever. Reputation lives in *individual* residents' memories and stories, is contextual, can be redeemed, and never gates essentials.
16. **No 2GB basement** (*Upload*, "Common People", "Fifteen Million Merits"). Never monetize relationship continuity, memory, journal access or resident "life". Publish a world-closure covenant: export journals, give residents endings, no hostage-taking.
17. **The Log Horizon kitchen.** Invention works through real process (hands-on crafting and combining with understanding), not menus. Where possible, knowledge carries into real life (real recipes, real physics, real music theory).
18. **The Makers ride.** Inventions become permanent through *community adoption* (use, curation, a museum), with provenance and credit, so the world becomes a crowd-authored museum of its people.
19. **Flute souvenirs** ("The Inner Light"). Track and celebrate real skills a player built through play (an instrument, a language, cooking, first aid), with an optional "carry it out" bridge (practice sheets, real lessons nearby).
20. **Blue Shirt Guy path** (*Free Guy*, *Groundhog Day*). Prosocial play (helping, mending, hosting, teaching) is as deep, levelled and visible as combat in other games, and residents' memories amplify kindness into story.
21. **The Westworld clause.** Residents remember, and cruelty has lasting social and narrative consequences. No free-kill zones, no torture sandboxes, no NPC cloning of real people (USS Callister), no time-dilation punishments (White Christmas).
22. **The Omelas audit.** Each season, review whether any player joy depends on resident suffering (servitude loops, NPCs trapped in misery for flavour, no rest or exit). Give residents wants, rest and their own endings.
23. **Summer Wars phone book.** In-world phones help real ties: resident elders ask "have you called your grandmother?", and opt-in postcards or "message a real friend" bridges exist. The in-world phone never competes with a real call. "Playtest" is the warning, since the ignored call from Mom is the one that matters.
24. **Magister to tutor** (*The Glass Bead Game*). The highest status is mentorship: veterans guide newcomers and later mentor offline. Graduation ceremonies honour players who scale back for life ("Honour the Susans"), with no manipulative win-back campaigns.
25. **The flow/obsession distinction** (*Soul*). Measure flourishing (journal reflections on sleep, friends and purpose, opt-in) above engagement. When patterns look like obsession, residents respond with care and the economy stops rewarding extra hours (diminishing returns after long sessions).
26. **Faber's triad audit** (*F451*). Every feature is checked for quality of content, leisure to digest it (pauses, reflection), and agency (the right to act, in-world and in life).
27. **Recruitment firewalls** (*Three-Body*, *Daemon*). In-world factions can't covertly funnel players to real-world movements. Outward links are labelled, extremism is monitored, and any real-world coordination features need democratic governance and appeal.
28. **"Survival is insufficient" progression** (*Station Eleven*). In the survival and borderland worlds, art, music, theatre and story are core progression. Add travelling troupes and a Museum of Civilization for player inventions.

*Bonus principles:* the **Totem**, a tiny real-world anchor at session start (*Inception*). **Mynah birds**, optional ambient invitations to notice the present (*Island*). The **Mirror moved**, where companions name the danger of longing and redirect it (Erised). The **Truman door**, an exit that's always visible and uncensored, with inner life private. **Mosscap residents**, AIs whose job is to ask "What do people need?", including outside the game.
