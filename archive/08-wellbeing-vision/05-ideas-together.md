# Ideas: together — community, civic life and real gatherings

Status: **open ideation** · 2026-09-28 · part of the [Wellbeing Vision](README.md). Tags: **Now / Next / Later / Moonshot**; evidence **[Strong] [Moderate] [Weak] [Wisdom] [Idea]**.

The institutions that reliably create belonging in the real world share a shape: people do a task together, and talk happens alongside it. The Men's Shed movement's slogan says it best: **"Men don't talk face to face, they talk shoulder to shoulder."** Barn raisings, parkrun, choirs, Repair Cafés and communal meals all work this way. So do Open Legend's forge, oven, harvest and building loops. This catalogue is about making those loops into real community — inside the world and, by invitation, outside it.

A second theme runs through the civic ideas. The proposed medieval world, [Threewater March](../../docs/repertoires/worlds/medieval-survival.md), is built around a commons problem: river crossings, landing rights, seed stores, tolls, a public oven. That makes it a natural **school of self-government**, where players can practise the skills of shared life with residents who remember what everyone did.

---

## A. Shoulder-to-shoulder rituals

### 1. Raisings
*Anyone can "call a raising" for a build too big for one household.* **Next · [Wisdom] + [Moderate]**
- A barn, a bridge at the disputed crossing, a flood wall, a wilderness longhouse. Tasks are sized so no one can finish alone. The host provides the feast. People who don't build still get real roles: cooking, water, tools, minding children. Residents remember who helped whom. Absence costs a little warmth, never currency. It ends in a feast and a dance.
- **Why:** communal-labour traditions everywhere share this form: Finnish *talkoot* (the person who benefits must "offer food and drink"), Andean *minka* (from Quechua "asking for help by promising something"), Indonesian *gotong royong* ("joint bearing of burdens"), Amish barn raisings, which end with a feast. Collective effervescence — shared emotional synchrony — correlates strongly with self-transcendent emotion and group identity ([meta-analysis, 2022](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2022.974683/full)).
- **Bridge:** park clean-ups, volunteer builds, helping a friend move.

### 2. The commons oven
*The village bread oven is a third place, and a commons rather than a monopoly.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- Design it against Ray Oldenburg's traits of a "third place": neutral ground, no status displays, conversation as the main activity, regulars, a playful mood, a home away from home. Built-in waiting helps: dough must rise, so people linger. A host resident greets regulars by name. Bake day is where news and quests circulate.
- **Why "in reverse":** the medieval French *four banal* was a lord's oven that peasants were required to use for a fee. Toronto's Dufferin Grove Park, where neighbours tend wood-fired ovens, is called "a community centre without a roof." Threewater's Bellwold is already described as "a market village around a public oven" whose fuel competes with winter reserves — a ready-made commons dilemma.

### 3. The common meal
*Every settlement holds a regular shared supper, and residents bring the stories.* **Next · [Moderate] for meals**
- Christopher Alexander's pattern 147, *Communal Eating*: "Give every institution and social group a place where people can eat together. Make the common meal a regular event." Robin Dunbar found that evening meals, laughter and reminiscing are what make shared meals bond people. Residents' durable memory makes them a *reminiscing engine*: they retell world history, toast players' inventions, remember last year's flood.
- **Why it matters so much:** in the World Happiness Report 2025, whether someone shared a meal in the past week predicted their life evaluation better than whether they were unemployed ([WHR 2025](https://www.worldhappiness.report/ed/2025/sharing-meals-with-others-how-sharing-meals-supports-happiness-and-social-connections/)).
- **Bridge:** a "Shared Supper Week" invitation: cook the in-world recipe for someone real. See [hands, body and nature](06-ideas-hands-body-and-nature.md).

### 4. Harvest Home
*The fair closes with a supper where residents thank every helper by name and tell their specific kindnesses back.* **Next · [Wisdom] + [Moderate]**
- Historically the harvest supper was held "to which all who had helped in the harvest were invited." Here it turns witnessed kindness — Keltner's *moral beauty* — into collective awe.

### 5. Ice-breaker songs
*In their first session, every newcomer joins a synchronous group activity.* **Now/Next · [Moderate]**
- A harvest work song, a rowing chant, a round by the fire. Success depends on keeping time together; individual voices are never scored.
- **Why:** in a seven-month study, adult singing classes bonded much faster than craft or creative-writing classes — "the first evidence for an 'ice-breaker effect' of singing" ([Pearce, Launay & Dunbar 2015](https://api.semanticscholar.org/graph/v1/paper/DOI:10.1098/rsos.150221?fields=title,abstract,authors,year,venue)). At a milestone, the world can mention local choirs.

### 6. The workshop
*A shared workshop where players craft side by side with a master resident, and conversation comes out of the work.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- The mechanism that matters is working side by side on something purposeful; the space isn't gendered. **Bridge:** Men's Sheds (roughly 3,000 worldwide), community workshops, makerspaces.

### 7. Mending Day
*A monthly event where fixers repair broken tools and inventions while teaching.* **Next · [Weak] evidence, strong precedent**
- An open repair ledger records what breaks, so inventions improve. Easy-to-repair inventions earn a "mender's mark" in the marketplace. **Bridge:** 3,800+ real Repair Cafés, whose foundation says the café "teaches people to see their possessions in a new light."

### 8. Beating the Bounds
*A free weekly walk or run around the village boundary, at a fixed time, timed but not raced.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- A tail walker ensures no one finishes last; rotating volunteer roles (marshal, timekeeper, token-giver) are first-class ways to take part. The name comes from the old English parish custom of walking the parish boundaries.
- **Why:** parkrun is the model — free, weekly, "not a race," walkers welcome, volunteers celebrated. In 2024–25 it had 2.3 million participants and 309,220 volunteers; volunteers reported bigger happiness gains than runners (86% vs 79%), even though, in an earlier survey, only 4.7% had volunteered for their mental health ([Sheffield Hallam](https://www.shu.ac.uk/news/all-articles/latest-news/parkrun-volunteering-research)). **Bridge:** the parkrun event finder.

### 9. One-time feasts
*A monthly feast with a unique name, a keepsake page, and the understanding that it will never happen the same way again.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- Epicurus's Garden held a monthly gathering, the *Eikas*. The Japanese tea ceremony's *ichi-go ichi-e*: "the guests must understand that the gathering cannot occur again." Seneca, quoting Epicurus: "reflect carefully beforehand with whom you are to eat and drink, rather than what you are to eat and drink."

### 10. Helper roles as the front door
*Every public event offers three to six small roles for newcomers.* **Now/Next · [Moderate]**
- Greeter, lantern-lighter, scorekeeper, water-bearer. Contribution comes before performance. Recognition is thanks from residents and a line in the chronicle, not grindable points. Lave and Wenger's *legitimate peripheral participation*: newcomers start with "simple and low-risk tasks that are nonetheless productive and necessary."

### 11. The Ceremony Hall
*A place to hold real weddings, graduations, birthdays and memorials inside the world.* **Later · [Moderate] precedent**
- Browser spectator links let non-playing family attend; invite-only "sanctuary mode" prevents disruption. **Why:** during COVID, people held weddings and graduations in Animal Crossing, and UC Berkeley students rebuilt their campus in Minecraft ("Blockeley") to hold commencement. A 2006 World of Warcraft memorial for a player who had died was ambushed by a rival guild — sacred gatherings need protection.

### 12. The Bench and the fireside questions
*A place where two players sit, the music and interface fade, and they talk.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Optionally, a deck of gradually deepening questions for pairs, modelled on Arthur Aron's 45-minute "36 questions" protocol, which created closeness between strangers ([Aron et al. 1997](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0146167297234003)). Opt-in, adults-only for the deeper tiers, with easy exits. **Why:** in *WALL-E*, John and Mary notice each other — and the ship's pool — only when their screens switch off.

## B. Kindness without obligation

### 13. Strands
*Players leave bridges, caches, tools and signposts that strangers find later.* **Next · [Moderate] precedent**
- Thanks flows one way; nothing is owed back; everything slowly decays. **Why:** *Death Stranding*'s "Social Strand System" makes indirect help across time zones feel like community without the risks of chat. Hideo Kojima wanted players to feel "the true importance of forging connections with others."

### 14. Letters at the Well
*Anonymous requests for encouragement, answered with one-shot letters that can be thanked but not replied to.* **Next · [Moderate] precedent**
- **Why:** *Kind Words* (2019) carried 4 million+ letters, with about 3% of the first 500,000+ needing moderation; its designers say trolls "get bored quickly." Crisis keywords route to real helplines; human moderators back it up.

### 15. The six charities
*Small kindnesses are world affordances, remembered by residents and written into the legend as prose, not points.* **Now/Next · [Wisdom]**
- From a hadith: "Your smiling in the face of your brother is charity… your giving directions to a man lost in the land is charity for you. Your seeing for a man with bad sight is a charity… your removal of a rock, a thorn or a bone from the road is charity… Your pouring what remains from your bucket into the bucket of your brother is charity for you." As affordances: greet, give directions, guide someone, clear the road, share water, encourage good.

### 16. Gifts without ledgers
*Gifting never creates debt.* **Now · [Strong] for the harm of social obligation**
- No reciprocity counters, no gifts that expire, no "help me or I lose," no invite-gated progress. Burning Man's principle: "The value of a gift is unconditional. Gifting does not contemplate a return or an exchange." FarmVille's gift obligations are the counter-example.

### 17. The Blue Shirt path
*Prosocial play is as deep, levelled and visible as combat is in other games.* **Next · [Wisdom] + [Moderate]**
- Helping, mending, hosting, teaching and healing have mastery curves, stories and renown. In *Free Guy*, a background character levels up by doing good deeds and players imitate him. In one preprint, cooperative players reported higher wellbeing than competitive ones ([Hakman et al. 2026](https://vuorre.com/bibliography/files/HTDFPRRY/hakman-etal_2026_not-all-play-is-equal-in-game-player-be.pdf)); the Proteus effect suggests the roles we play shape us a little.

### 18. Tell the inventor what it did
*When others use an invention, the inventor hears the concrete good it did.* **Now · [Strong] for informational feedback**
- "Your cord jig helped three families finish nets before the storm." Informational, not a leaderboard: expected tangible rewards undermine intrinsic motivation, while informative positive feedback supports it ([Deci, Koestner & Ryan 1999](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.125.6.627)). In a small experiment, a sense of positive impact on others ("beneficence") boosted wellbeing ([Martela & Ryan 2016](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-016-9552-z)).

### 19. Trust ladders and wayfinders
*Communication between strangers unlocks step by step; veterans guide newcomers.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Gestures first, then set phrases, then chat by mutual consent. Minors get restricted channels by default. **Why:** *Journey* removed names and chat so "players' biases and preconceptions" couldn't come between them; *Sky* unlocks chat as a friendship grows, and 62% of surveyed players had been helped by veterans, who can hold newcomers' hands.

## C. Civic rehearsal: Threewater as a school of self-government

### 20. The Ostrom toolkit
*Every commons gets the eight tools that real commons need.* **Next · [Moderate], from extensive case comparisons**
- Elinor Ostrom won a Nobel Prize for showing how communities govern shared resources without collapse. Her design principles map almost one-to-one onto Threewater's river crossing, mill-race, forest, forge and oven:

| Ostrom principle | In the world |
|---|---|
| Clear boundaries | Boundary stones and a membership roll |
| Rules that fit local conditions | A local rule editor for each commons |
| Collective choice | A village moot where users make the rules |
| Accountable monitoring | Elected monitors who answer to users |
| Graduated sanctions | Reminder, then fine, then temporary exclusion |
| Cheap conflict resolution | The elder's bench |
| Recognized right to organize | Charters recognized by the world's higher authority |
| Nested enterprises | Village councils within a valley council |

- The Narrator can name each principle as it comes up and point to real analogues: allotment committees, co-ops, tenants' associations. **Bridge:** these are transferable civic skills.

### 21. Sortition assemblies
*When a dispute escalates, a random draw of players and residents deliberates.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Briefing packets from each side, testimony from expert residents, facilitated small groups, recommendations to a ballot, and visible before/after opinion shifts. **Why:** Ireland's 99 randomly chosen citizens voted 79–12 on the constitutional question that led to the 2018 referendum; deliberative polls measurably shift opinion, in one case toward the centre for at least a year.

### 22. The Village Well
*A public board of short statements that others mark agree, disagree or pass — with no reply button.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Opinion clusters are shown, and statements that win agreement *across* clusters rise. Residents may seed statements but are labelled and excluded from human tallies. **Why:** Taiwan's vTaiwan process uses the open-source Pol.is tool; Audrey Tang: "If people can propose their ideas and comments but they cannot reply to each other, then it drastically reduces the motivation for trolls to troll." About 80% of its early cases led to government action.

### 23. The harvest budget
*The fair's surplus is allocated by participatory budgeting.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- Neighbourhood assemblies feed a district forum and a council, with visible before/after results. Under Porto Alegre's participatory budget, sewer and water connections rose from 75% to 98%; it lapsed when political support faded, so build in renewal rituals.

### 24. Forum theatre at the crossroads
*The Narrator stages short conflict scenes; players stop them and try other choices.* **Next · [Wisdom] + [Weak]**
- A bully at the mill; a boss who won't listen in the city world. A facilitator resident (Boal's "Joker") runs the debrief. A *legislative theatre* mode turns scenes that keep recurring into council proposals — as Augusto Boal did as a Rio city councillor, producing about 13 laws. Boal's line, as commonly quoted from the 1979 translation: "Perhaps the theater is not revolutionary in itself; but have no doubts, it is a rehearsal of revolution!"† For personal rehearsal, see the [Rehearsal Hall](07-ideas-growth-and-real-goals.md#4-the-rehearsal-hall).

### 25. A voice for the unborn
*When a world faces a lasting choice, an elder speaks for future generations.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- Felling the old wood, damming the river. The Haudenosaunee Great Law asks leaders to keep in view "not only the present but also the coming generations, even those whose faces are yet beneath the surface of the ground." Decisions persist across resident generations, and new players inherit them. (The popular "seventh generation" quotation is not verbatim in the Great Law; quote Law 28 or Chief Oren Lyons.)

### 26. The River Moot and the player council
*Players petition, gather signatures, vote laws; an elected council meets the developers.* **Later · [Moderate] precedent**
- *A Tale in the Desert* passes laws by petition and vote; EVE Online's elected Council of Stellar Management meets its developer; EVE's in-house economist published quarterly reports ("Economic information is the lifeblood of the game"). Publish a quarterly Threewater economic report. **Bridge:** representation plus published data is real civic practice.

### 27. Principles, not rules
*When a player-founded institution works, its founders can publish its principles so other worlds can adopt it.* **Later · [Moderate] precedent**
- Death Café, Repair Café, parkrun and Burning Man's regional events all spread as "social franchises" built on principles rather than rigid rules. (Buy Nothing's heavy trademark enforcement, which split its groups, is the failure mode.)

### 28. Civic level design
*Towns are laid out so that institutions can emerge.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- Jane Jacobs: mixed uses, short blocks, buildings of different ages, "eyes upon the street." Alexander's patterns: *Old People Everywhere* ("Old people need old people, but they also need the young"), *Common Land* ("Without common land no social system can survive"), *Street Café*, *Connected Play*, cheap storefronts around the town hall for new community groups.

### 29. Repair rituals for relationships
*Apology and repair leave a visible, honoured mark.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- Kintsugi repairs broken pottery with gold, treating "breakage and repair as part of the history of an object." Restorative circles, apology rites and graduated, forgiving sanctions model how communities heal. Reputation stays local, narrative and forgiving — never a global score (*Black Mirror*'s "Nosedive" is the warning).

## D. Across divides

### 30. Cooperative quests that meet Allport's conditions
*Cross-group quests with equal status, a real common goal, genuine interdependence and visible support from authorities.* **Next · [Strong]**
- Across 515 studies, contact reduced prejudice (r ≈ −.21), more strongly when those conditions were present (r ≈ −.29) ([Pettigrew & Tropp 2006](https://ideas.wharton.upenn.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/Pettigrew-Tropp.pdf)). **Crises must be real world events, never staged rivalries:** archival work by Gina Perry shows the famous Robbers Cave experiment's rivalry was engineered, and in its pilot "the boys worked out that the adults were engineering their conflict."

### 31. Friendship across lines, quietly celebrated
*Friendships formed across opt-in real-world differences — generation, country, politics — are lightly honoured.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- Cross-group friendship is the active ingredient in contact effects. Knowing that a friend has a friend in the other group also helps (extended contact, r ≈ .25), and imagined-contact warm-ups help anxious players (d ≈ .35). Seeds of Peace campers who made a friend across the line grew more positive toward the whole other group — though the effect faded after camp, so build in reunions and in-world pen-pal post.

### 32. Protection against bad contact
*Structured, moderated, easy-to-leave matchmaking across divides.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- Negative contact increases prejudice more strongly than positive contact reduces it. Borrow Braver Angels' ground rule — "Speak for yourself; don't interpret what's going on in the other person's mind" — and its "fishbowl" listening. Never leave someone with one bad match as their only experience.

### 33. Lantern Talks and the Living Library
*Structured conversations between people who would not otherwise meet.* **Later · [Weak] evidence, strong precedent**
- A Human Library mode where volunteer players are "books" for 30-minute conversations (tagline: "Unjudge someone"), and StoryCorps-style 40-minute conversations archived with consent in the village library — including StoryCorps' *One Small Step*, which pairs political opposites to talk about their lives, not politics. A family version pairs a player with a real grandparent.

## E. Real-world gatherings

### 34. Moot and Market Day
*Each world holds a monthly festival at a fixed real-world time, with host kits for real venues.* **Next · [Moderate] precedent**
- Libraries, cafés and game stores get a kit to host watch-and-play nights. Attending remotely is worth exactly the same as attending in person; in-person attendees get cosmetic keepsakes only.
- **Why:** Wizards of the Coast's Friday Night Magic ran in about 6,000 stores and routed rewards through local stores even during COVID; Pokémon GO has held a monthly Community Day since 2018. The lesson from Niantic's 2023 remote-raid price rise, which provoked a boycott and was partly reversed: reward in-person play distinctively, but never charge for the path that makes the game accessible to disabled, rural and housebound players.

### 35. The Bridge Board
*An opt-in noticeboard that lists real local groups related to what a player loves in the world.* **Next · [Idea]**
- Parkruns, Repair Cafés, Men's Sheds, choirs, community gardens, run clubs, Human Library and Death Café events, community ovens, archery clubs, makerspaces, Buy Nothing groups — framed as "your village has cousins in your town." Coarse location only, opt-in; the game logs only an optional "I went!", never location trails. Partners should be vetted for safeguarding before being promoted.

### 36. Game night mode
*Same-room play, and worlds that export to the tabletop.* **Later · [Moderate] market signal**
- A mode designed for a group around one screen, phones as controllers, the AI as a co-game-master that relieves the "forever GM" burden while humans stay central. A world's saga can be exported as a printable tabletop one-shot. **Why:** the tabletop boom (*Daggerheart*'s year's stock sold out in about two weeks in 2025) and co-op hits (*Peak*, *Split Fiction*) show that people want to play together.

### 37. Guest passes and family nights
*It is easy to bring someone you love into your world.* **Next · [Moderate]**
- A free guest pass into a friend's world (like *Split Fiction*'s Friend's Pass), couch co-op, and "hearth nights" for families. Family co-play is where bonding (close-tie) social capital grows.

### 38. From hearth circle to real table
*For verified adults, an optional path from a long-running in-world group to a real meetup.* **Later · [Moderate]**
- After many hours together, a hearth circle whose members live near each other can opt in to a real meetup, with safety features (public venues, no addresses shared by the game). Online ties turned into real support through offline meetings ([Trepte et al. 2012](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0747563211002706)). Minors: no location data and no meetups.

### 39. The Long Night
*An annual charity festival where donations shape world events.* **Later · [Moderate] precedent**
- Donors might name a new bridge or choose a festival's theme; a public tally; the player council chooses beneficiaries. Games Done Quick has raised about $60 million by letting donors shape the show and reading donations aloud; two of *Sky*'s charity seasons raised about $720,000 and $790,000, and a third planted more than 40,000 trees.

### 40. Mirror stewardship
*In-world restoration seasons paired with real clean-ups.* **Later · [Moderate] precedent**
- Clean the river in the world while players who want to join a real clean-up that weekend; a small cosmetic souvenir requires attendance confirmed by the organiser. Geocaching's "Cache In Trash Out" events have drawn 875,000+ attendees since 2002. **Caution:** never pay for civic acts. When a Swiss town was offered compensation to host a nuclear-waste site, support *fell* from 50.8% to 24.6%; paying for blood donation cut women's donations. A token of remembrance is fine; a wage crowds out the gift.

### 41. Time and gift economies
*One hour of help earns one credit, whatever the skill.* **Later · [Weak] evidence**
- Edgar Cahn's timebanking principle, where "everyone is an asset." Credits can't be bought or traded for gold. Residents do the coordination chores that make real timebanks fail.

## F. Culture-making

### 42. The invention commons, with lineage
*Every invention shows its family tree, and remixes credit their ancestors automatically.* **Next · [Moderate] precedent**
- Scratch credits original creators automatically on every remix and has over a billion projects. A world's inventions can be exported as zines — shareable, real-world how-to guides where they're safe.

### 43. The Museum of Civilization
*A player-built, community-curated museum of a world's inventions and history.* **Later · [Wisdom]**
- In *Station Eleven*, survivors keep a "Museum of Civilization" at an airport. In Cory Doctorow's *Makers*, a crowd-curated dark ride becomes a living story of its era. Credits and provenance for every exhibit.

### 44. Survival is insufficient
*Art, music, theatre and story are core progression, even in the wilderness.* **Next · [Wisdom]**
- *Station Eleven*'s Traveling Symphony performs Shakespeare for scattered settlements under the motto "Survival is insufficient" (borrowed from *Star Trek: Voyager*). Travelling troupes, songs that spread between villages, and plays at the fair make culture a reason to gather.
