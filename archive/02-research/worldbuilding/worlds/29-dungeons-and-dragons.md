# 29. Dungeons & Dragons: worlds made inhabitable through consequential differences

Research date: 2026-09-27. This is an independent worldbuilding study, not a proposed ruleset or implementation specification. [Library](../README.md) · [Research method](../research-method.md) · [D&D game-research hub](../../game-inspiration/games/dungeons-and-dragons.md).

**Spoilers and scope:** discusses setting premises, institutions, named characters, religious uncertainty, and the destruction of Cyre. It does not reveal the solution to a particular mystery adventure or claim that one campaign's ending is universal canon. The deepest lived-world evidence comes from Eberron creator commentary. Current Forgotten Realms publications provide a contemporary comparison; Greyhawk, Planescape, and Ravenloft are more selective lenses. Their different evidence depth is explicit below.

## 1. D&D is not one world, and an edition is not a chronology

Dungeons & Dragons supplies rules with which groups can inhabit many worlds. **Forgotten Realms, Greyhawk, Eberron, Planescape, and Ravenloft are not alternative names for the same setting.** A rule revision can change how a spell is represented without advancing every setting's history. A novel, computer game, published adventure, home campaign, and streamed campaign can also establish different continuities or local outcomes.

The current tabletop reference is the revised fifth-edition lineage, now labeled **5.5e** by Wizards, with SRD 5.2.1 as the latest listed English reference on its current SRD page. That establishes a rules version, not the most popular ongoing table practice or a single mandatory world. The [current-edition dossier](../../game-inspiration/dossiers/dungeons-and-dragons-2024.md) owns that verification and the mechanical details. [W1]

The current-world anchor here is the **2025 Forgotten Realms pair, Heroes of Faerûn and Adventures in Faerûn**. Their official contents were inspected, along with a substantive 2026 review. The complete paid chapters were not available. Older public rules provide a specifically labeled account of the Weave; they are not silently treated as an audit of every revised lore passage. Eberron material is similarly separated into published-book statements quoted by its creator and his explicitly personal extensions, often called “kanon.” [W2–W4, R1]

| Selected setting | Central comparison | Evidence boundary |

## 2. The audience's first foothold

**Interpretation:** a large fictional world becomes accessible when someone wants something understandable before they understand its cosmology. A traveler needs shelter. An apprentice wants a recognized qualification. A newcomer wants an invitation. A refugee wants a future that does not erase a former home. Someone facing a powerful host wants to leave safely. These are entrances into a world, not substitutes for it.

The selected settings offer different routes to that foothold. The Realms' current player book places character options beside a regional guide, religion, equipment, magic, and factions. The contents make belonging an alternative entrance to the atlas: a person can begin with an affiliation or occupation rather than memorizing a continent. Eberron's creator essays repeatedly connect unusual magic to familiar social activities. Planescape's city-centered organization gives the cosmic traveler a place from which to orient themselves. Ravenloft's separation of horror genres makes the desired emotional experience part of setting selection. [W2, W6–W7; E1–E10]

These are formal affordances, not measured audience outcomes. A beginner can still find a long list of names intimidating. A known faction can invite curiosity or feel like homework. A spectacular map can promise exploration while concealing that most interesting choices happen between a handful of people.

**Pleasures matter.** Wonder is not only the scale of a monster or catastrophe. Skillful work, delicious food, a memorable performance, a kindly mentor, and recognition from peers can make a place worth preserving. A world that explains only oppression and combat gives its inhabitants little reason to enjoy being alive. Conversely, comfort is more interesting when its maintenance and unequal availability remain visible.

## 3. Authorship, collaboration, and the production of a setting

Ed Greenwood identifies himself as the original creator of the Forgotten Realms. The setting's subsequent life is not simply one author's uninterrupted account: the current books are published by Wizards and organize a large body of material for contemporary players. This dossier does not infer Greenwood's biography, private intentions, or control over later editorial decisions from that creator credit. A complete production history of the Realms, including its early publication and later collaborative revisions, remains beyond the primary-source material inspected here. [P1, W2–W3]

Eberron offers unusually visible ongoing authorship through Keith Baker's essays. His discussions frequently identify what a published source says, where published sources disagree, and how he would handle the issue in his own campaign. This makes the difference between **canon, interpretation, and usable invention** observable rather than theoretical. A secondary bibliographic reference credits Baker, Bill Slavicsek, and James Wyatt for the 2004 setting; the original production archive and complete collaboration history were not recovered in this pass. [P2; E7–E10]

Visual authorship also matters. Tony DiTerlizzi describes using Bristol board, sepia dip-pen ink, watercolors, diluted inks, and colored-pencil detailing for his 1993–1996 Planescape work. That is direct production testimony, not a claim that every later Planescape illustration uses the same technique. His separate Monstrous Manual retrospective preserves an editorial demand for reference images that remained serious rather than comic. These examples reveal actual negotiated presentation choices rather than an invented claim that “a distinctive style caused the setting's success.” [P3–P4]

**Interpretation:** a setting has at least four authorship layers: the original conception, the published edition, the presenter or adapter, and the table that realizes particular events. They can cooperate or conflict. A new visual treatment can make an old location feel different without changing its map. A campaign can preserve a nation's institutions while changing its ruler. A sourcebook can provide a strong default without requiring every table to treat an unresolved mystery identically.

## 4. Forgotten Realms: a current entrance into an accumulated world

The 2025 **Adventures in Faerûn** organizes regional play around the Dalelands, Icewind Dale, Calimshan, the Moonshae Isles, and Baldur's Gate. Its contents contrast desert survival and Calimport's districts, island navigation, northern settlements, and urban divisions. The **Heroes** volume explicitly includes calendars and currency, regional languages, religious institutions, equipment and vehicles, the Weave and Circle Magic, and factions including the Harpers, Emerald Enclave, Lords' Alliance, Red Wizards, and Zhentarim. These are verified coverage categories, not a claim to have read every entry. [W2–W3]

**Interpretation:** the important worldbuilding opportunity is not the number of regions. It is the possibility that the same intention means something different in each place. A journey can be about weather, water, navigation, or access. An affiliation can help in one encounter and complicate another. A tool, local expression, coin, or travel custom can provide a small piece of orientation before a history lecture is necessary.

The public **2014** spellcasting explanation gives a concrete metaphysical account: Forgotten Realms casters identify the Weave with Mystra, and damaged access to that fabric can disrupt magic. The passage distinguishes raw magical potential from the means of shaping it. It also relates schools and casting practices to ways of accessing magic. This is an older explicit source, not proof that every D&D world must use Mystra or that current setting books preserve every sentence unchanged. [W4]

**Interpretation:** making magical access a feature of the world rather than merely a personal statistic allows landscape, religion, expertise, and history to touch the same problem. A damaged magical environment can become an infrastructure problem, a theological question, or an expedition. The existence of that possibility does not establish how often ordinary inhabitants encounter it, who understands it, or what a region's economy does in response. Those require local evidence.

The 2026 Old Game Hermit review praises the paired books' regional material, introductory usefulness, and portions of their lore, while criticizing recycled content and the cost/value implications of dividing the material into two books. The writer is already skeptical of the revision. That is one informed reception account, not a consensus. Its speculative sixth-edition rumors and loose age description are not adopted here. [R1]

The Realms comparison therefore has a meaningful limit: this pass establishes current entry structure and selected cosmological connections, but not a comprehensive history of Netheril, the Spellplague, every deity, or every regional society. Familiarity with famous names is not a substitute for examining the relevant sourcebooks.

## 5. Eberron: magic as infrastructure and labor

Baker's account of the dragonmarked houses frames their power around specialized magical capabilities, infrastructure, and institutions rather than the isolated strength of individual spellcasters. His explanation connects dynastic marks to services and large magical devices, while noting the political significance of the houses' relationship with the former unified kingdom. His edition-specific examples should not be converted into current rules without checking their version. [E1]

**Interpretation:** this makes a useful distinction between possessing a power and controlling a service. A traveler might need access to an institution even when magic is common. Expertise, equipment, contracts, and distribution can remain scarce after a supernatural effect is understood. A society can be magically sophisticated without every household independently reproducing all its infrastructure.

Baker's **magical education** essay sharply distinguishes a magewright's narrow professional training from an adventuring wizard's flexible spellcasting. Guilds, trade schools, and institutions can train specialized work; academic study and battlefield improvisation are not merely successive steps on one universal ladder. A healer or locksmith can be highly competent without receiving every capability of a high-level adventurer. The article's particular ritual costs belong to its historical rules context. [E2]

That distinction helps a world feel inhabited because it makes room for careers. An apprentice can take pride in consistency, a guild examiner in standards, a customer in finding a reliable specialist. The extraordinary need not erase ordinary competence. It can supply a different material for it.

The **rural Eberron** essay further distinguishes widespread low-level applications from rare high-end power. Baker proposes different agricultural and household expressions in different cultures: everyday repair and sanitation, regional magical techniques, and specific religious uses of undead labor. These examples are his setting elaborations, not an agricultural census. Their value is that “magic exists” does not produce one uniform village everywhere. [E3]

**Questions the evidence opens rather than answers completely:** who pays for maintenance, what happens when a specialist leaves, how rural and urban access differ, whether magical inputs are imported, and how a household combines paid services with its own labor. This dossier does not invent exact wages, crop yields, electrification-like coverage percentages, or repair schedules to fill those gaps.

## 6. Food, leisure, class, and the small signs of belonging

Baker's food-and-drink essay gives a particularly accessible example: Zilwater establishments use magical flavoring as part of the service experience. His further inventions include branded refreshments and distinctions between common and prestigious drinks. He explicitly does not claim a fully worked-out culinary ecology. The interest here is the combination of expertise, customization, pleasure, and commerce—not an assertion that every proposed menu item is official canon. [E4]

**Interpretation:** taste can carry class and place without an exposition scene. A character may know how to order, miss a familiar preparation, treat a friend, or find a prestigious drink disappointing. None of those requires trauma. A food custom becomes substantial when it changes a relationship or occasion, rather than existing only as an encyclopedia entry.

The **Tain Gala**, described through Baker's quotation of Sharn: City of Towers and his subsequent additions, is a more concentrated status institution. The established guest circle marks elite belonging; Lady Celyria ir'Tain can admit unusual guests and performers. The gathering combines pleasure and display with introductions and patronage. Baker's optional performers and situations are additional author proposals, not an exhaustive canonical attendee list. [E5]

The analytical distinction is between wealth and recognition. A person can possess money yet lack the right introduction; an entertainer can gain temporary access without becoming a hereditary insider. A meal, performance, or dance may therefore have both an immediate pleasure and a political consequence. Treating the gala only as a place to roll persuasion would flatten its social structure.

The cost of such concentration is selective visibility. A sourcebook may richly describe a gala while giving little space to the cooks, cleaners, garment workers, or servants supporting it. Their existence is a reasonable material question, but their particular names, schedules, and labor arrangements cannot be invented and presented as retrieved canon. This is a genuine gap in what this source sample foregrounds.

## 7. History, law, borders, and unfinished institutions

Baker's **Treaties and Laws** essay distinguishes the Treaty of Thronehold from a constitution or uniform legal code. In his account, states retain different laws within a fragile postwar settlement; the treaty also addresses warforged freedom, certain magical production, borders, and house operations. He explicitly frames its incompleteness as a consequence of an urgent settlement rather than a polished international system, and warns that his reading may conflict with other books. [E6]

**Interpretation:** historical depth becomes active when inherited structures no longer fit the present. A former common legal framework can survive differently in successor states. A person can possess treaty recognition without receiving every protection they expected at home. A border is consequently more than a line: it changes the meaning of a document, accusation, occupation, or claim.

This is also a case where incoherence and productive incompleteness must be distinguished. An institution that characters know is contested can generate choices. An unexplained contradiction that prevents anyone from knowing what a rule means is simply a continuity problem. It is not necessary to make every gap an intentional mystery.

The **Prince Oargev** essay makes that problem unusually explicit: Baker identifies contradictory published ages, parentage, and statistics, then selects a version for his discussion. The recurring premise is a surviving Cyran royal associated with refugees and New Cyre; his personal future is entangled with communal reconstruction. The essay's suggested suitors are possible campaign material, not a declaration of who he canonically marries. [E7]

**Interpretation:** memory is more than historical information. People may disagree over what deserves restoration, what must change, and which old status distinctions remain legitimate. A treasured song, a surviving friendship, a practical skill, and a political title can carry the past differently. Those are distinct forms of continuity even when a territory is lost.

## 8. Religion, cosmology, and the difference between evidence and conviction

In Baker's treatment of Eberron faith, access to divine magic is not proof of moral virtue, and religious institutions do not exhaust the meaning of belief. He distinguishes traditions of faith and community from a simple system in which gods constantly appear to confirm every doctrine. Dolurrh's relationship to death and fading memory can be known in one sense while its ultimate meaning remains interpreted differently. Non-spellcasting religious participants still have a role. [E8]

**Interpretation:** this lets a religion organize care, education, solidarity, ritual, conflict, and personal discipline without reducing it to a spell list. The important distinction is not merely “gods are real” versus “gods are imaginary.” It is what inhabitants can establish, how they interpret it, and which authorities they trust.

A setting can consequently contain sincere disagreement without making everyone foolish. A miracle can answer one question while leaving another open. A priest's kindness need not depend on supernatural power; a powerful religious official need not be trustworthy. Contrasting interpretations also prevent every follower of one faith from becoming the same personality.

Across the selected worlds, cosmology should remain local to its source. The Realms' Weave account, Eberron's religious ambiguities, Planescape's setting structure, and Ravenloft's domain premises should not be welded into one supposedly universal theological textbook. Crossovers can be authored, but an explicit crossover is different from accidental source confusion.

## 9. Language and communication: more than species labels

Baker's Elvish essay proposes an explicitly personal Eberron interpretation connecting the language to Thelanis and magical rather than ordinary historical transmission. It also recognizes differences from published assumptions. This is a provocative worldbuilding option, not a documented complete constructed language. The article does not supply enough grammar, phonology, or historical texts to classify it alongside a fully developed conlang. [E9]

**Interpretation:** language can be handled at several levels: a mechanical permission to understand speech, an invented naming aesthetic, a full linguistic system, or a social practice shaped by occupation and belonging. These levels need not coincide. A fluent speaker may still misunderstand a legal formula, ritual reference, or elite joke. Magical translation can convey words without generating trust or shared assumptions.

The current Realms book explicitly provides regional-language coverage; Planescape's contents separately identify language, travel, and city institutions. That establishes useful reading routes, not a complete audit of their dialects. [W2, W6]

For this source sample, occupational and institutional registers are more richly evidenced than actual invented syntax: the categories used by guilds, treaty participants, religious officials, and invited guests carry relationships. The absence of a complete grammar is not a failure if language's social effects are clear; claiming linguistic depth from a list of fantasy syllables would be.

## 10. Named people: institutions interpreted differently

These are short, source-grounded character studies, not psychological diagnoses or claims that an origin determines a personality.

**High Cardinal Krozen and Jaela Daran — two readings of obligation.** In Baker's explicitly personal account, Krozen is a capable, charismatic, ruthlessly pragmatic protector of a narrowly defined community, while Jaela's concern reaches more universally. Shared religious language does not guarantee agreement about who must be protected or what means are justified. The comparison turns a religious institution into an argument rather than one moral personality. [E10]

**Zerasha — dangerous knowledge and community significance.** Baker identifies the medusa priestess as a trusted oracle in Graywall, then develops his own possibilities for mentorship and ambitious knowledge-seeking. Her form and affiliation do not exhaust her relationship to the people who consult her. A source can distinguish someone feared by outsiders from someone whose community values her competence. These elaborations are not universally binding canon. [E10]

**Lady Celyria ir'Tain — hospitality as power.** Her capacity to include unusual guests makes entertaining and patronage overlap. The interesting power is not an attack bonus but the ability to make others recognizable to a consequential audience. Enjoyment, curiosity, and social hierarchy can coexist in the same invitation. [E5]

**Prince Oargev — charm, inheritance, and an unfinished future.** Baker's selected version combines artistic and social promise with leadership among displaced people. Courtship can become a question about the community's direction, but affection need not be merely a disguise for political calculation. The source's competing possible partners deliberately leave that future open. His disagreements with published genealogy are disclosed rather than harmonized silently. [E7]

**Strahd von Zarovich — the host as threat.** The official Curse of Strahd premise presents the vampire lord, Castle Ravenloft, and Barovia through a threatening invitation. At this level of evidence, the useful contrast is social presentation: hospitality can announce domination instead of refuge. His full biography and every adventure portrayal were not freshly read here. [W8]

Together these examples complicate the idea that an interesting character is simply a backstory plus a disposition. Position supplies opportunities; interpretation supplies choices; relationships make those choices matter. The same institution can form rivals, allies, reformers, opportunists, and conscientious professionals.

## 11. Three other settings as contrasting lenses

### Greyhawk: a usable foothold rather than compulsory total knowledge

The 2024 DMG's public contents place Greyhawk within campaign construction and include the Free City as a detailed starting area. That choice makes it a contemporary reference, not merely a historical footnote. The complete paid chapter was not accessed. [W5]

**Interpretation:** a bounded example can teach what a campaign setting is for: orient people, supply plausible pressures, and leave room for choices. It need not first explain every king or century. That is a different promise from an encyclopedia, even when an encyclopedia exists elsewhere.

### Planescape: a vast cosmos with local addresses

The 2023 collection organizes Sigil, its wards and factions, portals, the Lady of Pain, the Outlands, and gate-towns into distinct sections. Named urban areas include the Hive, Lower, Market, Clerks', and Lady's Wards. This is a verified organizational map, not a reproduction of the complete planar cosmology or every faction's doctrine. [W6]

**Interpretation:** an incomprehensibly large universe becomes easier to encounter when a traveler has a street, contact, route, and immediate reason to go somewhere. Metaphysical difference can become a question of access or affiliation rather than a lecture. The risk is making every philosophy a costume or every plane a single mood. The contents alone cannot prove that the book avoids those reductions.

### Ravenloft: concentrated emotional conditions

Van Richten's Guide explicitly distinguishes creating a Darklord, creating a domain, multiple horror genres, life in the domains, travelers, and preparation for and aftermath of horror. Its range includes Barovia, Borca, Darkon, and other domains rather than only one vampire story. [W7]

**Interpretation:** this treats a place partly as an organizing emotional condition. The benefit is concentration: landscape, authority, and danger can reinforce an experience. The danger is flattening inhabitants into props for another person's nightmare. A humane reading still asks who cares for whom, what daily activities persist, and what inhabitants value beyond escape. Those questions must be answered from the chosen domain rather than presumed identical everywhere.

## 12. Systems working together: constructed sequences

The following are **original hypothetical illustrations**, not published scenes, historical setting events, or observed actual play. They use the distinctions developed above without declaring their invented people and details canonical.

### A. A journeyman's commission

A young urban craftsperson wants recognition for dependable magical repair work. A patron offers an introduction at a prestigious social event, but the repair is needed before the event begins. Materials are available; the workshop's trained assistant is not. The problem links professional training, time, labor, reputation, and elite access.

Paying another workshop might meet the deadline but reduce the craftsperson's credit. Asking a friend for help creates an obligation. Declining the introduction preserves professional standards but loses an opportunity. The consequential choice is not just whether the repair succeeds: it is what kind of professional the person is trying to become.

This example would remain socially meaningful without a combat encounter. It also exposes a worldbuilding requirement: the story must establish who recognizes the qualification, who can judge the work, and what the invitation actually grants. “Magic plus nobility” is not yet an explanation.

### B. A border dispute involving a free worker

A traveling worker reaches a jurisdiction where officials interpret their documents differently from the issuing authority. Their employer wants the journey completed; a local advocate wants a precedent clarified; the worker wants fair treatment without becoming a public symbol. Competing legal systems, institutional prestige, work, and personal autonomy now intersect.

A quick private payment might solve the journey while leaving the disputed practice intact. Public litigation might improve recognition but expose the person to delay and attention they never sought. No single alignment label tells the whole story, and the employer's helpfulness can coexist with self-interest.

This is not a legal analysis of the real world or a claim that the cited treaty source specifies this exact case. It is an illustration of how contested institutional arrangements can produce personal stakes without a cosmic villain.

### C. An invitation to rebuild a lost home

A displaced musician is invited to perform for donors supporting a new settlement. The proposed program celebrates a former court; the musician prefers songs associated with ordinary households. Another survivor wants familiar ceremony because it offers continuity. A younger relative wants a repertoire that welcomes their new neighbors.

The choice links memory, class, patronage, art, and community formation. Any outcome can preserve something and exclude something else. A performance can be joyful and politically consequential without being reduced to propaganda or trauma. The world becomes deeper through the disagreement about what deserves a future.

## 13. Selective depth, contradictions, and criticism

**Not everything needs an answer.** An unspecified distant village is not necessarily a defect. An unresolved cause can support several campaigns. The useful question is whether the missing information prevents people from understanding their immediate choices. A mystery can remain open while available evidence and local consequences stay reliable.

**Not every contradiction is productive.** A character's incompatible ages or parentage can be a source-management error rather than a hidden cosmic truth. Baker's disclosure of chosen versions is a valuable model of honest presentation. Revision should not be defended automatically as intentional depth.

**Species, culture, and ethics are different dimensions.** A fantasy people becomes flattened when biology determines every profession, opinion, and moral choice. Likewise, a setting can complicate one stereotype while leaving another untouched. The contrasting character cases support plurality, but do not establish that the whole franchise has solved representation. No broad audience survey or comprehensive cultural audit was conducted.

**Magic requires selective consequence.** A world need not be an econometric simulation, but ubiquitous healing, production, transport, or communication should not be ignored whenever inconvenient. The strongest material in this sample makes ordinary specialists and institutions part of the explanation. The weakest inference would be to calculate a spell's maximum output and announce that every fictional economy must already have reorganized around it.

**Adventurers are not the population.** Player abilities are optimized for exciting expeditions. Residents need care, maintenance, legal standing, relationships, and livelihoods. A setting can use different levels of mechanical detail for those lives without declaring them unimportant.

**Popularity is not proof of a particular technique.** This research does not show that one setting is currently most played, that one lore feature caused D&D's success, or that a published world is automatically more coherent than a home campaign. Long familiarity and social investment can coexist with frustration about revisions or commercial packaging.

## 14. Reading routes, evidence, and completion boundary

For **current Forgotten Realms orientation**, inspect the two current books' contents, then choose a region and institution rather than starting with every historical event. For **ordinary magic and social consequences**, the Baker essays on houses, education, rural life, food, law, and faith supply complementary primary perspectives. For **character formation**, compare the Krozen/Jaela, Oargev, and Tain Gala sources. For **medium and selective emphasis**, compare Planescape's organization and artist testimony with Ravenloft's explicit genre structure.

These routes are not claims that all linked paid books were read. Full chapters, complete novels, videos, and every comment thread remain distinct evidence surfaces. The dossier covers the requested worldbuilding dimensions through a representative corpus; it does not certify exhaustive franchise coverage, complete production history, or equally deep treatment of all five settings.
