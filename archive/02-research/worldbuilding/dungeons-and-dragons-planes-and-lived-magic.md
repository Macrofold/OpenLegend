# D&D: local laws, inherited magic, and the lives built around them

**Research date:** 2026-09-27. Companion to [D&D worldbuilding dossier 29](worlds/29-dungeons-and-dragons.md), not a replacement for its character studies, production history, or worked cases. [Worldbuilding method](research-method.md) · [Game-research packet](../game-inspiration/games/dungeons-and-dragons.md) · [Complete-SRD systems synthesis](../game-inspiration/mechanics/dungeons-and-dragons-srd-systems.md)

**Purpose:** deepen the independent worldbuilding analysis using the wiki routes Mike supplied, the uploaded SRD, and clearly separated publisher/creator sources. Project-facing mechanics and adaptation proposals belong in the linked game-research synthesis, not in this document.

**Spoilers:** setting history, the nature of mythals, Mythanthar's fate, Candlekeep's protections, and examples of supernatural environments. No complete novel or adventure is retold.

## 1. Reading the sources without inventing a single universal D&D world

The two supplied landing pages were blocked on direct retrieval. A later search-index route exposed substantial article bodies from the Forgotten Realms Wiki. This pass read those available bodies for **Mythal, Mythanthar, Weave, Candlekeep, Planar traits, Faerzress, Portal, and Feywild**. The indexed versions were reported as crawled roughly three months earlier; no exact revision ID or complete revision history was available. This is deeper selected wiki research, **not a claim to have read either entire wiki or the inaccessible Dungeons & Dragons Wiki Plane article**.

The distinction matters beyond access. The wiki combines different editions, publication types, and fictional dates. A page's 3rd-edition rule table, a novel incident, a game adaptation, and a later creator comment are not automatically one current rules contract. The uploaded SRD supplies a specific rules corpus; it is not an encyclopedia of every plane or a complete set of commercial core books.

The analysis below uses three layers: **reported source content**, **interpretation**, and **constructed illustration**. The latter two are not hidden lore. References within a wiki article are valuable routes to books, but listing a reference does not mean this research independently read that book.

## 2. The most productive unit of worldbuilding is a consequential difference

**Interpretation:** a world becomes distinctive when a fact changes someone's options. “Magic is common” is less revealing than a particular community depending on magical food, a library controlling access to a rare technique, or a road bypassed by a forgotten portal. A local difference can explain work, settlement, etiquette, conflict, pleasure, and historical memory at once.

The selected material repeatedly creates such differences. Some are environmental; some are inherited achievements; some are institutional choices. A place can be dangerous to a visitor and ordinary to its residents. A scarce capability can be a luxury in one city and a basic service in another. A very old enchantment can shape people who neither built it nor agree about its purpose.

This gives the setting a useful kind of density. A single phenomenon can be encountered as a landscape, a professional specialty, a political resource, a family memory, and a practical nuisance. Density does not require introducing five new proper nouns at every step. The audience can first encounter the problem through an understandable desire: get home, make a living, impress someone, discover a route, preserve a craft, gain power, or spend an enjoyable evening.

## 3. The Weave: access to power can have a history

**Reported content:** the Weave article distinguishes raw magic from a principal means of accessing and shaping it. It describes damage, dead-magic regions, wild-magic behavior, and alternative means of magical access. Its account spans Mystra's relationship to the Weave, the Spellplague, and later repair. These statements are setting and period specific, not universal SRD rules. [W1]

**Interpretation:** the important worldbuilding pattern is mediation. A person may possess talent while relying on a surrounding condition they did not create. Learning a technique and preserving the conditions that make the technique possible are different forms of expertise. An inherited magical environment can therefore be as important as a wizard's personal level.

That separation also creates historical vulnerability. A broken institution or damaged environment can strand skilled people without declaring their past learning meaningless. Recovery might involve adapting practice, preserving knowledge, finding another source, or rebuilding something collectively. Conversely, an individual may pursue an alternative tradition because independence matters more to them than convenience.

Do not overread this into one tidy explanation for every published spell. A sourcebook's cosmological metaphor is not necessarily a complete energy accounting system. Nor does calling a magical medium “fabric” imply literal threads that obey every physical textile analogy.

## 4. Mythals: an old achievement can govern a living place

**Reported content:** the Mythal article describes large-area enchantments created through exceptional collective magic. Such effects can alter what magic or entry is permitted within a region; creation can involve sacrifice, and age, damage, or neglect can affect their functioning. Its examples include protective and habitability-related enchantments. The article gathers several classifications and historical sources, so its categories should not be forced into a single perfectly consistent taxonomy. [W2]

**Interpretation:** this is more than a powerful spell covering a larger circle. It is an inherited arrangement that later inhabitants must live with. The builders' priorities may not match the current population's needs. A protected city can owe its safety to people it remembers imperfectly. Restoration can mean recovering an old design, changing it, or accepting that some former capability is no longer wanted.

A long-lived enchantment also gives history a material form. An inscription might identify a creator, a ceremony might honor a sacrifice, and a maintenance tradition might preserve practical knowledge without explaining the whole original theory. That allows partial understanding to be productive: residents can know how to live safely without being experts in metaphysics.

The cost is that a setting must decide which consequences remain dependable. A mysterious protective field is interesting when inhabitants can form reasonable expectations about it. An enchantment that changes its rules whenever the plot requires it is less an inherited institution than an authorial convenience.

### Mythanthar: the inventor is not interchangeable with the invention

**Reported content:** the Mythanthar article connects a lost capability, long research, an attempted replacement method, and the creation of Myth Drannor's mythal in 261 DR. His disappearance into that achievement contrasts with later loss or dispersal of the knowledge surrounding it. The page also draws on adaptation-specific material, which must not be silently transferred into every portrayal. [W3]

**Interpretation:** a memorable inventor can have a reason to invent that is not simply benevolence or ambition in the abstract. Loss, pride, curiosity, rivalry, affection, and the desire to make a contribution can coexist. The surviving work can become public while the creator's motives remain personal or disputed.

This makes an incomplete notebook more interesting than a generic technological unlock. A later scholar might reconstruct the procedure without agreeing with its purpose. A city might celebrate its founder while neglecting the workers who keep the result usable. A successor might want to exceed the original achievement rather than restore it faithfully. These are interpretive possibilities, not claims that the cited article depicts each of them.

## 5. Faerzress: one environment can support life and frustrate movement

**Reported content:** the Faerzress article describes Underdark magical radiation associated with interference in divination and teleportation. It connects concentrations to settlement choices, magical objects, detectable physical signs, and forms of underground nourishment. It also explicitly juxtaposes conflicting or misleading origin accounts rather than providing an uncontested single history. [W4]

**Interpretation:** the same environmental fact can be an asset and an obstacle. A community may value protection from remote observation while merchants dislike unreliable travel. A food source can be beneficial while tying inhabitants to a particular location. Altering the phenomenon to solve one problem could disturb another relationship.

This is a stronger ecological pattern than assigning each biome a different colored resource. The place has several coupled reasons to matter. Specialists can study concentrations, guide visitors, cultivate compatible organisms, or investigate unusual changes. Residents need not all have the same view: a farmer, courier, ruler, explorer, and religious practitioner can respond differently without any one of them misunderstanding the basic phenomenon.

The wiki's competing accounts also distinguish **world truth from what a character has been led to believe**. A misleading historical vision is not automatically the final objective chronology. That distinction is essential when magical information exists; supernatural presentation does not have to make every source infallible.

## 6. Portals: geography includes knowledge of connections

**Reported content:** the Portal article prominently includes older-edition mechanics and distinguishes ordinary, keyed, one-way, variable, transparent, and restricted-payload portals. It describes durable gateways whose operating knowledge can be lost, as well as malfunction and seepage of destination conditions into an origin. Its historical numerical costs and restrictions are not substituted for SRD 5.2.1 spells. [W5]

**Interpretation:** a map is not complete merely because it records distance. Connections can depend on knowledge, timing, equipment, permission, or a particular kind of traveler. A place a short walk away can be inaccessible; a distant city can become a familiar commute. The holder of a reliable route can become important without possessing the strongest army or spell.

A portal also changes the meaning of an ordinary object. A token might function as a key, a family heirloom, proof of access, or merchandise. The value is relational rather than a fixed intrinsic price. Counterfeiting, forgetting, sharing, inheriting, and trading that knowledge become possible activities.

Seepage offers a particularly effective way for distant places to become locally consequential. The first sign of a connection might be an unfamiliar organism, a change in weather, or a material behaving differently—not a glowing door with a destination label. People can encounter the effect before understanding its cause.

The worldbuilding risk is arbitrary access. A route that opens only when an author needs it can destroy the significance of travel and confinement. A setting can retain surprising doors while still giving them a cause, a discoverable rule, or an explicit uncertainty that inhabitants can discuss.

## 7. Planes: different laws, different experiences, different editions

**Reported content:** the Planar traits article organizes much older-edition material into physical, elemental/energy, alignment, magic, and setting-specific faith traits. Physical traits include time, size, shape, and how matter can change. It distinguishes flowing time from timelessness, and different forms of altered magical operation. The article is a comparative historical catalogue, not the missing chapter of SRD 5.2.1. [W6]

**Interpretation:** a plane need not be a new continent with different scenery. It can alter a relationship that visitors normally take for granted. Movement might depend on direction or thought; matter might resist ordinary work; particular processes might pause; a familiar spell might behave differently. The interesting question is what inhabitants build around those conditions.

“Time works differently” is especially underspecified. Does local experience proceed faster? Does aging stop while conversation continues? Do wounds heal? Do crops grow? Does a maintained spell expire? What happens on returning? These are distinct choices, not synonyms. A story can leave the mechanism mysterious while making immediate stakes clear enough for a character to choose.

Similarly, “thought shapes matter” does not necessarily mean every visitor has unlimited control. A region may respond to a particular being, require deliberate effort, or change unpredictably. That can make concentration, training, art, architecture, and social conduct consequential. The source does not prove how every society would adapt; those are questions for the chosen work or campaign.

### A plane can be familiar before it is explained

The public 2014 planes appendix distinguishes the Material worlds, transitive realms, Inner and Outer Planes, and demiplanes, while allowing different cosmological arrangements. Its portal examples can be ordinary-looking thresholds rather than grand machines. This is an explicitly older primary source supplement to the inaccessible requested Plane article. [P1]

**Interpretation:** a familiar room, road, or hill can provide an emotional and spatial anchor for a strange realm. A changed version of home often creates a sharper question than an entirely unfamiliar map: what remained, what changed, and who remembers the difference? A very large cosmology becomes accessible through a small comparison.

## 8. The Feywild: intensity is not the same as uniform friendliness

**Reported content:** the Feywild article describes an echo of the Material world with heightened sensation and emotion, altered geography, crossings, and variable accounts of time and memory. It distinguishes cosmological models rather than presenting every historical trait simultaneously. Some regions reflect a powerful inhabitant's emotional state without granting that inhabitant conscious control of every change. Its societies and inhabitants are not portrayed as uniformly welcoming. [W7]

**Interpretation:** heightened beauty can make danger more compelling rather than merely disguising it. A visitor may genuinely want to remain because the music, landscape, company, or sense of possibility is extraordinary. The important temptation is not just an illusion hiding a punishment; it can be a real good with a consequential cost.

A responsive landscape can externalize emotion, but it should not erase the complexity of the person. Anger might coexist with affection, grief with humor, and confidence with embarrassment. A forest that mechanically changes color according to one mood meter would be a much thinner interpretation than a place whose changes are suggestive, selective, and sometimes misunderstood.

### Production history prevents false continuity

Richard Baker's 2024 retrospective credits earlier faerie and shadow concepts, his work on Birthright, Jeff Grubb's third-edition reinterpretation, and collaborative fourth-edition development. He identifies his role in naming and clarifying the Feywild and Shadowfell without claiming to have invented their underlying folklore. [P2]

**Interpretation:** the modern setting is partly an act of editorial organization. Giving recurring ideas names and relationships can make them easier to use, even when much of the material is older. That is different from asserting that all earlier books always depicted exactly the same cosmology. Revision can improve accessibility while retaining real discontinuities.

## 9. Candlekeep: knowledge has gates, services, rituals, and bodily needs

**Reported content:** the wiki describes a library-fortress with graded access, specialist roles, protective wards, copying services, communal spaces, and an institutionally preserved body of prophecy. It distinguishes historical admission requirements rather than giving one rule for every era. Its mundane amenities include clothing work, food, lodging, stables, and heated bathing. [W8]

A 2021 publisher introduction emphasizes the contribution of a work the collection lacks as an entrance gift, with possibilities broader than an expensive magical tome. It also describes the Avowed and Miirym, the spectral dragon whose custodial role outlasts her living body. [P3]

**Interpretation:** this is a place whose central value creates activities. Discovery matters because someone can preserve and compare it. Copying requires labor and authorization. The researcher needs somewhere to sleep and people with whom to talk. An archive can be simultaneously a workplace, destination, sacred institution, refuge, and source of social frustration.

Admission through contribution is particularly different from admission through wealth alone. A travel account, recipe, song, observation, or corrected map can make a less powerful visitor valuable. But the institution still has to judge novelty and reliability. Collecting knowledge is not the same as accepting every claim as true.

Miirym creates a contrasting character position: a custodian can preserve collective knowledge while bearing a very personal constraint. The role gives her something to do, people to encounter, and a relationship to the place. It should not reduce her to an automatically hostile security mechanism or an omniscient answer service.

## 10. Waterdeep: comfort, ambition, and adventure can occupy the same address

**Reported content:** James Haeck's 2018 publisher introduction describes distinct urban districts, visible inequality, commerce, entertainment, and differing possibilities for character origins. Its Yawning Portal example places Durnan's hospitality and watchfulness beside an entrance to Undermountain. This is a dated introductory portrayal, not a complete current city history or an endorsement of every characterization in its rhetoric. [P4]

**Interpretation:** the ordinary and extraordinary need not be separated into “safe hub” and “adventure map.” A person can make a living beside danger, enjoy an evening in a storied place, or seek fame among people who know the cost of it. A former adventurer can become an innkeeper without ceasing to be formidable.

Durnan's position is useful for character formation because competence has acquired a different purpose. Experience can become judgment, a business, a reputation, or a habit of noticing trouble. Another veteran might instead crave renewed risk or resent the city's fascination with inexperienced heroes. Similar biographies need not produce identical people.

The limits of the introductory source remain important. A city's richness cannot be demonstrated solely by listing districts or contrasting wealth with poverty. Detailed work, domestic life, civic procedure, family relationships, and competing forms of ambition need their own evidence. The prior dossier's broader institutional and character analyses remain relevant rather than being replaced by a promotional overview.

## 11. A powerful resident can become a regional condition

A 2025 publisher article on lairs describes regional effects as part of how powerful monsters shape their surroundings, including effects that change when the creature dies or moves its lair. Its worked examples are GM advice, not observed campaigns. This supplements rather than replaces the SRD's narrower monster entries. [P5]

**Interpretation:** a threat can be encountered before it is seen. Weather, unusual behavior, altered travel, or local stories can gradually expose an influence. Removing it can change life in the region, making an adventure's aftermath visible. The inhabitants then have something to interpret besides a report that the heroes won a fight.

It is also possible for a powerful presence to be useful to some people and dangerous to others. A community may have adapted to a condition that visitors want removed. That does not imply every monster must become morally ambiguous; it means consequences deserve attention. Victory can be real while still producing further choices about rebuilding, ownership, or memory.

## 12. A worked comparison: how large systems become small decisions

The following is an **explicitly constructed illustration**, not an additional published Realms event. It does not replace the source-grounded situations in dossier 29.

An apprentice surveyor arrives at a scholarly fortress carrying observations of a newly reliable route through dangerous country. A merchant wants the route publicized; a community beside it fears losing control over access; a scholar suspects that its stability depends on a damaged regional enchantment. The apprentice wants recognition and enough money to continue exploring.

The same document now has several possible values: evidence, an entrance gift, a trade advantage, a public service, and a source of risk. The problem does not reduce to one persuasion check or a choice between being selfish and being kind. The apprentice might publish a partial map, verify the suspected enchantment, negotiate local participation, sell an exclusive copy, or abandon the commission.

Several concrete questions keep the situation grounded. Who actually observed the route? Which parts remain uncertain? Does the apprentice own the copied notes? What does the institution promise in return? Is the local objection informed by a real danger, an inherited fear, or an interest in controlling commerce? What happens if another copy already exists?

An interesting outcome need not be a catastrophe. The apprentice might become a respected guide, help establish a festival exchange, discover an error, or form a lasting friendship with a rival researcher. The world acquires depth when these activities leave records, relationships, and changed possibilities.

## 13. What this deeper pass adds to the comparison

The new material adds five distinct worldbuilding relationships to the previous dossier.

**First, a world can inherit functioning systems without inheriting complete understanding.** An old route or enchantment can remain useful after its creator and instructions are gone. This supports discovery, professional expertise, disagreement, and the selective survival of memory.

**Second, local laws are social facts as well as magical facts.** They affect where people settle, what they produce, whom they consult, and which risks they accept. The consequences should be examined before inventing more powers.

**Third, information can create standing.** A person with a discovery, translation, map, or practical technique can become valuable without being a great warrior. Institutions give that value a place to be recognized, contested, or exploited.

**Fourth, unusual environments can create real pleasures.** Beauty, hospitality, mastery, company, performance, and freedom can make a place desirable. A world that only explains its hazards has not explained why people remain there.

**Fifth, the setting's publication history is part of its apparent coherence.** Naming, reorganizing, revising, and adapting ideas can make them more usable while leaving contradictions. Good research keeps those layers visible rather than harmonizing everything into an invented canonical whole.

These are argued interpretations of the selected material, not measured explanations of D&D's commercial success. They also do not establish that every named setting is equally detailed or that a computer simulation automatically reproduces the social creativity of a table.
