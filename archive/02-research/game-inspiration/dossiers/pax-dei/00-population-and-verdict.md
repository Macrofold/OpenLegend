## 2. What each metric answers

| Metric | Useful question | What it cannot establish alone |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Peak concurrency | How many people used a particular client simultaneously at the busiest recorded moment? | Sales, average activity, retention, or profitability. |
| Average concurrency | How much simultaneous activity did that client sustain across a period? | How many distinct people generated it, or whether they paid. |
| Daily/monthly active users | How many distinct users returned within a defined interval? | Their simultaneous density or their purchase value. |
| Owners or units | How many licenses/accounts qualify under the publisher's definition? | Active population, current satisfaction, or recurring revenue. |
| Paid subscribers / plot customers | How many current customers fund recurring offers? | Total population or contribution margin without prices and costs. |
| Review positivity | How participating reviewers rate their experience under the platform's rules. | A representative survey of all buyers or the cause of departures. |
| Company revenue / operating result | The named entity's recorded financial performance. | A game's gross sales, exact production budget, or cash runway. |

The distinct-user count depends on time spent per person. One thousand concurrent users does not mean only one thousand people play the game. Conversely, a large launch-period active-user announcement does not establish a durable paying base.

For example, Mainframe reported more than **90,000 active players across platforms during the first week of 1.0**, as quoted by MassivelyOP from Discord. The original Discord analytics and definition were not independently inspected. This is a period-active claim, not 90,000 sales or concurrent users. [MassivelyOP, October 23, 2025](https://massivelyop.com/2025/10/23/pax-dei-says-its-seen-more-than-90000-active-players-on-all-platforms-since-launch-last-week/)

## 3. The commercial verdict

**Assessment: a product with a committed audience, significant commercial pressure, and unresolved prospects—not a demonstrated large commercial success, and not an established shutdown.**

Later 2026 activity also recovered partly from its midyear lows. The more favorable recent review snapshot in [chapter 04](04-reception.md) is encouraging, although neither signal yet establishes durable recovery or profitability.

The stronger evidence for pressure is the combination of population history, reported staffing reduction, company financial indicators, and the team's own scope and funding statements. [Business evidence](03-business-marketing.md) records each source, date, and accounting boundary. None supplies the current all-platform payer count, complete cost base, or cash position needed for a definitive profitability calculation.

“Successful” also needs a denominator. A small audience can sustain a modestly staffed game with controlled service costs, reliable recurring purchases, and manageable development obligations. The same audience can be insufficient for a much larger production organization and the expectations attached to substantial external financing. There is no universal minimum concurrency that makes an MMO viable.

The public record supports concern about **sustained demand relative to the scale of the undertaking**. It does not justify declaring insolvency, predicting a closure date, or treating every investor dollar as money already spent on this one game.

## 4. A causal interpretation with explicit limits

This dossier's main hypothesis is that the product offered a particularly appealing destination—a beautiful, persistent place built with other people—before enough supporting systems made that destination reliably rewarding for different kinds of players.

That hypothesis connects several independently useful observations in the [reception chapter](04-reception.md): people can admire building while disliking the tasks required to support it; cooperation can be valuable while being difficult to organize; specialization can sound compelling while an individual still feels forced to do everything. It does not imply that every critic shares the same problem or that every departure reflects dissatisfaction.

A plausible reinforcing process is:

1. A player arrives for construction, social life, exploration, or an MMO role.
2. Their preferred activity depends on other activities or available people.
3. When that connection is missing, progress becomes repetitive work with an unclear payoff.
4. Some players depart or reduce their hours.
5. Fewer neighbors, customers, and available companions make the original promise harder for remaining players to realize.

This is **an analytical mechanism to investigate**, not a measured funnel or a model fitted to Pax Dei's internal data. Regional distribution, clans, changing patches, launch curiosity, competing games, seasonality, platform substitution, and ordinary completion of personal projects can also affect the observed population.

### What the evidence changes our confidence in

| Explanation | Assessment |
| --- | --- |
| “Nobody heard about it.” | Too strong. The documented campaign and renewed launch interest contradict a simple absence-of-awareness account. Acquisition efficiency and audience fit remain unknown. |
| “The game has no appeal.” | Contradicted by sustained positive construction and community accounts. The question is how widely, reliably, and economically that appeal can be delivered. |
| “Graphics should have guaranteed success.” | Unsupported. Visual attraction is one component of the experience; it cannot establish the usefulness of its activities or the sustainability of its economics. |
| “The population decline proves these exact design flaws caused it.” | Unsupported. Repeated testimony and developer admissions identify plausible mechanisms, not their causal shares. |
| “The 2024 launch explains the current game completely.” | Incorrect. Important systems and convenience improvements shipped later. Current evidence must be checked against those changes. |
| “This disproves persistent social worlds.” | Not established by one execution. The evidence is more directly informative about interdependence, ordinary-session value, trust, scope, and recurring costs. |

## 5. What remains unknown

A decisive commercial analysis would need platform-separated acquisition cohorts, return rates and playtime, current paid conversion, plot retention, refunds, regional density, and contribution margins. A decisive design analysis would join those cohorts to behavior and departure interviews, including players who left without reviewing.

The absence of that private evidence does not make the public research useless. It determines the appropriate output: a strong warning about several concrete failure modes, bounded claims about commercial performance, and testable implications for Open Legend. It does not support a precise probability of success or a retrospective claim that one change would have rescued the game.
