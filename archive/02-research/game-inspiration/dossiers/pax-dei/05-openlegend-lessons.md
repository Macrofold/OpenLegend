# 05. What Open Legend should learn—and what this research does not decide

**Research synthesis, not an accepted feature specification.** [Dossier index](../pax-dei.md)

## 1. The central judgment

Pax Dei should make Open Legend more demanding about evidence of enjoyable, repeatable play. It should not by itself cause the project to abandon persistent places or human-scale social worlds.

The important comparison is between promises and the systems that fulfill them. In Pax Dei, the promise of a place shaped by its inhabitants can be emotionally compelling. The [reception evidence](04-reception.md) also shows how that promise becomes fragile when ordinary activities, available collaborators, economic exchange, and continuing goals do not support one another. A more attractive landscape does not repair a missing relationship between effort and reward.

Open Legend's opportunity is therefore not simply “Pax Dei, plus AI.” A resident who notices, needs, acts, remembers, and changes the world could make a small community meaningful even when few humans are present. A resident who only produces agreeable conversation would not establish that difference. The added simulation also creates cost, reliability, comprehension, and continuity obligations.

This is a hypothesis worth testing through the existing first-playable work, not evidence that autonomous characters guarantee retention or a sustainable business.

## 3. Make a small place valuable before expanding it

A settlement has several possible kinds of value: it looks good, stores effort, enables activity, expresses identity, attracts visitors, or contains relationships. These are complementary, not interchangeable. A building can be enjoyable as an act of creation even if it has no numerical bonus. Equally, adding arbitrary bonuses does not guarantee that an inhabitant feels real.

For Open Legend, the most useful near-term question is concrete: **does a player understand how one change they make affects one other person's subsequent behavior?** If the answer requires the designer to explain an invisible system or promise future content, the experience is not yet doing the work.

A constructed illustration: a player helps complete a shelter; a resident later uses it for an intelligible reason; that changes where an activity takes place or what the group can attempt. The important observation is the resident's independent follow-through, not an appreciative line of dialogue. This is a possible study within the existing shelter/cooperation scope, not a new requirement for housing recruitment or an economy expansion.

The alternative hypothesis deserves equal respect: players may care more about building expression, discovery, or companionship than a utility consequence. Study what they voluntarily pursue rather than deciding in advance that only instrumental actions count.

## 4. Design interdependence around ordinary availability

A role can be meaningful because another person needs it. It can also become a dependency trap: progress stops whenever a particular specialist is offline, or the player must learn every profession to avoid that interruption.

Open Legend's resident population might reduce that coordination burden, but only if capabilities, willingness, resources, and obligations are legible. Automatically granting every request would eliminate some of the negotiation and independence that make the premise interesting. Refusal without a comprehensible reason would feel arbitrary.

The existing small-group cooperation work is the appropriate place to examine this tension. A useful outcome is an evening in which a human can contribute, receive help, make a choice, and leave a shared undertaking in a clear state without synchronizing a large guild. The same system should leave room for stronger human collaboration when friends are present.

The research does not establish a required player-to-resident ratio, a target population, or a new multiplayer mode. Those require experience and cost evidence from Open Legend itself.

## 5. Give production an actual destination

An economic loop needs more than recipes, skill levels, and a marketplace. It needs someone with a reason and an ability to acquire what is made. If production is primarily a means of gaining crafting XP, output can become a disposal problem rather than a meaningful trade.

For Open Legend, independent residents offer a possible source of demand. That should mean finite needs, budgets or exchangeable goods, preferences, and changing circumstances—not an unlimited buyer installed to make every item profitable. A resident who always buys surplus at a fixed generous price is an economic subsidy, even if an AI describes it convincingly.

The existing immediate-barter and durable-agreement owners are better starting points than importing a global currency or auction house. A small exchange can expose whether the player understands value, whether the counterparty follows through, and whether both parties' later circumstances change.

A constructed comparison: making a tool because a specific person will use it creates a different purpose from making twenty tools solely to unlock the next recipe. Both can have a place. The hypothesis to test is whether the former creates a more memorable and voluntary next action for this audience, not whether all repetition should disappear.

## 6. Preserve the meaning of a shared event

The [technology chapter](02-technology.md) distinguishes stored structures from co-present people and tactical state. That distinction is especially important for a world of persistent characters.

If two human groups interact with apparently the same resident, promise, resource, or construction project, the system needs an intelligible answer about identity and outcomes. Silently creating incompatible versions could damage the very continuity Open Legend is trying to offer.

The current travel proposal already treats shared identity and overload honestly. This study supports examining that contract early; it does not select Pax Dei's implementation, require a global single shard, or propose duplicating residents to increase capacity. Smaller encounter scopes, reservations, admission limits, travel boundaries, and different update rates have different product costs. The relevant choice depends on which interactions must remain jointly observable.

An inexpensive absence strategy may be appropriate for a personal world that is meant to pause. A continuing shared community may need a different policy. Persisting records is not permission to charge continuously for detailed offscreen cognition. Any lower-detail progression must preserve the outcomes and expectations that matter to the experience.

## 7. Treat returning as part of ownership

A player can lose something important even when every construction material is recoverable: the location, familiar route, neighbors, layout, and meaning of a place can be the actual investment.

That is why absence, upkeep, closure, and recovery belong in the experience contract. A future player should understand what continues, what pauses, what can expire, how other people are affected, and what returning restores. The business offer should explain those consequences before they become urgent.

This research selects no Open Legend subscription, rent, idle penalty, or server shutdown rule. It identifies a question existing commercial and continuing-world owners must answer consistently. If a recurring charge is needed to fund real service costs, its explanation should connect to a service the user can recognize and value. Monetization ambiguity can create distrust even when some fees were technically mentioned earlier.

## 8. Graphics: take the concern seriously, then test the right proposition

Mike's concern is reasonable: if a richly produced game struggles, a smaller project cannot assume that good intentions will overcome a weaker presentation. Visual quality affects attention, comprehension, atmosphere, and attachment.

The inference should stop there. Comparing production budgets does not establish a ranking of total player value. Open Legend's current direction deliberately chooses a different visual target. The practical requirement is to make that target coherent, readable, and appealing while demonstrating an experience players cannot get from scenery alone.

A revealing study would let a new player encounter a small, representative place and its people without a developer's explanation of the intended future. Do they understand what they can do? Do the characters' actions matter to their choices? Do they remember an event or person? Do they choose another session? These questions help distinguish a compelling experience from interest in the concept of AI.

Do not excuse confusing UI, poor feedback, unstable performance, or visually inconsistent assets by calling the project a prototype indefinitely. Those problems can prevent a fair test of the premise. Equally, do not expand asset scope simply to imitate the surface of a much larger production.

## 9. Learn from marketing without confusing reach with demand

The [campaign study](03-business-marketing.md) shows why a successful-looking showcase needs careful interpretation. A scheduled event can supply organizers, teammates, goals, rewards, and an audience. Those supports may be excellent marketing while remaining absent from an ordinary new player's session.

For Open Legend, an honest demonstration should make clear which part of an interesting event came from native behavior and which came from facilitation. A small, uncoached session is useful evidence alongside a polished showcase. Founder enthusiasm, creator participation, clicks, and watch time measure different things from return behavior and willingness to pay.

This does not argue against promotion. It argues for aligning the campaign's promise with the experience an acquired player can actually have. A narrow audience that values the delivered experience may be more commercially useful than a much larger audience attracted to an imagined future product.

## 11. The decision this case supports now

Keep the ambition to make places and people worth caring about. Be much stricter about demonstrating that value in a small, repeatable experience before paying to extend it across more geography, more concurrent users, more systems, or more autonomous activity.

Pax Dei's strongest lesson is a dependency: the more a game promises that inhabitants will supply its purpose, the more carefully it must support the conditions under which those inhabitants can do so. Open Legend's characters may change those conditions. They still have to prove their usefulness, reliability, and cost.
