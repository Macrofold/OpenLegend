# G79 — Final Fantasy II

[Library](../README.md) · [Roster](../research-roster.md) · [Progress](../research-progress.md) · [Requirements](../research-requirements.md)

**Full-category research pass: September 26, 2026.** This completes the earlier G79 checkpoint and preserves its six-review comparison and dated player/patch evidence. The subject is the **1988 Final Fantasy II**, not Final Fantasy IV's original North American SNES title and not Final Fantasy Legend II. Research hypotheses are not accepted OpenLegend designs. Story and companion spoilers follow. No firsthand play, full-video viewing or proprietary implementation inspection is claimed.

## 1. Identity, editions and the central promise — R01

Four young survivors become involved in the conflict between the Palamecian Empire and the rebellion. The game replaces the first installment's starting-class commitment with a different promise: characters develop through what they do. It remains a single-player, menu-driven party RPG, with an authored campaign rather than an open-ended war simulation. The original appeared in Japan in 1988; the Steam Pixel Remaster launched July 28, 2021. [I9]

| Edition family | Boundary important to this research |
| --- | --- |
| **Famicom original** | Historical rules include equipment magic penalties, statistical regressions and implementation quirks. The explicitly Famicom-labeled references below must not be treated as universal remaster formulas. [I12][I14] |
| **Origins and other intervening remakes** | Presentation, convenience and balancing changed over repeated releases. A page carrying a modern platform label may still reproduce a decades-old walkthrough. Its internal version and date take precedence over the wrapper. |
| **GBA / PSP** | The additional Soul of Rebirth scenario belongs to this remake lineage; the PSP release additionally has the Arcane dungeon material. The PSP reviews concern these expanded packages, not Pixel Remaster. [I2][I5][I6] |
| **Pixel Remaster** | A new 2D treatment with modern conveniences, not a complete aggregation of every prior version's extras. It omits those bonus scenarios/dungeons. Departing companions return equipment automatically, unlike the original reference's gear-loss rule. [I11][I14] |

The console remaster launch was April 19, 2023 on PS4 and Switch. Subsequent PC updates brought additional options; features visible now should not be projected backward onto a 2021 review. Other current platform routes and aggregate series business evidence are distinguished in §8. [I27][I28]

**Interpretation:** this is an experiment in replacing a single progression ladder with a history of practiced behaviors. That can make a character feel personally authored, but also obscures whether an unsuccessful action means poor planning, inadequate training, incompatible equipment or simply chance.

## 2. Combat and growth vocabulary — R02, R03

### Actions now and consequences later

The immediate verbs remain physical attack, cast, use an item, select targets, manage formation and escape. The long-term consequence is less familiar: different actions contribute to different kinds of development. A named character is not simply a level number plus a fixed class. [I10]

### Rows make formation a targeting and training decision

The original pass named formation but did not explain why it matters. In the Famicom rules, characters in the **back row cannot be targeted by ordinary physical attacks and cannot make normal physical attacks themselves unless using a Bow**; spells are unaffected. If the front row falls, the rear automatically moves forward. Enemy formations also have front/back ranks that determine which monsters can physically attack or be reached in melee. [I12]

That rule composes directly with FFII's growth model: protecting a fragile caster in the back can reduce the physical hits that would otherwise contribute to HP/evasion development, while putting them in front for training exposes them to much more danger. The row system is therefore not just “tank in front, mage in back”; it can change what a character practices and which future stats mature.

This paragraph is explicitly **original/Famicom mechanics evidence**. Pixel Remaster preserves front/back-row battle behavior broadly enough that early patches fixed a back-row promotion bug, but exact original targeting/growth implications should not be projected mechanically unchanged across every remake. [I32]

| Developing component | What it represents and why it matters |
| --- | --- |
| **HP / MP capacity** | Survival and casting reserves. Spending or losing resources can contribute to capacity growth; the remaster also has compensatory HP growth. This is not proof that every individual fight guarantees an increase. |
| **Strength** | Physical capability developed through attacking. It does not make an unfamiliar weapon category already practiced. |
| **Stamina / Magic** | Affect the size of relevant HP/MP growth; they are not the current resource pools themselves. |
| **Spirit / Intelligence** | Support White / Black Magic respectively. They are separate from the proficiency of a particular spell. |
| **Agility, evasion and magical defense** | Concern acting and surviving, not merely reducing a displayed damage number. Exposure and avoidance interact with development. |
| **Weapon and spell proficiency** | Repeated use develops the specific category or spell. A strong swordsman can still be an inexperienced spear user. |

This inventory follows the remaster-era growth explanation; its uncertain phrasing about particular growth triggers is not converted into a guaranteed formula. [I10]

The eight weapon-skill categories are swords, knives, spears, axes, staves, bows, unarmed combat and shields. Weapon and spell skill levels extend to 16, but a skill cap is not a required campaign-completion target. The original systems analysis describes battle rank interacting with current proficiency and action count, making repeated trivial fights progressively less useful. Its exact rank calculation differs from the remaster-era guide's description; this dossier does not silently choose one as an all-version formula. [I12][I15]

**Interpretation:** success has at least two objectives: win this fight and develop a useful future capability. A player may deliberately choose a weaker action for practice. The design becomes fragile when those objectives diverge so far that the best development strategy is to avoid finishing an already solved encounter.

### The original's awkward incentives are not universal instructions

The Famicom reference documents gaining attack/spell-use credit from repeatedly confirming and canceling commands. It also records growth regressions and unusual dual-wield behavior: an off-hand weapon can gain practice without dealing the expected second weapon's damage. These are historical implementation details, not an endorsement of exploiting them or a claim that current Pixel Remaster retains them all. [I12][I13]

One review treats aggressive grinding as central; another guide explicitly says ordinary progress can provide sufficient HP growth without attacking allies. That disagreement matters. A dossier should not turn a famous exploit into a mandatory tutorial simply because the anecdote is memorable. [I1][I10]

## 3. Equipment, magic, supplies and object roles — R03

### Legal equipment is not automatically effective equipment

Characters can be shaped into different roles rather than being locked to the silhouette implied by their starting weapon. A build nevertheless has costs: category practice, available money, action opportunities and equipment interference. Starting Maria with a bow does not establish a permanent archer class. [I3][I13]

Weapons distinguish attack, accuracy, evasion contribution, elemental or monster-family effectiveness, status effects, magic penalty and sometimes an item-use spell. Shields contribute to defense through evasion rather than functioning merely as another armor number. Head, body and hand equipment make additional tradeoffs. The Famicom weapon table separately records whether a spell-casting item can break: using an item is not necessarily equivalent to equipping it. [I15]

**Magic Interference** is a particularly consequential interaction. An otherwise legal weapon/armor setup can undermine spell reliability. Official Steam version 1.1.0, dated January 30, 2024, added the statistic to the status screen and explained that lower values improve success. It exposed the cost rather than announcing the penalty's removal. [I8]

This is a clear design distinction: permission answers whether an action is allowed; compatibility answers how well a combination works. A “can equip anything” promise can remain technically true while a player experiences the practical range of useful combinations as much narrower.

### Named gear changes decisions

The **Blood Sword** is the strongest illustrative exception to choosing by attack value alone. In the original, its low ordinary statistics conceal maximum-HP-based draining, powerful against large-HP targets and reversed against undead. The remaster walkthrough places the available sword in Paul's hidden room during the Cyclone stage; do not copy the original's two-location count into that edition. [I20][I11]

An **Ice Bow**, **Main Gauche** or **Mage's Staff** should likewise be examined for role and secondary function rather than sorted only by raw damage. A guide's recommendation to keep a category consistent is advice about sunk practice, not a rule forbidding later experimentation. [I11][I15]

There is no general weapon-crafting tree. Obtaining **Mythril** and bringing it to the blacksmith changes available shop stock through a specific story transaction. That differs from freely combining arbitrary ores into player-invented equipment. [I11]

### Learn a spell, train a spell, or spend its tome

A spell tome can become a learned capability; using a tome directly in combat instead produces its specified effect and consumes the object. Learning and emergency item use are therefore different spending decisions. Individual spell levels matter independently, and the party-planning reference limits each character to sixteen learned spells rather than all forty. [I16][I13]

Magic uses a shared MP reserve, not FFI's per-tier charge pools. Increasing proficiency can also raise a spell's MP cost. Single-target and group targeting trade concentration against coverage rather than granting equally strong effects on every recipient for free. [I1][I2]

| Family / named example | Concrete function and limit |
| --- | --- |
| **Cure / Life** | Health restoration and revival solve different states. Cure also damages undead in the original rules. Field casting and battle casting do not necessarily use identical modifiers. [I21] |
| **Esuna / Basuna** | Condition recovery is distinct from HP healing. Esuna's available cures improve with training; merely learning it does not guarantee that every ailment can be removed. Exact level/success thresholds are version-dependent. [I22][I1] |
| **Fire / Blizzard / Thunder** | Elemental options share the need for individual practice; training one does not automatically train the other two. Tomes can provide an immediate alternative to developing a new caster. [I16] |
| **Blink / Berserk** | Avoidance and offensive support change subsequent actions instead of only causing immediate damage. Equipment compatibility is relevant to support as well as spectacular attacks. [I13] |
| **Toad** | A condition on a party member can effectively remove an enemy from battle. This is not evidence that every boss accepts the effect. [I24] |
| **Teleport** | A retreat tool outside battle and a removal attempt inside it. The original entry adds an HP cost to dungeon escape; that exact historical formula is not assumed for every remake. [I23] |

**Interpretation:** access, training, capacity, target legality and reliability are separate gates. A player who buys a promising spell has not automatically acquired its mature effectiveness. This supports personalization, but needs unusually good feedback to avoid making purchases appear broken.

### Consumables and persistent knowledge

Potions restore health; Ethers restore MP; Phoenix Downs address incapacitation; Antidotes, Gold Needles and other condition-specific objects bypass dependence on a trained cure. Cottages support travel recovery. Offensive objects such as the **Gaia Drum** and **Antarctic Wind** offer effects outside a character's learned spell list. Original item targeting and break rules are explicitly version-labeled in the item reference. [I17]

A **keyword** and a **key item** are different inventories. The former records a topic to ask about; the latter can physically satisfy an interaction. The vocabulary includes Wild Rose, Mythril, Dreadnought, Sunfire, Wyverns and Ultima Tome. The object list includes the Ring, Canoe, Snowcraft, Goddess's Bell, Egil's Torch, Pendant, Wyvern Egg, masks and Crystal Rod. Knowing a term does not necessarily mean possessing its associated object. [I18][I19]

## 4. Exploration, companions and the authored world — R02, R06, R08

### Where freedom is real and where it is bounded

The repeating route is town information and preparation, travel, dungeon or mission, then changed access or capabilities. Canoe travel, Josef's Snowcraft and later ships/airship extend reach. **Final Fantasy II also introduces the series' first Chocobo**: the forest below Kashuan provides a temporary fast-overland mount that helps the party cross dangerous world-map distance without treating it as a permanent owned vehicle. [I11][I33] Travel remains a terrain-and-access system, not a seamless simulation of every apparent surface. The walkthrough's route warnings also matter: a reachable region can contain enemies far beyond the party's present ability. [I11]

Dungeon doors do not always promise treasure. Empty trap rooms and their additional encounter burden recur in both criticism and player accounts. Navigation can therefore become a test of prior knowledge rather than a meaningful choice among visible rewards. A map that reduces getting lost does not itself make those empty detours valuable. [I1][I7]

### A stable core with a changing fourth member

Firion, Maria and Guy form the sustained core; temporary companions occupy the fourth position, with Leon returning for the endgame. Minwu arrives as an accomplished healer, whereas Josef, Gordon, Leila and Ricard offer different initial capabilities and narrative associations. These are authored joins and departures, not freely recruited citizens from a simulated population. [I13][I14]

The practical question is not only whether a guest is strong, but what the party becomes dependent on while that guest is present. A powerful healer can obscure that the permanent core has neglected recovery training. Pixel Remaster's automatic return of departing equipment removes one avoidable inventory punishment; it does not preserve the departed character's future turns or learned spells in the active party. [I11]

The Empire and rebellion are plot factions, not interchangeable employers with continuously simulated reputation. NPCs offer dialogue, access and services; there is no general diplomacy engine, romance approval system, cooperative multiplayer, housing construction or player market. A scene of affection or temptation should not be inflated into a player-controlled relationship mechanic. [I4][I9]

### Keywords as a conversational tool

**Learn** saves selected terms; **Ask** reuses them with appropriate NPCs; presenting items addresses another class of response. Wild Rose is the rebellion's password and can signal loyalty in scripted conversations. This is a finite authored vocabulary, not free-form natural-language persuasion, rumor propagation or an autonomous belief model. [I18][I30]

The keyword system can give conversation a satisfying state: the player returns knowing something they did not know before. Its weakness is also visible: when every highlighted term must be learned and repeated only to advance a single route, the extra input need not create a meaningful decision.

### War, loss and remembered places

**Story spoilers:** the opening establishes vulnerability through a defeat, then situates the survivors among resistance leaders and occupied places. Altair, Fynn, Kashuan, Deist and Mysidia are not simply shop tiers; they connect shelter, occupation, lost royalty, endangered dragoons and forbidden magic. Later destruction changes the meaning of returning to familiar settlements. [I9][I11]

The Dreadnought translates imperial menace into a concrete machine the party must sabotage. Sunfire is visible before the party can carry it: Egil's Torch supplies the missing affordance. This turns a location into a remembered future opportunity rather than a room useful only once. [I31]

Character attachment remains uneven by modern standards. Firion has relatively little personal development, whereas temporary allies can be memorable through a specialized role, a short relationship and an irreversible authored consequence. A story death is not the same rule as a recoverable combat knockout; neither implies universal permadeath. [I1][I6]

**Interpretation:** the story makes the party's changing composition emotionally legible while the growth system makes it mechanically consequential. This works best when the player understands which investments will persist; surprise loss can otherwise feel like the game confiscating effort rather than dramatizing sacrifice.

## 5. Economy, failure and return play — R04

Gil funds spells, equipment, supplies and services. Training adds another cost that is not priced in the shop: action time. A cheap new spell can be expensive to make useful, while an expensive consumable can solve an immediate problem without that investment. Treating all costs as currency misses much of the game's economy. [I10][I16]

Inn expenditure depends on restoration needs, rather than merely the town's identity. The original Cure entry notes that healing before an inn can reduce the bill, illustrating a conversion between spell use and currency. That is a historical worked rule, not a universal fixed-price table for all remakes. [I21]

A defeat can indicate a dangerous region, neglected defense, incompatible equipment, an untrained response or bad luck. Increasing HP alone does not cure paralysis or ensure an escape command works. The game's strongest planning is diagnostic; its weakest loop is repeating an activity without understanding which variable is actually improving. [I10][I12]

Pixel Remaster's save conveniences and later boost controls reduce different costs. Faster growth changes preparation time; an encounter toggle changes travel exposure; showing an existing penalty changes information. These should not all be collapsed into “easy mode.” Difficulty, inconvenience and opacity are different problems. [I8]

Return play includes alternate builds, completion records and the challenge content of the relevant edition. In the PSP Arcane material, chosen keywords determine rooms and puzzles and connect to rewards; the same conversational vocabulary gains a different use. [I6]

**Soul of Rebirth** deserves a more concrete boundary than the original one-line mention. In the GBA/PSP lineage it unlocks after completing the main story and shifts control to **Minwu, Scott, Josef and Ricard**—dead supporting characters whose prior combat development materially affects the bonus scenario—inside an afterlife story that parallels/completes the ending. [I34] It is therefore both a story-perspective shift and a consequence of how much the player invested in temporary/departing allies before their authored deaths.

Pixel Remaster does **not** include Soul of Rebirth or the PSP Arcane dungeons. Those are historical remake extras, not a present-day service update or hidden PR endgame. [I6][I9]

There is no native daily-energy economy, seasonal battle pass or paid power progression in the studied campaign. Current soundtrack and bundle listings are commercial extras, not additional playable FFII scenarios. [I9]

## 6. Worked interactions — R05

These are **constructed explanations of documented rules**, not firsthand playtest logs. They identify the decision and its failure boundary without promising optimal builds or guaranteed growth.

### A. A newly purchased weapon can make a character worse

**Intention:** strengthen a practiced swordsman. **Conditions:** a spear with attractive raw statistics becomes available. **Sequence:** compare the item's numbers and the wielder's category proficiency; try it in a manageable encounter before relying on it against a boss. **Interaction:** equipment quality and learned proficiency are independent. **Result:** the purchase creates a possible future role, not an automatic immediate upgrade. **Next decision:** invest practice or keep the mature sword setup. **Limit:** a comparison that ignores enemy defenses or affinities still cannot identify the best choice. [I10][I15]

### B. Expose a hidden cost before abandoning magic

**Intention:** make an unreliable support spell useful. **Conditions:** current Pixel Remaster with the Magic Interference display. **Sequence:** inspect the statistic, compare a different equipment set, then test the same spell against a comparable situation. **Interaction:** legal gear can penalize a different action family. **Result:** the player can isolate one plausible cause rather than buying another spell blindly. **Next decision:** accept the defense tradeoff or reserve that character for another role. **Limit:** lowering interference does not remove insufficient training, targeting restrictions or chance. [I8][I13]

### C. Take the immediate effect or keep the learning opportunity

**Intention:** answer a threat with an element the party has not trained. **Conditions:** own an appropriate tome. **Sequence:** choose between learning it and using the object's specified combat effect. **Interaction:** the same object is an investment or a consumable intervention. **Result:** direct use can avoid waiting for a weak new spell to mature. **Next decision:** replace the spent tome or train a permanent answer. **Limit:** consuming it does not also teach the character; the effect's targets and level must be checked rather than inferred from the tome's name. [I16]

### D. Turn remembered fire into a usable mission resource

**Intention:** carry Sunfire for the Dreadnought mission. **Conditions:** reach Kashuan but lack the proper carrier. **Sequence:** notice the flame, obtain Egil's Torch, return and use it. **Interaction:** knowledge of a location, possession of a tool and a particular interaction combine. **Result:** a previously unusable object becomes transportable. **Next decision:** proceed with the mission after preparing for its risks. **Limit:** knowing the Sunfire keyword is not equivalent to carrying Sunfire; arbitrary inventory containers are not documented substitutes. [I31]

### E. Reassess the core when a specialist leaves

**Intention:** preserve a functioning party after temporary support ends. **Conditions:** the group has relied on Minwu or another guest. **Sequence:** inspect the permanent members' recovery options, supplies and practiced roles; equip returned items where useful in Pixel Remaster. **Interaction:** narrative membership changes the available action set while inventory handling follows an edition-specific rule. **Result:** preparation addresses the capability gap rather than only replacing a weapon. **Next decision:** train, purchase supplies or take a shorter route. **Limit:** returned equipment cannot reproduce a departed character's developed magic. [I11][I14]

### F. A weak-looking sword tests how the player reads an enemy

**Intention:** defeat a high-HP opponent. **Conditions:** possess the Blood Sword and understand the relevant edition's drain behavior. **Sequence:** evaluate the opponent's nature and the weapon's secondary effect rather than only attack power. **Interaction:** damage tied to the target's maximum HP can invert normal equipment ranking. **Result:** a specialized tool becomes disproportionately useful. **Next decision:** keep it for the next target or restore ordinary equipment. **Limit:** undead reverse the benefit; poor hit reliability and other costs still matter. This is not a claim that the sword trivializes every encounter. [I20]

## 7. Presentation, information and feel — R07

The remake lineage demonstrates two different visual ambitions. VanOrd's PSP review singles out places such as snowy Salamand and larger presentation flourishes, while the remaster uses revised pixel characters and new musical arrangements instead of simply reproducing the PSP look. Neither treatment is an objectively complete preservation of the other. [I5][I9]

Lada finds the remaster's color and character flexibility appealing, while recording font/spacing reservations. DeAngelus prefers some earlier sprite choices and finds the new typeface weak despite praising the music. Their responses distinguish visual identity, readability and nostalgic preference rather than making detail count a proxy for quality. [I2][I3]

The later **Magic Interference** display is a more consequential interface example than a cosmetic refresh. The same action can feel arbitrary when its penalty is invisible and intelligible when the relationship is exposed. This is analysis of the dated player complaint and official change; there is no evidence that a particular Steam review caused the patch. [I7][I8]

Maps, repeat-action combat and modern saving reduce navigation/input/recovery friction. They cannot independently resolve whether the progression incentives are satisfying. A faster repetitive action is still repetitive, and an automated routine can practice the wrong capability as efficiently as the right one. [I2][I4]

## 8. Development, discovery and commercial boundaries — R09–R11

Sakaguchi's anniversary recollection attributes the growth/story experiment strongly to Akitoshi Kawazu and describes choosing a new protagonist and system instead of a direct continuation. He links the abrupt opening defeat to the appeal of cinematic opening hooks. This is retrospective participant testimony, inspected through a written transcription rather than watched footage. [I25]

TOSE separately identifies its development work on the mobile/Steam Pixel Remaster series. The storefront's Square Enix credit therefore should not be read as a complete staffing list. No verified FFII remaster budget, total team count, proprietary engine analysis or profitability figure was established. [I26]

The visible marketing proposition is a recoverable piece of series history with modern presentation and convenience. The six-game console rollout and individual/bundle purchase routes offer both franchise completion and selective entry. These are observable distribution choices, not proof that nostalgia caused a particular share of purchases. [I27][I9]

**Sharing hypothesis:** unusual self-training stories, a surprising equipment interaction and disagreement over the keyword system are compact topics fans can explain to one another. The same oddity can generate advocacy or ridicule. No measured creator-conversion rate or origin of a specific viral trend is claimed.

### September 2026 current-store cross-check

The September 27 audit re-read the live Steam storefront rather than relying only on 2021 helpful reviews. The English corpus remains **Very Positive** at roughly **81% positive across about 2.2k English reviews**, with the recent 30-day sample around **90% positive**. The current product page also explicitly advertises the modern assist layer: encounter toggling, compensatory HP and weapon-skill-growth multipliers from 0× to 4×, while warning that this remaster omits some content from earlier rereleases. [I9][I35]

That current aggregate does not settle the old design dispute. The most useful interpretation is narrower: modern distribution lets players **change the cost of practicing the system** without changing its fundamental use-based identity, while the Pixel Remaster's omission of Soul of Rebirth/Arcane content remains a separate preservation tradeoff.

## 10. Transferable patterns and failure modes — R13

**Practice can describe a character without a class label.** Dependency: the player understands which action develops which capability. Failure mode: visible behavior and hidden accounting diverge, rewarding repetitive manipulation instead of interesting use.

**A general permission can coexist with sharp compatibility costs.** Dependency: expose penalties at the point of composition. Failure mode: “any character can do anything” feels misleading because the legal combinations conceal severe dysfunction.

**Knowledge can be an inventory with consequences.** Dependency: a learned term opens more than a mandatory extra click. Failure mode: automatic collection and repetition masquerade as investigation.

**Temporary companions can teach through capability contrast.** Dependency: investment and departure rules are intelligible. Failure mode: the guest solves the very problems the permanent party needed to learn, leaving a sudden difficulty cliff.

**Changing a location can communicate war without simulating a war economy.** Dependency: players know the place before it changes. Failure mode: destruction is merely another inaccessible shop, without remembered human context.

**Show the cost before removing the cost.** Dependency: distinguish a balance problem from an information problem. Failure mode: adjusting numbers without exposing the relationship leaves players just as confused, while simplifying everything can destroy the intended tradeoff.

**Exceptional equipment needs counterexamples.** Dependency: its secondary effect has legible conditions and limits. Failure mode: a percentage-based effect dominates every boss, or a tool appears useless because the only useful property is hidden.

These are analytical patterns, not instructions to reproduce protected names, assets, story events or exact formulas in another game.
