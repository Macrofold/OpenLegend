# S-GAME: studio history, production and creative identity

**Research date:** 2026-10-31. **Scope:** the company and production history behind _Phantom Blade Zero_, with dated evidence distinguished from company claims and analysis. For audience acquisition, advertising and sales, see [marketing and business](04-marketing-business.md).

## Why the studio's history matters

S-GAME already had years of experience developing the world, characters, combat and commercial products behind its 3D action presentation. Its route included commercial PC action, Chinese mobile games, outside investment and international publishing. Calling _Phantom Blade Zero_ a debut without qualification obscures that preparation. [S02](#s02) [S03](#s03)

The useful interpretation is accumulated capability, accompanied by considerable risk. A recognizable creative identity survived several changes of format, but the commercial history includes financial pressure, monetization criticism and a decision to concentrate production on one large project. Neither the founder's passion nor launch demand establishes profitability or makes this a low-risk model for another studio. [S04](#s04) [S05](#s05)

### The original games established identity before spectacle

The first _Rainblood_ was a compact turn-based RPG with hand-drawn presentation. Its author's 2010 description emphasized a dark martial world, a concentrated story in one town, and a distinction between manageable main-story combat and more demanding side challenges. The contemporary post also advertised _City of Flame_ as its forthcoming continuation. These are roots of the series' mood and storytelling, rather than evidence that Zero retained its original combat structure. [S06](#s06)

RPGFan's Neal Chandran awarded _Town of Death_ 88/100 in June 2010. He particularly valued its distinctive artwork and atmosphere, while describing a short experience and less distinctive stock sound effects. This is evidence of an early favorable critical response from one reviewer, not a broad consensus or proof of commercial scale. [S13](#s13)

_Mirage_ already combined recognizable protagonists with real-time action: players could switch between Soul and Shang or play locally together, collect equipment and abilities, and discover story through documents. Its Steam page showed 71% positive among 148 reviews when accessed, and displayed a Metacritic score of 68. That modest, platform-specific record is a useful corrective to treating the earlier catalog as uniformly acclaimed. [S07](#s07)

### Mobile games supplied experience and a commercial base

The Chinese _Phantom Blade_ games gave S-GAME repeated opportunities to develop action, presentation and progression around the same fictional world. Liang's later account says mobile proceeds initially financed a smaller version of Zero before outside investment expanded its scope. That establishes a financial bridge between formats; it does not disclose the amount of retained earnings invested. [S14](#s14)

The mobile era also brought tension between creative identity and monetization. A reported history describes criticism of the second and third games for commercialization and paid advantages. Treat that as documented criticism, not a claim that every player rejected those titles or that those complaints automatically apply to Zero. [S04](#s04)

_Executioners_ matters because it prevents an inaccurate “first overseas game” narrative. S-GAME's 2023 announcement presented a free-to-play side-scrolling RPG with four heroes, skill-chain combinations, Phantom bonuses, equipment, procedural dungeons and cooperative play. Its global launch was an earlier publishing exercise and a way for overseas players to encounter the same universe. [S03](#s03)

Individual App Store comments from 2023–2024 praise its art, direct combat and customization; others describe freezing, overheating, repetition or dissatisfaction with mobile monetization. These examples show divergent player experiences, not representative prevalence or verified current defects. Regional storefront availability is also insufficient to establish a worldwide service closure. [S15](#s15)

## Funding, team and cost

### Investment increased capacity without establishing a publisher takeover

Contemporary reporting on corporate records identifies Tencent as acquiring a 25% stake in 2021. It also records earlier backing associated with ZhenFund, NetEase and other investors, and the exit of early shareholders. The percentage is evidence about that transaction, not an independently audited current ownership table. [S16](#s16)

In his 2021 letter, Liang described financing in the hundreds of millions of renminbi and said the agreement preserved operating and creative independence without requiring product partnerships. This supports describing S-GAME as Tencent-backed. It does not support saying Tencent controlled the game's creative decisions. The letter also describes earlier private PC/console development from around 2018. [S02](#s02)

Liang's August 2025 interview again described S-GAME retaining development and publishing control. He framed the objective as a polished expression of the team's ideas, rather than simply making the largest possible game. That is a stated creative and business position; financial results require separate evidence. [S14](#s14)

### The budget figure has a traceable but limited meaning

The commonly repeated **RMB300–400 million** development figure appeared as Liang's projection of eventual research and development cost in an August 2024 _China Business Journal_ interview. Launch reporting repeated the range. The careful description is a founder-reported development budget, initially projected in 2024, rather than an audited final all-in expenditure. [S17](#s17) [S18](#s18)

Public evidence does not establish an advertising budget, complete contractor costs, financing returns, taxes, refunds, platform deductions or developer net receipts. Gross preorder revenue therefore cannot simply be compared with that development estimate to declare the project profitable. Recoupment calculations need consistent cost and revenue definitions.

### Internal headcount was only part of the production network

In December 2025, Liang reported approximately 140 employees: about 100 in Beijing, 20 handling capture and animation in Shanghai, 16 in Hong Kong's creative art team, and an overseas publishing team in Los Angeles. That is a dated company account, not verified launch staffing or the total number of contributors. [S11](#s11)

In an October 2026 interview, he credited experienced Chinese outsourcing studios for animation, modeling and concept art. He also described assigning substantial content to young developers. A relatively small internal team can therefore coexist with extensive production support; headcount alone is a poor proxy for either cost or ease of replication. [S19](#s19)

Invest Hong Kong announced a new S-GAME office in August 2026 covering publishing, distribution, promotion and intellectual-property business. The company identified international advertising and possible future screen adaptations among its remit. These are organizational capabilities and ambitions, not evidence that any particular film or television project was commissioned. [S20](#s20)

## Production process: focus, iteration and exposure

### Separate the age of the world from development of this game

The fictional world dates back to Liang's student work. Zero's concept planning, small-team experimentation and full production are different stages. The 2017 planning date, circa-2018 private development account and 2022 full-production date should not be collapsed into either “made in two years” or “twenty years in development.” [S02](#s02) [S11](#s11)

This distinction matters when evaluating production efficiency. A late-stage game benefits from earlier writing, design practice, commercial relationships and tools, even when those years did not employ its eventual production team. Conversely, counting all earlier creative work as full production exaggerates the project's staffing cost and duration.

### Public demonstrations also tested the product's credibility

Liang explained in 2024 that the reveal's fluid movement led some viewers to suspect a cinematic presentation rather than playable action. Bringing hands-on demonstrations to events was intended to answer that doubt. The lesson from this account is concrete: when the core promise seems unusually strong, independent interaction can resolve a credibility problem that additional edited trailers may leave intact. [S21](#s21)

A contemporary reporter documented heavy demand for the Chinese demonstrations and Liang reported more than 2,800 participants across Bilibili World and ChinaJoy. His comments about relatively efficient early exposure belong in the [marketing chapter](04-marketing-business.md), with their date and self-report limitations preserved. [S17](#s17)

### Concentrating resources: potential benefits and financial exposure

Launch-week reporting of Liang's retrospective describes canceling other projects, concentrating resources on Zero, and discarding or rebuilding material that had already been developed. This supports a history of consequential iteration, not an assumption that every production decision was efficient. [S05](#s05)

The creative benefit is the ability to align people and resources around a coherent result. The tradeoff is less diversification and more sunk cost if major revisions or weak sales follow. Those are analytical consequences of the described approach, not a disclosed cash-flow forecast. Open Legend should not infer from an attractive result that abandoning other income or expanding expenditure is necessary to achieve a recognizable game.

The June 2026 delay letter attributed the 50-day postponement to specific improvements, including character models, environments and visual quality without ray tracing. That is the developer's explanation for the delay, not independent confirmation that all performance or quality problems were solved before launch. [S12](#s12)

## Kungfupunk and the animation pipeline

### A specific identity with an accessible promise

Liang's 2023 description combines martial arts, machinery and occult influences under the name “kungfupunk.” This is a useful example of cultural specificity supporting a legible player fantasy. [S01](#s01)

The analytical value is coherence: the appearance, movement, weapons and story can point toward the same experience. A player need not understand every historical or literary reference to recognize the appeal of fighting like a martial-arts film protagonist. That makes the identity useful in both play and promotion, while leaving room for deeper interpretation.

### Do not freeze the production account at the reveal

The 2023 announcement described filmed martial-arts reference and handcrafted animation. Later official disclosures explicitly describe motion capture and Donnie Yen's performance. These accounts should be dated, not flattened into a categorical claim that the finished game used no motion capture. [S01](#s01) [S20](#s20)

The August 2026 government announcement, quoting S-GAME, identifies Yen as creative consultant since 2023 and facial/motion performer for Mó Yuan. It also describes contributions from Hong Kong film choreographers. His role therefore has more substance in the public account than a launch endorsement, while its precise extent remains a company disclosure. [S20](#s20)

In the October 2026 director interview, the internal action team translates cinematic movement into playable segments, including transitions, pauses and connections. Liang also describes bosses through the combined effect of weapon, personality, staging and music, with earned weapons carrying part of an encounter's identity forward. [S19](#s19)

For analysis, the important principle is the connection between production craft and player control. Authentic reference material does not automatically produce responsive combat; it must be adapted to interruption, input, readability and encounter pacing. Launch reviews and player reports determine how successfully that translation worked.

## Audience inheritance and remaining limits

Liang claimed a franchise fanbase above 20 million in 2023, mostly from games released in China. The metric was not defined. It should not become a count of active players, premium purchasers or people already intending to buy Zero. [S01](#s01)

An existing audience can explain why an apparently unfamiliar overseas studio had a starting base. It cannot establish how many launch customers came from that base, from action-game audiences, from cultural interest or from publicity. Those are separate attribution questions addressed in [marketing and business](04-marketing-business.md).

Several corrections keep the history usable:

- Use **2014** for the first mobile _Phantom Blade_. A later retrospective's September 2017 date conflicts with contemporary coverage. [S08](#s08)
- Qualify “debut” with **large-scale 3D AAA production**; earlier PC and global releases are documented. [S03](#s03) [S07](#s07)
- Keep fan counts, downloads, paid copies, wishlists, gross billings and profits separate.
- Treat ownership, team size, budget and production plans as dated evidence, with their stated boundaries.
- Do not infer a worldwide _Executioners_ shutdown from an isolated storefront removal, or a complete canceled-project list from a general statement that projects were cut.

The unresolved items are current audited ownership, final all-in costs, advertising expenditure, the older games' comparable financial results and service status by region. These gaps limit financial conclusions; they do not prevent identifying the studio's accumulated creative and production experience.
