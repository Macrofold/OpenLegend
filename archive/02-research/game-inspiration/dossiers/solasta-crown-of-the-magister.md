# G179 — Solasta: Crown of the Magister

**Full R01–R14 research pass, economics/enjoyment amendment, and applicable R15 investigation.** Research: October 20, 2026. Scope: the original game and its expansions; **Solasta II is a separate product**. This is source-based reference research, not a playtest or an accepted OpenLegend mechanic. **Spoilers:** the setting's historical disaster, the main threat, and expansion premises. Standards: [research requirements](../research-requirements.md), [economics, enjoyment and creation amendment](../research-economics-engagement-and-creation.md), and [roster](../research-roster.md).

## R01 — Identity, versions and the player promise

Tactical Adventures developed and published Solasta, a party-based tactical RPG using the SRD 5.1 rules framework. Steam Early Access began October 20, 2020; the original PC release followed May 27, 2021. The player creates four adventurers and leads them through exploration, conversations, travel and turn-based encounters. The distinguishing promise is a readable, three-dimensional tabletop battlefield: illumination, elevation and movement spells affect what the party can do. [S01]

This is especially relevant to OpenLegend as a study of **capabilities that make a place tactically meaningful**. It is a weaker direct model for personalities developing through experience. Its original personality flags select written dialogue; the developer explicitly described them as fixed. A dramatic rescue can become the player's remembered story without the game subsequently changing the rescued character's temperament. [S05] That distinction prevents attributing an emergent character simulation to a system that primarily supplies combat situations and authored reactions.

| Version or addition | Material difference | Boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Original campaign, Crown of the Magister | Four custom protagonists; Council commissions, ruins and a central escalating threat. | Current official campaign cap is 12. Launch reviews describe an earlier level-10 game. [S01, S17] |
| Free updates and Primal Calling | Sorcerer was added free; Primal Calling supplies barbarian, druid and half-orc options. | Class DLC and free system changes are different purchases/dependencies. [S07] |
| Lost Valley, April 14, 2022 | Separate level 1–12 adventure; faction choices determine escape from Orenetis's isolated Dominion. | Its branching faction campaign should not be attributed to the original's mostly linear plot. The accompanying online co-op update was free. [S18] |
| Inner Strength, November 14, 2022 | Bard, monk, warlock and dragonborn; additional subclass options with Lost Valley. | Its free backgrounds lack original-campaign background quests. More character options do not imply more personal questlines. [S19] |
| Palace of Ice, May 25, 2023 | Level 10–16 continuation in the Snow Alliance; import an existing party or create level-10 characters; gnome and tiefling options. | Original campaigns remain capped at 12. Higher-level custom campaigns and new editor facilities accompanied the expansion. [S17] |
| Community campaigns and Unfinished Business | New authored adventures versus unofficial changes to classes, progression and rules. | Neither is evidence that those mechanics exist in unmodified Solasta. See R15. |

The developer's 2024 account describes a multi-platform release history, continued DLC and a later sequel. This dossier's detailed interface and mod observations concern PC. It does not certify feature parity across PC, Xbox and PlayStation, or substitute the sequel's rules and family narrative for the original. [S02]

## R02 — Actual play and the repeated decision loop

The central sequence is **prepare a party → inspect an encounter → commit resources and positions → resolve uncertain actions → recover and acquire new options → try those options in another environment**. Early play teaches basic actions and survival; established play adds combinations and resource management; late play tests a much larger spell and equipment vocabulary. The pleasure is often recognizing a solution the party could not execute earlier, rather than accumulating a higher damage number.

| System | What the player actually does |
| --- | --- |
| Identity and development | Select ancestry, class, background, ability scores, skills, appearance and personality; choose later class features, spells and feats. The four members are a party of protagonists rather than one hero plus three interchangeable followers. [S02, S10] |
| Tactical actions | Move, attack, cast, dodge, disengage, shove, ready attacks and respond to reaction prompts. Position, attack rolls, saving throws and condition rules matter. [S29] |
| Space and objects | Climb, jump, fly and use designated passages or interactable objects; illuminate an area or exploit destructible scenery. This is an authored interaction vocabulary, not unrestricted manipulation of everything visible. [S01, S30] |
| Stealth and discovery | Use cautious movement, scout, detect traps, arrange the opening engagement and seek concealed information. A discovered encounter can be a positioning problem before it becomes an initiative sequence. [S29] |
| Magic | Choose damage, healing, protection, control and traversal effects; manage spell resources and concentration. Competing uses of concentration prevent every useful persistent spell operating together. [S10, S29] |
| Equipment and economy | Equip and identify gear, choose attuned items, buy and sell, learn recipes, craft and allocate faction rewards. Acquisition is principally NPC exchange and adventuring, not a player marketplace. [S07, S14] |
| Quests and social play | Undertake main, side and eligible background quests; choose dialogue responses; gain faction standing. Lost Valley supplies a different political choice structure. [S06, S18] |
| Construction and management | No general settlement, household or dynasty-management loop is documented. Dungeon Maker authors adventures outside an ordinary campaign. |

Solasta makes a particularly useful distinction between **having a rule** and **making it comfortable to use**. MMORPG's reviewer valued adjustable rules, generous tutorials and the ability to correct some action-order mistakes. PCGamesN valued environmental tactics but found much exploration predetermined by the build chosen before entering the dungeon. Both accounts can be true: a clear interface can expose a strategically narrow set of available answers. [S11, S12]

## R03 — Equipment, party composition and the fictional economy

Party composition determines both combat options and access to useful tasks. A party that can illuminate enemies, handle locks, heal, engage at range and survive melee has several ways to recover from a bad opening. Four individually strong damage builds need not provide those complementary capabilities. This is an analytical consequence of the documented action vocabulary, not a claim that one compulsory party composition exists.

Recipes are learned for the party, but crafting requires suitable tools, materials and capability. Magic equipment uses **primed** items rather than arbitrary ordinary weapons; a found recipe is therefore not automatically a usable upgrade. Crafting advances during travel or resting. Faction relationships also gate specialized stock: the Arcaneum supplies crafting inputs, the Tower of Knowledge recipes, the Circle of Danantar magic arms and armor, and the Antiquarians accessories. Relics and commissions connect exploration to those relationships. [S14]

The practical flow is loot or quest reward → decide what to keep → sell surplus or assign a relic → buy an enabling item or complete a recipe → equip within character restrictions. Gold, components, consumables, carrying capacity and attunement make different kinds of scarcity. The 2023 balance notes explicitly say shops and crafting were intended to compensate for poor loot luck; some strength-setting items were too cheap and were repriced. This is a documented economic intention, not evidence of a simulated competitive market. [S07]

Scavengers are a notable reduction of inventory labor. After a location is cleared, they recover abandoned loot for later collection or sale. The player can keep expedition pacing without personally carrying every mundane weapon home. PC Gamer and PCGamesN singled this out as useful design. [S08, S12] The transferable question is which collection decisions are interesting and which merely require repeated transport; automation can remove the latter while leaving scarce equipment choices intact.

## R04 — Progression, time, loss and reasons to return

Levels expand capabilities along a chosen class, while equipment and party composition change how those capabilities combine. Feats need not be passive increments: the March 2021 redesign intentionally gave martial characters more uses for bonus actions and reactions, including weapon-style distinctions. A documented example is spending a reaction to raise a shield against ranged fire. The developer was trying to change decisions during play, not just character-sheet totals. [S31]

Travel and rest connect encounters. Food and recovery resources create preparation choices, while crafting converts otherwise passing time into equipment. The developer originally avoided timed quests and limited long rests to designated opportunities. [S05] These systems can make the expedition coherent, but can also become solved chores. Steam reviewer Krave stopped enjoying ration management and disabled encumbrance; the ability to alter the rules helped that player finish. That is an observed preference, not proof that everyone wants logistics removed. [S13]

| Boundary | Lost, retained and restored |
| --- | --- |
| Character reaches zero HP | A dying member may be stabilized or healed. Failed death saves can produce death; resurrection is a distinct intervention. [S29] |
| Unrecoverable death | An unresurrectable party member produces game over rather than a normal replacement-recruitment story. Ordinary recovery is loading a save. [S05, S29] |
| Ordinary failed encounter | Reloading restores the chosen save's state; the player retains knowledge of opponents and the attempted plan. This is learning across attempts, not a character remembering the erased timeline. |
| Ironman | An optional stricter save mode was a crowdfunding feature. It changes the cost of failure; it does not add an injury-driven replacement economy. [S03] |
| New campaign | A new party/build and campaign state create new choices; player rules knowledge persists. There is no documented account-wide prestige economy. |
| Palace of Ice continuation | Import can preserve the previous party into a new high-level adventure. A fresh level-10 start is also supported. [S17] |
| Logout | Campaign saves preserve state. No seasonal wipe or obligation to maintain an always-online settlement was found. |

Solasta is consequently a **finite campaign with optional further adventures**, not a service whose value requires endless retention. Finishing happily is an acceptable outcome. New builds, co-op, expansions, community adventures and mods offer reasons to return, but no credible cohort-retention or completion dataset was found. Review hours cannot establish an average completion time or an addiction mechanism.

For the user's character-story interest, the decisive absence is a native Battle Brothers-style loop of lasting wounds, changing employability, retirement and replacement. Cosmetic scars and dramatic combat events should not be mistaken for that loop. Solasta instead asks how strong shared tactical memories can become when an authored party remains together.

## R05 — Worked situations

These are **constructed examples from documented mechanics**, except the explicitly attributed reviewer anecdote. They are not claimed playtest results.

1. **An illuminated threat.** A party meets opponents in darkness. Spending an action on light can improve subsequent attacks instead of dealing immediate damage. The interesting choice is the delayed payoff across the whole party, balanced against the enemy's next turn. The environment changes the value of an otherwise modest spell. [S01, S29]
2. **Flight creates a new vulnerability.** A caster uses flight to reach a useful position. An incoming attack threatens concentration; elevation now exposes the caster to a different failure consequence. This interaction was a specific developer example when describing the value of implementing verticality early. It creates a memorable incident without requiring a bespoke cutscene for every combination. [S30]
3. **A recipe is not an upgrade yet.** The party knows a magic-weapon recipe but lacks its primed base. It can pursue the relevant faction's stock or continue exploring. The opportunity cost is which relationship or purchase receives scarce resources. Crafting becomes a planning decision when materials are distinct; merely waiting through repeated rests is less informative. [S14, S07]
4. **A successful conversation does not grant every permission.** MMORPG's Robin Baird reported negotiating past Aksha, then provoking a fight by taking recipes from a shelf. The reviewer enjoyed discovering that boundary without a conspicuous theft warning. Another player could read the same event as inadequately signaled ownership. The useful question is what evidence makes an NPC's response understandable before the costly action. [S11]

## R06 — People, factions, AI and cooperation

The original party's background quests give selected biographies moments in the campaign. They are authored content unlocked by a starting choice, not dynamically generated responses to arbitrary personal history. The Spring Update added quests for the eight backgrounds then available; the later free backgrounds explicitly did not all receive equivalent quests. [S06, S19]

Enemy behavior is part of the tactical texture. Reviewers describe opponents using mobility, attacking exposed targets and forcing adaptation. [S10, S12] This should not be conflated with a general NPC motive simulation. The developer itself warned that combat difficulty ratings do not transfer cleanly from tabletop because predictable AI does not improvise like a human game master. [S31]

Online co-op added a second interpretation of the same four-character party: several people can share responsibility for tactics. The 2022 beta documentation permitted ordinary saves to move between solo and multiplayer and synchronized available DLC with the host. It also documented desynchronization recovery through saving/reloading, and distinguished multiplayer-compatible custom campaigns from standalone custom dungeons. These are versioned implementation facts, not a claim that every modern session desynchronizes or that unofficial mods are supported. [S32]

For OpenLegend, co-op illustrates a possible source of remembered stories outside authored dialogue: another human's risky plan, rescue or mistake. Whether an autonomous character should form a lasting belief about that incident remains a separate design question owned by the character specification.

## R07 — Presentation, clarity and feel

Solasta's environment can communicate an actionable tactical situation while its close-up characters communicate less convincingly. RPGFan praised the interface, spell effects and environmental spaces, but found faces and party personalities weak; navigating a flying character could be awkward. [S09] PC Gamer similarly admired the dungeon design while criticizing dialogue and performances. [S08]

The contrast matters for production priorities. A visible ledge, illuminated target and clear reaction prompt help the player reason about a choice. A poorly delivered conversation can reduce belief in the people making it. Solasta's reception suggests these forms of clarity and attachment are related but independently fallible; excellent combat feedback does not automatically produce compelling social characters.

The sound response was also more nuanced than a single production-quality verdict. RPGFan found the orchestral style familiar but appreciated quieter passages; selected Steam testimony strongly praised music while accepting weaker voice acting. [S09, S13] These are listener assessments. No claim is made that soundtrack spending caused sales or that a larger voice budget would have repaid itself.

## R08 — Lore, institutions and how the player encounters them

### Historical structure — spoilers

The official historical account describes a native world of elves, dwarves and halflings. The high elves built the Manacalon Empire through military power, arcane institutions and coerced labor; sylvan elves resisted, and the northern Snow Alliance checked imperial expansion. The Rift then brought human refugees and their gods from Tirmar, pursued by an older threat. Closing the Rift caused the Cataclysm, breaking the empire and deforming its heartland into the Badlands. A millennium later, magic and settlement have recovered unevenly. The official public overview preserves uncertainty about the Rift's original cause rather than resolving it into a fully explained creation myth. [S15, S16]

This history produces a concrete present-day political problem. The Copperhead Road gives the Principality of Masgarth favorable access to ancient treasures. Neighboring powers fear an advantage, so Caer Cyflen hosts the Legacy Council, overseen by priests of Einar, the god of justice. Competing scholarly and magical institutions seek old knowledge under an arrangement intended to preserve peace. The missing garrison at Caer Lem turns an archaeological commission into the main adventure. [S16]

### Delivered setting and its limits

The player encounters the past through ruins, recovered objects, institutional representatives and dialogue. Relics have economic and relationship value as well as exposition value. RPGFan found incidental stories, including remains and an ancient elf encountered during travel through the setting, more engaging than portions of the central save-the-world plot. [S09] That supports a specific hypothesis: local evidence of somebody's life can carry more emotional weight than a large historical summary. It does not prove every background object is simulated or individually authored.

The expansions examine different consequences of this history. Lost Valley's Dominion preserves old Manacalon arrangements under Orenetis, making faction alignment part of escaping isolation. Palace of Ice moves to the Snow Alliance as Gallivan's aggression and continued Sorak activity enlarge the conflict. These are distinct campaign premises and institutions, not a single endlessly updating political simulation. [S18, S17]

The setting has substantial written history, but much daily behavior remains quest-driven. The reviewed evidence does not establish autonomous household economies, changing religious conviction, generational succession or memories privately interpreted by each NPC. Those would require additional systems rather than more lore paragraphs.

## R09 — Origin, production and documented iteration

Mathieu Girard's 2019 account describes beginning with an approximately nine-month playable prototype before building out the team. The Ruins of Telema demo then exercised the production pipeline: spaces, vertical encounters, tutorials, performance, voices and presentation. Early AI was replaced after its limitations became clear through actual play. The account is valuable because it links technical change to a playable deficiency rather than presenting an elaborate architecture as the goal. [S04]

His rules-adaptation article describes Unity, editable rule assets and a coded core. Reusable parameters could express many spells, with new effects requiring further engineering. Lighting and three-dimensional traversal were expensive foundations to plan early. This is a developer's account of internal authoring, not an assertion that ordinary players receive that complete internal editor or the game's source code. [S30]

The March 2021 balance discussion is unusually diagnostic. Players reached early Sorak fights after spending resources long before the encounter; a reload immediately before the battle did not repair that earlier decision. High enemy defenses and low health also produced swingy experiences: some players repeatedly missed, while lucky players saw an easy fight. The developer adjusted encounter composition and enemy roles. **Lesson:** a failure can result from an earlier poorly taught commitment, even when the final encounter's controls are clear. [S31]

Postlaunch iteration also addressed labor with weak payoff. The 2023 notes shortened crafting, made consumables faster, and strengthened poisons after explaining why long crafting times, enemy saves and immunities discouraged use. Higher-rarity work still required stronger capability. These are documented attempts to make opportunity cost proportionate to value, not evidence that every player accepted the eventual balance. [S07]

By December 2024 the studio described roughly 35 people and a history of expansions, free co-op and editor support. That later headcount should not be backdated as the entire original game's development staffing. [S02]

## R10 — Positioning, distribution and discovery

The project gave potential buyers something concrete to evaluate through a public demo, Kickstarter and Early Access. The official crowdfunding summary rounds the campaign to 6,000 supporters and US$270,000; stretch goals supplied additional playable content and presentation. [S03] This established a committed initial audience, but crowdfunding participation alone cannot identify the later paying market.

The commercial proposition was legible: a comparatively focused tabletop combat adaptation with a custom four-person party. The SRD license supplied a recognizable rules foundation without the entire licensed D&D setting and subclass catalog. [S05] Strong specialization can help discovery among tactical players while narrowing appeal among players primarily seeking authored companions and dramatic dialogue; the latter is an interpretation supported by the review contrast, not a measured conversion funnel.

Pricing and updates were deliberate distribution choices. In March 2021 the developer announced moving the US price from $35 to $40 before full release because of Steam's discount timing rules. [S06] Later packages combined campaigns and class options; community adventures supplied additional discovery through Workshop subscriptions and recommendations. Neither bundle visibility nor subscription count identifies incremental paid sales attributable to those features.

## R11 — Commercial evidence and economic interpretation

### Evidence record

| Scope and period | Metric | Evidence and confidence | What it cannot establish |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 2019 Kickstarter | Approximately **US$270,000** from **6,000 supporters** | Rounded official retrospective; primary, unaudited. [S03] | Complete budget, net proceeds, or postlaunch sales. Reward delivery and fees are not itemized. |
| Original Solasta, through December 12, 2024 | **More than 1.5 million players** | Developer announcement; strong evidence for what was claimed, with no paid/access-channel breakdown. [S02] | 1.5 million paid copies, full-price purchases, or developer revenue. |
| Steam original base game, provider snapshot September 25, 2026; retrieved October 20 | Approximately **0.57 million modeled units** and **US$10.3 million modeled gross spending** | Raijin, third-party estimate; its own label is medium confidence. Treat as low-confidence order-of-magnitude evidence, not audited sales. [S20] | DLC, other stores, consoles, subscription arrangements, receipts or profit. |
| Original game's entire lifetime business | **Not estimable from available evidence** | No all-platform paid-unit split, realized prices, licensing/subscription receipts or complete cost account found. | A defensible profit figure or all-platform low/base/high range. |

Raijin describes a review-based model adjusted by genre and purchase signals, checked against sampled ownership and disclosed sales. Its revenue method applies a price-bracket discount factor to modeled units and US pricing; it is not a reconstruction of Solasta's actual transactions. Its gross measure excludes deductions for storefront share, refunds, taxes and regional-price differences. Approximately 573,000 modeled units at an implied US$18 realized-price assumption produces its roughly US$10.3 million result. That arithmetic explains the model, **not actual cash received**. No independently validated error interval was available, so a fabricated low/high band would add false confidence. [S20, S21]

Other searches found revenue calculators and developer-level totals, but these were not reliable substitutes for a game-specific account: list-price multiplication ignores sale mix, while developer totals can mix products. The reported player milestone and the Steam model differ in platform, date and definition; they are not contradictory measurements of the same quantity. Do not average them.

### What success means here

**Observed outcome:** a substantial audience, several content releases, continuing community creation and a studio that proceeded to a sequel. **Financial limit:** the original project's profit, return on capital and contribution to subsequent funding remain undisclosed. Continued operation is evidence of continuity, not a balance sheet.

The strongest economic explanation is a differentiated product serving an identifiable audience, with further value from additions to a reusable combat system. Five independent reviews repeatedly identify tactical play as the attraction even when they dislike presentation or plot. [S08–S12] That pattern is consistent with customers tolerating weaknesses outside the core promise. It does not measure how many potential buyers those weaknesses deterred.

Free co-op and creator tools plausibly increased reasons to revisit and recommend the game; DLC plausibly monetized appetite for more encounters and classes. These are **causal hypotheses**, since no controlled comparison or attributable revenue series was found. Rival explanations include genre growth, discounts and distribution reach. Without staffing schedules, outsourcing, marketing spend and deal terms, calling Solasta a commercial failure—or declaring an exceptional profit margin—would be unwarranted.

## R12 — Reviews, player testimony and enjoyment mechanisms

### Five independent substantive written reviews

| Reviewer and version | What worked | What did not; interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rick Lane, **PC Gamer**, June 2, 2021; 70/100. [S08] | Varied tactical spaces, approachable rules and useful dungeon design. | Derivative writing and inconsistent performances weaken the adventure around combat. A good tactical engine did not fully satisfy expectations for an RPG's people. |
| Abraham Kobylanski, **RPGFan**, June 16, 2021; 78/100. [S09] | Clear UI, party customization, environmental encounters and useful conveniences. | Thin central stakes, limited personalities and uneven presentation; random outcomes can make repeated attempts feel different without changing the plan. |
| Phillip Willis, **RPGamer**, November 9, 2021. [S10] | Party banter, individual tutorial flashbacks and tactical satisfaction. | Rushed later storytelling, limited options and repetitive encounter patterns. His favorable reaction to banter is a useful counterexample to dismissing it as universally disliked. |
| Robin Baird, **MMORPG**, June 14, 2021; 9.5/10. [S11] | Tutorials, configurable difficulty/rules and room to learn through mistakes. | Vertical selection and visual glitches sometimes interrupt otherwise engaging play. This much more favorable assessment emphasizes tabletop convenience and tolerance for modest presentation. |
| Jarrett Green, **PCGamesN**, updated March 3, 2022; 7/10. [S12] | Combat options, enemy behavior and reduced loot-carrying chores. | Bland hubs, cosmetic-feeling personality and constrained exploration rewards. Tactical mastery did not necessarily generate equal interest between battles. |

These reviews describe the original game's early period. Statements about absent multiplayer or limited classes cannot be carried forward after their additions. Review scores are not sales estimates and are not treated as a representative survey.

### Accessible helpful Steam sample

The top-rated English surface was inspected October 20, 2026. Selected substantive accounts include **Oak** (originally October 2020), who valued enemy combinations, light and tactical preparation while criticizing story and limited variety; **Entreri42** (May 16, 2021, Early Access), who recommended the game despite save/crafting problems; and **Krave** (May 29, 2021), who reported finishing the story in 37 hours, praised combat and music, but found little attachment to the custom party and disabled burdensome rules. These are dated accounts, not proof those bugs or launch limitations persist. **GaulGallant** (April 2022) particularly valued the feeling of a homemade tabletop adventure with friends. [S13]

The separate negative-only endpoint was inaccessible during retrieval. The accessible helpful sample nonetheless includes specific criticism inside recommendations; it should not be mislabeled a balanced random sample of positive and negative recommendations. The store snapshot showed **87% positive among 13,827 English reviews**, with **72% among 224 recent reviews**. These changing, language-specific counts establish broad recommendation sentiment, not the prevalence of each complaint or an explanation for recent changes. [S01]

### Why people continue, stop or replay

The evidence supports three competing experiences. A tactically oriented player sees repeated opportunities to improve preparation and execute combinations. A story-oriented player may exhaust interest in the central cast long before combat ends. A rules enthusiast may enjoy logistics that another player disables to preserve pacing. Confidence is high that these preferences exist in the sample, moderate that they explain the observed review divide, and low concerning their market shares.

The same distinction appears in community campaigns. Ruins of Ilthismar comments include a player leaving because encounters punished preferred party compositions, alongside players reporting enjoyable completion with unusual parties and repeat co-op runs. [S28] Difficult encounters can reward mastery or impose unwanted build conformity. Selected comments cannot establish which reading is more common.

## R13 — OpenLegend hypotheses and limits

These hypotheses relate to [gameplay priorities](../../../../docs/repertoires/gameplay-priorities.md) and the [compelling characters specification](../../../../docs/projects/compelling-characters-feature-spec.md). They do not amend those owners.

| Candidate lesson | Why it matters | Tradeoff or test |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Give a capability several contextual uses. | Light, movement and protection can change a situation rather than simply raise damage. | Test whether a player can recognize the relevant opportunity before paying its cost; concealed interactions alone are not understandable agency. |
| Separate the event, its remembered meaning and a persistent change. | A rescue can be narratively memorable without the game changing anyone's personality. | OpenLegend would need evidence for who experienced or learned about the event and how that person's interpretation affects later choices. Do not infer psychological change from generic banter. |
| Preserve consequences that generate a new playable situation. | Solasta's reload-oriented death highlights the alternative to continuing with a changed survivor or roster. | Persistent injury is only useful if it creates meaningful adaptation; otherwise it can become a permanent numerical punishment. Solasta itself does not demonstrate that solution. |
| Remove low-value transport while preserving decisions about goods. | Scavengers keep acquisition connected to adventure without requiring every junk trip. | Determine which ownership, scarcity or travel decisions would be lost if collection were automated. |
| Teach costly commitments before the failure they cause. | The Sorak balance account identifies resource decisions made well before the apparent difficulty spike. | A clear final failure message cannot repair an earlier missing lesson. Test the complete expedition, including recovery opportunities. |
| Let authored scenarios and systemic rules do different jobs. | Reusable combat rules allow new environments to create fresh problems; campaigns supply situation and meaning. | More permutations do not replace credible people, a coherent setting or sufficient encounter testing. |
| Budget for emotional presentation as well as tactical explanation. | Several reviewers understood and enjoyed combat while caring little about the party. | Decide what kind of attachment is essential; do not assume full voice acting or extra biography automatically creates it. |

The commercial lesson is similarly bounded: identify the promise a specific audience will value, then investigate the cost of fulfilling it. Solasta supports studying tactical differentiation and reuse. It does not establish that every RPG should pursue its license, camera, content scale or DLC structure.

## R15 — Modding and player creation

### Applicability: yes, for the original PC ecosystem

The October 2026 Workshop surface listed **443 UserCampaign** and **345 UserLocation** entries. Those categories are not unique authors or active players. More persuasive adoption evidence comes from sustained named works: **Morrows Deep** by Artyoan had approximately **14,260 current subscribers**, and **Ruins of Ilthismar** by Silverquick approximately **30,846**. Both had multi-year publication/update histories and continuing discussion. The maintained Unfinished Business code project and community discussion provide an independent form of authorship. Together these support the gate without treating any universal download threshold as decisive. [S22, S24, S26–S28]

### Capabilities and responsibility

| Route | Tool/platform supplies | Author must supply; boundary |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Play and party construction | Combat rules, existing classes, gear, scenes and campaign UI. | A player chooses a build and tactics; this alone is not authoring a new ruleset. |
| Native Dungeon Maker | Rooms, visual themes, props, monsters, interactable gadgets, location links, playtest and publishing facilities. | Layout, encounters, authored text, rewards, pacing and verification. Ordinary authoring uses supported data/configuration, not arbitrary scripts with the game's full source. [S23] |
| Expanded campaign tools | Official updates add facilities such as world-map links, travel encounters and controllable NPC followers. | Meaningful quests, balanced follower use and a coherent campaign remain authored work. Owning the relevant content can be a dependency. [S17] |
| Unfinished Business | Community-maintained optional classes, feats, multiclassing, recipes, UI and rule changes; Unity Mod Manager installation. | Contributors maintain executable extensions, test compatibility and handle their bugs. It is not an official general-purpose scripting SDK. [S24, S25] |

**Constructed creation-to-play example, not personally executed:** begin with an author-owned Dungeon Maker location; select an environment and visual mood, arrange rooms and props, then place an entrance, exit, opponents and an interactable objective. Use the playtest facility to check arrival, traversal, combat and exit. Import the location into a new campaign, mark its start, save, add a matching thumbnail and publish through the supported Workshop flow. Another player subscribes and starts that campaign. Revisions require retesting and a versioned update; a new author can study the configuration where permissions allow. The official manual documents the workflow, including converting a standalone dungeon to a campaign for multiplayer. [S23]

This is reusable scenario creation, not evidence of importing arbitrary animation, audio or rendering pipelines through the native editor. AI assistance could help draft text or propose encounters, but does not remove the need to verify reachable geometry, solvable objectives, reward balance and dependencies. No executed AI authoring pipeline was investigated.

### Representative creations, incentives and failure cases

**Morrows Deep** is a complete fan campaign, originally published January 2023 and updated November 2024. Its author describes a roughly 35-hour, level 1–15 adventure with optional content, dialogue skills, custom equipment and crafting, requiring Lost Valley and Inner Strength. It continues some Forsaken Isle themes while using a new party. The listing documents a multiplayer ending-transition workaround and a separate six-person variant. These are author claims and support notes, not our measured completion time. [S27]

**Ruins of Ilthismar** demonstrates a second author's durable adventure and a different reception profile. Its listing dates to November 2021, with an August 2024 update, Lost Valley dependency and ongoing reports of difficult final encounters. The unusually composed party that one commenter enjoyed is not proof that all builds are equally supported. [S28]

**Unfinished Business** is a wider rules/content collection. The currently accessible EnderWiggin repository credits a community collection and lists itself as a fork; its inherited commit count should not be attributed to one maintainer's work. Its README asks contributors to branch, test and submit changes for review, describing maintenance as a hobby. Issue reports require game/mod versions, reproducible steps and supporting saves/logs. [S24] This documents a support process and creative community effort, not commercial employment or known creator income.

Compatibility is a real part of authoring. The installation wiki, edited December 12, 2025, requires a bounded Unity Mod Manager version range and warns that incompatible versions can damage saves. Windows and macOS use different procedures. [S25] A 2026 community thread reports the old repository disappearing and directs users to the successor; several people describe installation trouble, while others report success and value the expanded replay options. That corroborates continuity and friction without establishing a verified reason for every outage. [S26]

Workshop and alternative community hosting provide discovery, comments and updates. A campaign's subscription is free access to its distribution artifact, not proof that the base game or required DLC is free. No verified standard creator royalty or marketplace sales account was found. Free fan distribution also does not establish permission to reuse third-party intellectual property. Native campaign data and a locally installed executable modification have different trust boundaries; publication alone does not make either an accepted OpenLegend extension mechanism.

The transferable lesson is to make a small complete activity easy to author, test, share and revise, while keeping responsibility visible when rules are extended. The cost is documentation, version support and author/player debugging. Apply any future OpenLegend interpretation through [root policy](../../../../AGENTS.md), particularly generated-content authority and compatibility constraints.
