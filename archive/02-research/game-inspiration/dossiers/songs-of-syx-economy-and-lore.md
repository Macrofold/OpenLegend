# Songs of Syx — economy, mythology and the meaning of rebuilding

**G149 scoped supplement · October 13, 2026.** [Full dossier](songs-of-syx.md) · [Roster](../research-roster.md) · [Expanded requirements](../research-economics-engagement-and-creation.md) · [Scope and verification](../research-progress-expansion-2026-10-13.md).

Mike asked whether the existing study recorded the economy and deep lore. **The economy already had substantial operational coverage; the lore had only a limited outline.** This supplement deepens the setting and makes the relationship between fiction, economic decisions and player enjoyment explicit. It preserves the original dossier and its evidence history rather than replacing it.

This is research, not an OpenLegend implementation proposal. The new game shortlist remains roster-only. No game executable was run, and no new full R01–R14 review or all-store revenue model is claimed.

## 1. What is already recorded about the economy

The original dossier's R02–R04 and R05 remain the detailed owners. They cover production and processing; seasonal food supply; warehouses, hauling and local access; research and administrative work; civilian and military consumption; species-dependent needs; imports, exports and delivered trade costs; and expansion versus resilience. Named examples include grain becoming bread, ore and coal supporting metal production, and intermediate goods serving more than one downstream use.

Its important finding is that **a city-wide total is not the same thing as usable provision**. Enough food somewhere does not necessarily feed the district that needs it. A profitable-looking workshop can be limited by an upstream resource, travel, staffing or another institution competing for the same goods. These are existing researched mechanics and examples, not newly measured outcomes in this supplement. [Original economy and cases](songs-of-syx.md#r04--progression-economy-time-and-recovery).

### A more useful way to read that economic research

| Question | What the original study already supplies | Interpretive value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Where does value come from? | Gathering, agriculture, extraction, processing, trade and regional support | Different locations and populations can make different production plans sensible. |
| Where does it go? | Consumption, construction, services, upkeep, research, military commitments and loss | An expansion is a continuing claim on the rest of the settlement, not just a construction price. |
| Who can actually use it? | Storage assignment, transport, proximity, service access and labor | Ownership and access should be studied separately. |
| What competes for the same input? | Named crafting and institutional chains | A local improvement can weaken another useful activity. |
| What does wealth let the player attempt? | More provision, specialization, grandeur, influence or military reach | Economic progression can change ambitions, not merely increase a balance. |
| What can go wrong? | Seasonal gaps, broken supply, unrest, disease and military losses | The meaningful question is whether the cause and recovery are understandable. |

This table is a reading framework for the existing evidence, not a new simulation formula. Exact V71 balance values belong to the versioned sources in the main dossier; this supplement does not replace them with old wiki numbers.

**Three meanings of economic success also differ.** A prosperous fictional city is not evidence of a satisfied player, and either can coexist with an unsuccessful real-world business. The main dossier's R11 records dated crowdfunding and early sales disclosures while explicitly lacking a verified current lifetime revenue or profit total. The new requirements now demand a defensible estimate where possible and an economic-outcome explanation; creating that requirement does not retroactively produce a lifetime estimate for every archived game.

## 2. Lore evidence: established text, belief and older promotional material

The most useful accessible material was the developer-hosted community wiki's lore and transcribed in-game descriptions, plus a newer community wiki version of one species description. **Hosting on the developer's domain does not make every wiki sentence developer-authored or current.** Direct access to several wiki pages failed; substantive indexed passages were inspected. Their page titles, source types and age are recorded below.

The older general game page mixes setting text with pre-release promises and obsolete release information. Its narrative is useful historical worldbuilding evidence; its claims about generated stories, heroes or future systems are not accepted here as proof of delivered V71 gameplay. Similarly, a species page's centuries-long fictional lifespan should not be substituted for a current numerical survival or reproduction rule.

The following account is therefore a **source-qualified lore map**, not an assertion that the game has a complete, internally consistent public canon bible. It contains setting spoilers but not a verified walkthrough of an authored campaign ending.

## 3. The broad history: creation, divine conflict and a world after catastrophe

The legacy setting account describes an earlier multispecies empire watched over by the Gods of Order. Connected cities and roads made a shared civilization possible. Humans later freed an imprisoned chaos god in exchange for promised immortality. War destroyed cities and accumulated knowledge; the chaos side raised the dead and used destructive magical weapons. After the Order gods' physical manifestations were defeated, the chaos god's followers killed him for failing to deliver his promise. An age of ice followed. The player's era begins after that ice recedes. [L01]

The individual deity descriptions also refer to a **First War** and a **Second War of the Gods**. They do not provide enough consistent dates and connecting narrative to construct a precise calendar here. Treat the numbered wars and the broad catastrophe story as related source traditions without inventing missing chronology. [L02]

**Interpretation:** this framing can make building a settlement feel like recovering civilization rather than growing an abstract scoreboard. Ruin and lost knowledge give ordinary construction a historical horizon. Whether players actually encounter enough of that framing to feel it is an empirical question; the existence of a lore paragraph does not prove that the game communicates it effectively.

## 4. Gods, enemies and unresolved relationships

| Figure | What the accessible lore says | Important qualification |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Athuri** | Leader among the Astari; victorious over his brother Bardok in two wars. | The inspected passages do not fully explain Bardok's relationship to every other divine name. |
| **Aminion** | The fallen chaos power opposed to Athuri; connected to destructive creations and conflict with other faiths. | Some pages use Aminon. The spelling variation is not evidence of another deity. |
| **Crator** | A creator associated with living things, later coerced into making creatures for chaos. | His mythology contains both creative and compromised aspects, not simply a generic farming bonus. |
| **Shmalor / Shamalor** | Queen associated with the Argonosh and the First War; killed by the Tilapi chieftain Trinduri, with continued influence presented as uncertain. | Preserve the source's uncertainty rather than declaring a proven resurrection. |

[L02], [L03]

The text's relationships are more useful than the names alone: creation can be corrupted; divine relatives can be enemies; a dead queen can remain an object of belief; a species' enemy may be another population's sacred figure. **Do not silently equate Bardok with Aminion**, or resolve the Astari/Istari and Shmalor/Shamalor wording into a newly invented definitive theology. Those connections require stronger source evidence than was recovered.

Religion also has material expression. The older temple descriptions associate Athuri with food offerings, Crator with livestock, and Aminion and Shmalor with human sacrifice. They describe religious opposition and costly temple construction. These establish a documented link between belief and resource or institutional choices; the old unlock costs and component quantities are not certified as current V71 values. The same page says “three” while listing four temples, reinforcing the need to avoid copying its counts mechanically. [L03]

## 5. Peoples, origins and the everyday world

### Dondorians: origin as a mystery, not ordinary birth

Their transcribed description says they emerge fully grown near **Mount Cerebus**, with divine creation offered as a belief rather than a witnessed event. Rumors of flashes in the forest and fragmented memories of the divine wars leave room for uncertainty. Their crafting, mining and stubbornness are connected to this inherited identity, and older descriptions associate them with conflict against Amevias. The intriguing element is not merely longevity: it is a people with unusual continuity but imperfect access to their own beginnings. [L04]

That offers a useful contrast for research into characters: long life does not necessarily imply complete historical knowledge. It does not establish that vanilla Dondorian characters dynamically investigate or converse about their origins.

### Cantors: living remnants of a lost order

The transcribed Cantor account presents them as rare giants who protected divine beings and survived the final war, afterward withdrawing north. Their dislike of humans gives the ancient catastrophe a present social echo. Rarity and exceptional usefulness make their presence consequential rather than simply adding another common workforce type. This is historical descriptive evidence; no exact current recruitment formula, lifespan or damage value is imported. [L05]

The main dossier's attributed PC Gamer story—protecting rare Cantors until an unintended privileged group emerged—is especially relevant here. That is inherited, dated player evidence, not a new claim that every campaign creates the same social structure. Lore can change how a player values a person even when the mechanical decision begins with protecting a useful resident.

### Tilapi: stewardship without universal benevolence

The Tilapi description places their origins in guarding a great barrier forest and associates them with both care for living nature and territorial violence. That combination complicates the easy assumption that an affinity for animals and forests implies kindness toward every other population. The newer community transcription includes a fuller account of the boundary they guarded; neither source is used here to certify current numerical preferences. [L06]

### Garthimi: war-made creatures with their own continuity

Their transcribed origin connects them to the chaos power's First War and to a later retreat into mountain caverns. Hatchery reproduction and a protective exoskeleton give the account a different bodily and social texture from ordinary human households. This is a fictional species description, not a moral judgment about all individuals or a reason another game's engine should hard-code a whole population's destiny. [L07]

### Argonosh: diminished descendants of an ancient war brood

The Argonosh account links their ancestry to Shmalor's forces. It describes ancient forms as much greater than their current descendants and a withdrawal after defeat. That provides a sense of historical diminishment rather than assuming the present population is identical to the mythic army. The retrieved long description is visibly incomplete in places, so it does not support filling in the missing battle narrative. [L08]

### Amevias, Humans and Cretonians: do not manufacture equal amounts of backstory

Amevias have a strongly aquatic identity in the accessible description, while the Dondorian text supplies a relationship of antagonism. The Amevia page's introductory claim about intolerance conflicts with the tone of its quoted short description. That inconsistency is retained; this supplement does not resolve it through a guessed universal personality. [L09]

Humans' part in the setting's catastrophe is covered above. Cretonians' agricultural and nature-oriented place is already described in the main dossier. No comparably detailed, reliable new origin narrative for every group was recovered in this focused pass. A symmetrical table would look tidy but should not be achieved by inventing mythology.

## 6. How lore can change the meaning of an economic decision

The main dossier already explains production, faith, population and institutions as mechanics. The added lore makes several **interpretive connections** worth examining:

**An offering is also an opportunity cost.** Supporting a faith can require goods that have other uses. A religious building therefore connects belief to farming, trade, staffing and the ruler's priorities. The question is not merely which temple increases a value most efficiently, but what sort of settlement that allocation expresses. Historical temple texts establish the offering distinction; the exact present cost must come from the current game. [L03]

**A rare resident can carry historical meaning.** A population described as surviving an ancient catastrophe may feel less replaceable than its productivity number suggests. The main dossier's Cantor play account supports one such reaction, not a universal emotional response.

**A place can be a project and an inheritance.** Mount Cerebus gives Dondorian identity a particular place, while uncertain origins and memory prevent that place from being only a resource deposit. A future study should distinguish discovering this through actual play from reading it outside the game. [L04]

**A mixed settlement brings more than complementary labor.** Different fictional histories and religious associations can make cooperation or conflict meaningful beyond production optimization. Conversely, rigid species-wide prejudices can make every campaign feel predetermined. The useful lesson is to examine what gives characters and cultures texture without treating stereotype tables as a sufficient model of personhood.

### Constructed case: a new temple during a food shortage

A ruler wants to support a population's religious life while provisions are tight. Using the historically documented offering distinctions, imagine choosing between supporting a food-consuming institution now, changing the supply plan, or delaying construction. The relevant chain is **belief → institutional choice → demand on the economy → effect on other needs → a new political decision**. This is a constructed illustration, not a tested current-build strategy or a claim that different religions are mechanically interchangeable. Its value is showing how lore might give an ordinary resource allocation a second meaning. [L03]

### Constructed case: protecting an irreplaceable-seeming resident

Imagine a player learning the story of a rare surviving people and then declining to expose one of those residents to a risky task, even where it might be numerically efficient. That choice could change who works, who fights and whom the settlement protects. It is an interpretation inspired by the main dossier's existing Cantor account, not an additional observed playthrough. A counterexample matters: a player who never encounters or values the lore may make the same decision solely because the resident is costly to replace.

## 7. Why this may be enjoyable—and why it may not

The original reception section already records a useful disagreement: some players enjoy diagnosing a deeply connected economy, while others find the maintenance, learning burden or military/institutional obligations exhausting. The same dependency can create an interesting decision or an opaque recurring chore. The additional mythology does not settle that disagreement.

A fresh PROC3SS critical essay describes the settlement's history as emerging through trade, production and political choices, while suggesting that the peoples' histories could be explored more deeply. This is a critic's interpretation, not evidence that the game already delivers every implied narrative possibility. It usefully separates having a flavorful setting from communicating that setting richly in play. [L10]

**Research interpretation:** several possible sources of appeal reinforce each other: watching a place grow, understanding a system that once seemed mysterious, making a consequential allocation, and assigning meaning to the people who live with its consequences. Their counterweights are repetition, unclear feedback, an economy that never provides relief, and lore that is too remote from what the player actually does.

Calling the game addictive would not explain which of those is operating. A player returning to finish a district, solve a shortage, protect a valued population or try a different society has four different motivations. A future enjoyment audit should seek those specific accounts and their opposites. None of the present evidence establishes a causal retention uplift from deeper lore, exact population scale or a particular production chain.

## 8. Transferable research lessons, not adopted OpenLegend rules

The strongest general lesson is **give history something to change in play**: the meaning of an object, the value of a relationship, the reason to protect a place, or the cost of an institution. A long encyclopedia that never affects attention or choice can be less effective than one well-communicated local history.

A second lesson is to preserve the difference between what happened, what people believe and what remains unknown. Dondorian origins and Shmalor's continued influence are interesting partly because the sources leave uncertainty. Inventing definitive answers during research would remove, rather than enrich, that texture.

For OpenLegend, a hypothetical application could be a settlement whose residents disagree about a place's history and make different choices because of that disagreement. This is not a claim that such authored content currently exists, nor an instruction to implement a new mythology or general religious simulation. Existing engine/world boundaries and player-focused priorities remain unchanged.

Do not automatically copy universal species hostility, a punitive governance system, permanent upkeep or catastrophe-scale lore. Ask whether the pattern produces a worthwhile moment for the player and meaningful individuality for the characters in the particular authored world.
