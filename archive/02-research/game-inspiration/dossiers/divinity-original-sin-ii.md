# G57 — Divinity: Original Sin II

**Status: completed research pass, September 26, 2026.** Reference research and conditional inspiration, not an approved OpenLegend design or implementation plan. [Roster](../research-roster.md) · [Progress](../research-progress.md) · [Requirements](../research-requirements.md) · [Original Sin I](divinity-original-sin.md).

## 1. Identity, scope, and the player promise

Larian's second Original Sin game is a party-based role-playing adventure with free exploration and turn-based battles. The original PC release was September 14, 2017. The central promise is to solve authored problems through a combination of character identity, conversation, objects, positioning and combat rather than by discovering one prescribed interaction. The campaign supports a party of up to four and online cooperation; the PC product also advertises Arena, Game Master mode and Workshop support. A store feature list is not proof that every port includes every authoring feature. [S01]

The fiction makes that promise personal: a world frightened of Source imprisons its users, while prospective Godwoken become entangled in a struggle over divinity. Choosing a custom protagonist or one of six authored origins changes the perspective from which the world is encountered. The official presentation deliberately pairs cooperation with potentially competing ambitions. This is an authored campaign with systemic alternatives, not a continuously simulated civilization or an infinitely generated story. [S02]

A 2018 developer interview explicitly described host-owned campaign continuity and no PlayStation/Xbox cross-play at that time. That is a dated boundary, not a fresh network test. [S03]

## 2. What the player actually does

The ordinary rhythm is **investigate a place → identify several possible goals → prepare people and equipment → choose a route or intervention → resolve its consequences → reassess the party**. That rhythm has two kinds of progress: numerical capability and better understanding of the world. The following inventory separates documented mechanics from the researcher's interpretation of their usefulness.

### 2.1 Identity, origins, tags and builds

Custom characters choose an identity; origin characters supply an authored history. Fane, Lohse, Ifan ben-Mezd, Sebille, the Red Prince and Beast are alternatives to an otherwise blank protagonist and can also be encountered as companions. Origin identity does not simply lock a character into the combat role suggested by the initial preset. Races and background tags affect interactions; a preset is a starting configuration rather than an immutable class. [S02, C05, C06]

**Interpretation:** the important combination is an authored reason to care plus mechanical latitude. These are separable freedoms. Changing a build need not erase a personal history; conversely, a long character-creation menu does not itself create a history that other people acknowledge. An adaptation should decide which facts belong to identity and which are revisable skills.

### 2.2 Attributes, learned abilities and preparation

The attribute vocabulary includes Strength, Finesse, Intelligence, Constitution, Memory and Wits. The accessible indexed reference identifies Memory with available skill slots, Constitution with vitality, and Wits with initiative/critical capability; its full page was inaccessible, so this dossier does not reproduce an exact balance table. Player discussions also show the opportunity cost between a wider prepared toolkit and offensive investment. Exact optimal allocations are contested advice, not rules. [S08, P07]

Learning a skill, meeting its requirements, preparing it and having the resources to use it are different stages. Combining disciplines supports hybrid characters, but breadth can impose a cost in memory, purchasing and turn economy. The return loop therefore contains experimentation as well as accumulation. The later respecialization opportunity reduces permanent build lock-in without making every early decision costless. [C01, C05]

**Interpretation:** a useful progression system distinguishes possession of knowledge from readiness to deploy it. Otherwise collecting more abilities inevitably becomes equivalent to carrying every solution at once. Readable preparation costs can preserve meaningful alternatives without requiring arbitrary prohibitions.

### 2.3 Action points, positioning and turn-based commitment

Combat allocates a limited turn budget between movement, attacks, skills and other actions. Skills also differ in range, cooldown and resource requirements. A character can spend the turn reaching a better location rather than immediately damaging an enemy. Out-of-combat movement and party separation make preparation relevant before initiative begins. This is not the real-time-with-pause combat of the Pathfinder games, nor the first Original Sin's identical progression rules. [C02, C03, S09]

**Interpretation:** movement becomes interesting when it competes with action rather than occurring in a separate cost-free phase. However, a player needs to see the cost before committing. A high-value tactical question is not only whether a position is safe, but whether reaching it leaves enough budget to achieve the intended intervention.

### 2.4 Physical armor, magical armor and control

The two armor pools are central to the sequel's combat identity. Matching damage removes protection before ordinarily exposing vitality and associated status vulnerability. Systems designer Nick Pechenin described the change as a response to the first game's uncertain status resistance: players needed more dependable grounds for planning. Damage variation and particular abilities still prevent this from being a universal guarantee that every effect behaves identically. [S09]

This creates a target-selection problem: damage and disabling effects need a compatible opening. It also creates a reception disagreement. Vitale values the resulting tactical structure; Hancock finds repeated armor removal followed by control too formulaic. Neither response establishes that every viable party must use a single prescribed physical/magical composition. [C02, C05]

**Interpretation:** a visible gate can make a plan intelligible and simultaneously narrow its opening moves. The question for borrowing this pattern is whether the gate creates different decisions across encounters or merely forces the same preparatory tax before the interesting action becomes available.

### 2.5 Terrain, surfaces and object manipulation

Fire, water, oil and other environmental states are more than background decoration. Creating or transforming a hazard changes routes and possible attacks. Elevation and teleportation can make the arrangement of participants as important as their numerical power. A source's spectacular combination should not be expanded into a claim that every material obeys real chemistry or every surface conducts electricity. [C04, S09]

Objects can have several kinds of value: a container to search, goods to sell, something to move, or part of a tactical setup. A usable rule can cross the conventional boundary between exploration and combat. The player is encouraged to ask what an object permits, not only which quest originally introduced it.

**Interpretation:** reused verbs reduce the need to teach a new button for every puzzle. Their limitation is expectation expansion: once moving or transforming an object works somewhere, similar-looking situations invite the same attempt. Consistency and an explanation of exceptions matter as much as the number of combinations.

### 2.6 Source as power, access and replenishment

Source is a separate constraint from ordinary learned skills. Removing the collar, gaining access to stronger Source use and replenishing spent points are not the same accomplishment. Player accounts describe collecting Source, using replenishment locations and later reducing the inconvenience with teleportation-pyramid routines. Those are concrete reports of how a supposedly scarce resource can become a travel-management problem. [P04, P05]

Optional **Source Meditation** restores Source through a bedroll. Optional **From the Ashes** changes out-of-combat resurrection. These features must not be silently attributed to the original rules; Larian presented them as selectable Gift Bag changes in January 2020. [S05]

**Interpretation:** a resource is not meaningfully scarce merely because it has a small displayed capacity. If refilling is repeatable and safe, its practical price may be inconvenience. That can encourage hoarding or repetitive transport rather than a satisfying decision about when to spend power.

### 2.7 Equipment, consumables, loot and money

Weapons and armor provide combat capability; skillbooks expand the action vocabulary; potions, scrolls and grenades provide consumable interventions. Objects found through exploration can be used, retained, transferred or converted into purchasing power. Replacing equipment matters because capability changes substantially with level. Pechenin described both the intended temporary excitement of powerful finds and a post-release adjustment after players disliked how quickly their discoveries became obsolete. [S09]

**Interpretation:** this is a tension between a strong loot rhythm and attachment to particular objects. An artifact that becomes numerically irrelevant soon after its discovery can retain narrative meaning while losing practical meaning. A borrowing decision should explicitly choose whether it values frequent replacement, enduring customization or different item roles; it should not assume all three arise automatically.

### 2.8 Crafting and combining useful knowledge

Crafting connects collected ingredients to consumables and other useful outputs. Unlike Original Sin I's separate crafting/blacksmith investment, Vitale describes a recipe-and-ingredient approach and hybrid skillbook combinations. The important distinction is between knowing a recipe, owning compatible inputs and producing an action the party can actually exploit. The dossier does not invent a complete recipe list from an inaccessible wiki. [C05]

**Interpretation:** a recipe is most interesting when its result changes a possible plan rather than merely increasing sale value. Ingredient clutter is the corresponding cost. A design can expose promising combinations or remember discovered recipes without turning every miscellaneous object into compulsory inventory homework.

### 2.9 Sneaking, theft, persuasion and civil specialization

Conversation and noncombat capabilities offer alternatives to fighting. Theft depends on positioning and detection; witnesses, searches and attitude consequences make it different from unowned looting. A detailed Steam discussion describes players separating a thief from the group, respecting value/weight limits and dealing with the aftermath. These are player-tested tactics as reported in 2020, not a new reproduction or a promise that every NPC interaction permits them. [P06]

Persuasion, trading and information-oriented abilities create reasons for party specialization. However, who actually starts a conversation matters. A player may experience social specialization as useful role ownership or as frustrating inventory/speaker switching. Hancock's criticism identifies that interface boundary rather than proving that specialization itself is undesirable. [C02]

**Interpretation:** social skills need a clear rule for whose competence is being tested. Automatically borrowing the best party value removes friction but weakens individual roles; always testing the nearest character preserves roles but can punish an accidental click. Both are design choices, not inevitable consequences of having party members.

### 2.10 Animals, spirits and information access

Animal conversation and interaction with the dead broaden the set of possible informants. These are information-changing capabilities, not simply combat spells with different visual effects. They make revisiting an apparently understood location potentially worthwhile. The official product advertises these unusual conversational opportunities; the optional Spirit Vision change later alters how persistently one of those information layers is available. [S01, S05]

**Interpretation:** a new perception capability can renew old places without adding another map. The cost is missing-content anxiety: players may feel obliged to keep the capability continuously active or repeat every conversation. The useful research question is how to make a discovered perspective rewarding without making ordinary perception feel deliberately incomplete.

### 2.11 Death, recovery and difficulty

Death can consume recovery resources or lead to reloading; the chosen difficulty changes the severity and tolerance for mistakes. Honour-style play makes failure persistence a different commitment from ordinary save/reload experimentation. Definitive Edition's Story option supplies a route for players who value the narrative without wanting the same encounter burden. Hashimoto found the easier mode especially valuable when repeated PS4 loading made retries costly. [C02, C06]

**Interpretation:** failure cost includes the time before another meaningful decision, not merely lost hit points. A recovery item, a reload and a campaign-ending wipe all answer different audience needs. They should not be described as interchangeable ways of increasing challenge.

### 2.12 Progression across places and sessions

The opening imprisonment emphasizes escape and assembling basic competence. Later play expands build options, Source access and interlocking investigations; the closing regions bring accumulated identities and decisions into conflict. Fort Joy, Driftwood/Reaper's Coast, the Lady Vengeance and Arx provide recognizable changes in purpose rather than an endlessly repeatable leveling circuit. [C01, C03, S03]

There is no native colony-building or kingdom-management layer to insert into this inventory. The campaign's return value instead comes from different origins, party compositions, quest outcomes, difficulty choices, cooperation and optional authored/community experiences. Arena is a separate combat activity, not an MMO endgame economy. [S01, C06]

**Interpretation:** a finite campaign can support replay by making choices exclude other experiences. It need not promise endless progression. That approach depends on accepting that one run is not a completionist acquisition of everything the game contains.

## 3. People, party independence and authored narrative

### Individuality without autonomous-life claims

The companions have personal histories and agendas, and conversations can develop relationships and romance. Playing an origin supplies a different perspective from recruiting that person. Hashimoto's account of playing Ifan and developing a relationship with the Red Prince is useful evidence of a specific experience, not proof that every pairing has identical narrative treatment. The four-person limit creates a choice about which histories receive attention in one campaign. [C06]

Do not mistake reactive dialogue and quest state for a general-purpose psychological simulation. This research found no basis to claim that ordinary NPCs independently plan full lives, that companion affection is an unrestricted model of emotion, or that the world runs a complete offscreen economy. Their value can instead come from authored specificity, contextual response and consequences that the player remembers.

### Co-op freedom has an owner and a cost

Players can separate rather than moving as one indivisible unit. The developer describes dynamic split-screen for nearby/distant couch participants and a host-owned campaign file. That protects spatial independence but does not give every player an independently exportable continuation of the shared story. [S03]

**Interpretation:** shared presence, shared information and shared preferences are three different things. A party may occupy the same world while one participant is reading, another is optimizing loot and a third is initiating combat. More independence can produce discovery and comic disruption, but also deprive someone of a conversation or decision. Explicit group norms and legible consequence ownership are part of the experience, even when they live outside the formal rules.

### Spoiler-light narrative structure

The Source persecution and Godwoken premise put identity under institutional pressure. Origin stories give the larger struggle personal stakes; smaller conversations make the world more than a collection of fights. Brown's reported encounter with a proud mother after he had killed her son demonstrates retrospective significance: the social meaning of an earlier action changed after the mechanical outcome was settled. [S02, C01]

The design is neither wholly scripted nor wholly emergent. Authored people and situations supply meaning; interacting rules allow an unusual route through them. A quest closing unexpectedly can consequently feel like legitimate consequence or like a broken story, depending on what the player understood beforehand. The distinction cannot be resolved by counting the number of possible quest branches.

## 4. Ten concrete interaction studies

**Method:** cases 1–8 are constructed illustrations of documented mechanics, not claims that this researcher played them. Cases 9–10 are explicitly attributed player accounts. Each includes a limitation rather than presenting a universal exploit.

### 1. Break the protection that matters

**Intention:** stop an enemy before its next dangerous action. **Conditions:** different armor pools remain, and the party has a suitable disabling effect. **Action:** inspect the target and coordinate damage with the intended control. **Interaction:** reducing the relevant protection can create an opening that unrelated damage does not. **Result:** the party may prevent an attack instead of merely subtracting vitality. **Next decision:** commit another character to the same plan or protect a threatened ally. **Limit:** the plan still depends on ability-specific rules, immunity, damage variation and timing. [S09]

### 2. Relocate rather than chase

**Intention:** deal with a distant threat without spending the whole turn moving. **Conditions:** a teleportation capability, a legal destination and a useful follow-up. **Action:** move the threat into a more favorable arrangement. **Interaction:** a spatial change can alter several later characters' options. **Result:** the tool bought access, not simply damage. **Next decision:** exploit the new position or use the remaining budget elsewhere. **Limit:** Pechenin describes encounters with Fortify-protected enemies designed to defeat habitual teleportation solutions. [S09]

### 3. Make terrain, then account for allies

**Intention:** restrict an enemy route. **Conditions:** a manipulable surface and a compatible effect. **Action:** create or transform the hazard before advancing. **Interaction:** route planning and offensive effects now share the same space. **Result:** the enemy may face damage or a worse approach, but the party inherits that altered battlefield too. **Next decision:** cross, wait, reposition or change the surface again. **Limit:** treating every fight as another carpet of hazards can become repetitive and hard to read. [C04, P02]

### 4. Spend power instead of saving it forever

**Intention:** use a strong Source intervention now. **Conditions:** access to Source and a known later replenishment route. **Action:** spend a point, then decide whether returning to refill is worth the interruption. **Interaction:** replenishment logistics change the practical price of power. **Result:** a supposedly rare ability becomes available more often than a hoarding player assumed. **Next decision:** accept the travel routine or enable an optional convenience rule. **Limit:** ordinary bedrolls and Source Meditation are different rule sets. [P04, P05, S05]

### 5. A thief changes the group's risk

**Intention:** acquire something without paying the asking price. **Conditions:** a suitable thief, access, available capacity and an opportunity outside observation. **Action:** steal and withdraw. **Interaction:** the theft can trigger searches or hostility; the rest of the party is not automatically insulated from consequences. **Result:** the apparent saving may create social and travel costs. **Next decision:** leave, face inspection or repair the relationship. **Limit:** a reported tactic is not a guarantee against every witness or scripted response. [P06]

### 6. A new informant changes the route

**Intention:** understand a place without brute-force searching every object. **Conditions:** an information capability such as animal conversation. **Action:** address a previously inaccessible informant. **Interaction:** an ordinary creature becomes a source of context or a new goal. **Result:** perception and character preparation change the investigation rather than only the combat. **Next decision:** follow the lead or decide it is irrelevant. **Limit:** this is authored information access, not evidence that arbitrary animals generate unlimited coherent quests. [S01]

### 7. Rebuild without replacing the person

**Intention:** retain a companion while changing the party's tactical approach. **Conditions:** access to respecialization and the means to equip the new plan. **Action:** revise capabilities rather than restart the entire campaign. **Interaction:** personal continuity survives a mechanical reconfiguration. **Result:** experimentation becomes less expensive. **Next decision:** test the new role and adjust preparedness. **Limit:** knowing that a respec exists later does not remove the early learning burden, and changing attributes does not automatically supply missing equipment or knowledge. [C01, P01]

### 8. An equipment set becomes a discovery objective

**Intention:** obtain a qualitatively different movement or combat option. **Conditions:** a new game containing Four Relics content and access to its quest chain. **Action:** investigate and assemble an appropriate set rather than merely buying another numerical upgrade. **Interaction:** Larian describes the Vulture set connecting high-ground/ranged capability with wings when complete. **Result:** equipment becomes a story-linked objective. **Next decision:** decide who benefits and whether to alter the party's positioning strategy. **Limit:** this later addition should not be used to describe the 2017 launch campaign. [S06]

### 9. A reported corpse preparation strategy

**Attributed account:** The Dunbar Beam's June 14, 2020 helpful Steam review describes transporting a large number of corpses before using Mass Corpse Explosion against a boss. The anecdote links persistent objects, preparation and an ability whose value depends on the prepared situation. It is evidence of a memorable player story, not a reproduced benchmark or assurance that every encounter permits the same setup. The design question is whether preparation itself remains enjoyable after the surprise of the first success. [P01]

### 10. Shared quests, mismatched intentions

**Attributed account:** App Store reviewer michaelin3d, November 28, 2021, praises cooperative play while recommending ground rules: one participant can accept a quest that another subsequently resolves without the same awareness. **Interaction:** shared quest state does not imply shared understanding. **Consequence:** participation can be lost without a network failure. **Next decision:** coordinate who initiates and concludes important scenes. **Limit:** one account cannot establish how often groups encounter this problem. [P03]

## 5. Presentation, interface and feel

The visual vocabulary is colorful fantasy rather than photorealistic close-up performance. Terrain and effect layers carry tactical information, while characters and voices carry much of the interpersonal identity. That division can support a dense readable adventure, but overlapping effects and small targets can also make an intended action harder to select. Williams specifically separates tactical enjoyment from awkward clicking and launch-era technical behavior; those are usability observations, not an implementation audit. [C03]

The interface has to mediate unusually different activities: selecting an object, assessing armor, moving a party member, choosing a line of dialogue and comparing equipment. A failure at the selection layer can feel like the world rejected a sensible idea even when the underlying rules permit it. **Interpretation:** supporting many verbs is insufficient if the player cannot reliably indicate their intended target or understand the cost.

Definitive Edition's developer interview describes a rewritten, shorter journal and a party inventory view designed to reduce character-by-character management. It also describes composer Borislav Slavov's adaptive Lady Vengeance theme. These are concrete responses in the interface and audio layers, not merely more content. [S03]

The console review provides a useful counterweight to generic accessibility claims: Hashimoto found the radial controls learnable and the text readable, but reported long loads and split-screen visual problems on the tested PS4 version. Story difficulty improved access to the narrative for that reviewer; it did not remove all control or presentation friction. [C06]

**Interpretation:** audio can mark continuity while the screen is busy with tactical information. An evolving hub theme can make return visits feel connected to accumulated choices without requiring another explanatory scene. Conversely, a strong soundtrack cannot compensate for an unreadable target or unclear quest state. Presentation and systems are mutually supporting, not substitutes.

## 6. Production, iteration and distribution

### Documented production evidence

The Kickstarter ran August 26–September 30, 2015, raising **$2,032,434 in pledges from 42,713 backers against a $500,000 goal**. The campaign positioned the game as a cooperative/competitive RPG sequel. These are crowdfunding records, not the full development budget or net proceeds after fees and fulfillment. [S10]

Pechenin's February 2018 account is especially useful because it explains an actual design problem and revision: predictable status openings, the pacing of protection/vitality loss, temporary overpowered discoveries and encounter counters. He describes deliberately shaping enemy pressure rather than assuming maximally efficient focus-fire always produces the best experience. This supports a design-intent account; it does not expose proprietary AI code. [S09]

Michael Douse's July 2018 interview describes console collaboration with Bandai Namco, player feedback through preview access, and encounter revisions based on observed player approaches. It also identifies narrative and interface work rather than portraying a port as a simple platform checkbox. The interview and the June announcement give different counts for newly recorded words; this dossier preserves the existence and scope of revision without merging those counts into a false exact production total. [S03, S04]

The later free-content program and 2025 ports show that release history continued well beyond launch. That does not establish the cost of long-term maintenance, the current size of the team or a guaranteed future update schedule. [S05–S07]

### Distribution and discovery: evidence versus explanation

**Recorded:** crowdfunding and public updates, an established sequel identity, storefront distribution, console publishing collaboration, PC community-authoring surfaces, free revisions/content and later platform releases. The official press page also supplies a public press-kit route. [S01, S03, S07, S10, S12]

**Interpretation:** unusual but explainable interactions are useful word-of-mouth material because a player can tell a short causal story: an object was repurposed, a quest actor reacted, or a friend interrupted a plan. Such stories can communicate the product's freedom better than a count of skills. They are nevertheless selected highlights; they do not establish that the ordinary session avoids administration or repetition.

**Interpretation:** Game Master mode and Workshop extend possible authorship, but the mere availability of tools does not establish a successful creator economy, a measured retention contribution or a low support burden. A reusable adventure needs discovery, compatibility and a group willing to play it, not just an editor export.

## 8. Six substantive written reviews from five independent publications

These articles were read for their arguments, not counted from score snippets. Five publications supply independent editorial perspectives; two RPG Site articles are separate version assessments, not two independent organizations.

### C01 — Fraser Brown, PC Gamer, September 21, 2017

Brown values experiments that the game accommodates rather than treats as invalid inputs: teleportation, unusual builds and quest consequences. He describes conversations that retrospectively give a killed NPC a social identity. His caveats are the hard-to-parse journal, intimidating encounters, and anxiety that one quest decision might close another. His enthusiasm for origin stories and companions is not a consensus claim. His reported cooperative mischief also demonstrates that one participant's freedom can disrupt another's conversation. [C01]

### C02 — Patrick Hancock, Destructoid, October 16, 2017

Hancock praises branching side quests, earlier engagement with the main story, spoken performances and unrestricted cooperative movement. He criticizes repetitive armor-removal followed by crowd control, encounter foreknowledge, inventory transfers for trading, and conversations selecting an unsuitable party member. His suggestion that balanced damage is necessary is a reviewer interpretation, not a universal build restriction. His launch-version bug and balance observations must not be presented as a September 2026 technical audit. [C02]

### C03 — Jerry Williams, RPGFan, November 7, 2017

Williams finds investigation and tactical customization rewarding, but objects to obscure quest progression, awkward clicking and early technical/AI problems. He is less emotionally connected to the companions than Brown, despite appreciating their writing and performances. His discussion distinguishes enjoyable moment-to-moment play from the emotional effect of the larger story. Suspected causes of slowdown in his review remain his interpretation; this research did not inspect Larian's implementation. [C03]

### C04 — Daniel Cunningham, Geek to Geek Media, February 17, 2020

Cunningham emphasizes alternative escape routes, environment-sensitive combat, race reactions and companion backstories. His criticisms concern the effort of locating inventory objects and insufficiently informative quest descriptions. This later assessment is useful alongside launch criticism: improved or enduring reception cannot be inferred solely from release reviews. He also warns readers about the game's body-horror imagery. The article's then-current platform and price information is historical, not a present availability check. [C04]

### C05 — Bryan Vitale, RPG Site, September 28, 2017

Vitale welcomes the armor system's tactical implications and the crafting changes, while noting launch problems when quests or conversations encounter unusual ordering. He finds the tone more grounded without losing absurd side stories. His response to incidental voice performances and the transition music shows how relatively small presentation moments can strengthen place. This contrasts with Williams's weaker emotional attachment and Hancock's more repetitive experience of armor. [C05]

### C06 — Kazuma Hashimoto, RPG Site, August 29, 2018

Hashimoto tested PS4 Story and Classic modes and local cooperation. The account praises the accessible narrative route, origin-specific interactions, relationships and controller adaptation, but records slow loading and split-screen glitches. This is valuable version-specific evidence: an excellent underlying campaign can remain costly to use on a particular input/performance configuration. The review discloses a publisher-provided copy. [C06]

## 9. Helpful Steam sampling and contrasting player testimony

**Method:** inspected the English **Most Helpful (All Time)** page and its **Negative Only** counterpart on September 26, 2026. The visible selections are a convenience sample shaped by Steam's ranking and retrieval, not random respondents or a fresh survey. Displayed current hours were not treated as hours at publication. The initial filtered URL failed; the simpler helpful-page route succeeded, resolving the checkpoint's access gap.

**Helpful sample:** Vaporwave (August 31, 2023) supplies practical onboarding advice; The Dunbar Beam (June 14, 2020) celebrates a corpse-based setup; miltonwhatever (November 12, 2023) appreciates control and environmental interaction while preferring other aspects of BG3. Their build advice and comparisons remain personal judgments. An older Early Access review contains superseded terminology, and another overstates the number of origins; neither is used as the final rules authority. [P01]

**Negative sample:** Shogoki (February 25, 2018) feels level/gear demands undermine advertised freedom; Dog of Wisdom (December 30, 2020) dislikes armor-gated control and guide dependence; Pygmy Hippo (August 23, 2020) dislikes rapid equipment obsolescence and frustrating retries. These accounts establish genuine friction, not that every quest is literally mandatory or every encounter requires an exploit. Launcher complaints on the page are a distinct access concern rather than evidence about combat. [P02]

**Other platforms:** App Store accounts include enthusiasm for cooperative discovery alongside complaints about touch response, overheating and late-game crashes on specified historical iPads. These reports widen the usability evidence without establishing current failure rates. [P03]

**Synthesis:** the productive disagreement is between players who experience freedom as a supply of clever alternatives and players who experience it as a responsibility to optimize, search and administer. Lower difficulty, clearer information and easier experimentation address different parts of that problem; none is a universal substitute for the others.

## 10. Conditional inspiration for OpenLegend

These are research hypotheses, not tasks or commitments.

| Pattern worth studying | Dependency | Failure mode / alternative |
| --- | --- | --- |
| An object or ability works across exploration and combat | Shared, understandable interaction rules and discoverable exceptions | An impressive one-off exploit is not a reusable language. A narrower set of consistently useful verbs may be better. |
| A visible protection state creates a tactical opening | The player can predict which intervention follows from removing it | Every encounter becomes the same damage tax. Alternative: varied vulnerabilities or situational opportunities rather than a universal double gate. |
| Authored identity survives mechanical respecialization | The world responds to history independently of the current build | A person becomes a costume over interchangeable statistics. Alternative: meaningful but revisable commitments. |
| New perception makes old places newly informative | Clues are useful and the player understands the new perspective | Compulsory repeated scanning. Alternative: contextual prompts or a limited set of meaningful revisits. |
| Independent cooperative action produces shared stories | Participants understand ownership of consequential actions | One player's amusement cancels another's participation. Alternative: consent around major transitions while retaining ordinary spatial freedom. |
| Strong loot temporarily changes tactics | Temporary power is enjoyable and replacement is legible | Objects lose identity too quickly. Alternative: upgrades, specialization or enduring utility alongside numerical progression. |
| Replenishable power has a clear logistical cost | Returning to replenish creates a real choice | The optimal routine is only tedious. Alternative: transparent recovery without repetitive travel. |
| Authoring tools reuse a known play vocabulary | Published creations are discoverable, compatible and worth playing | Tool availability mistaken for a functioning creator ecosystem. Alternative: small curated scenario templates before unrestricted publishing. |

A useful study would compare ordinary sessions, not only exceptional clips: time spent investigating, preparing, fighting, reorganizing, recovering and waiting for others. The hypothesis would be that consistent cross-context actions improve agency **without** allowing information and administration costs to dominate. This dossier supplies reasons to ask that question; it does not supply a measured answer.
