# Dragon Age — series comparison, smaller games and continuity

**Required G51–G54 supplement · Research completed September 26, 2026.** This comparison connects the four independently researched mainline games and adds the separately required **Journeys, Legends, Remix 01, The Last Court and Heroes of Dragon Age**. It does not replace their parent dossiers or promote every spin-off into a new numbered roster entry. Dragon Age Keep is treated as continuity infrastructure. Current fan preservation is distinguished from the original commercial services.

## 1. Four different promises about who the player is

| Dimension | Origins | Dragon Age II | Inquisition | The Veilguard |

| Main social scale | A traveling coalition negotiating local societies and a national emergency. | A family and circle of acquaintances rooted in Kirkwall across years. | An institution spanning regions and national jurisdictions. | A focused ensemble operating from the Lighthouse against a defined crisis. |

The factual comparison is grounded in the four linked dossiers, not in an assumed linear progression from primitive to advanced design. The last row summarizes **contested reactions**, not an objective ranking. Each dossier includes five independent written-review assessments and its own platform/version boundaries.

**Interpretation:** the series repeatedly changes the answer to “what does my presence matter to?” Origins emphasizes remembered social placement; DAII emphasizes duration and relationships in a place; Inquisition emphasizes public authority; Veilguard emphasizes coordinated heroic action and a household-like ensemble. A sequel can preserve names and lore while changing that underlying player contract. Audience disagreement becomes more intelligible when these contracts are named.

A broad character creator and a deeply reactive identity are separate investments. So are a large map and a meaningful geography, a friendly cast and a convincing independent person, or an impressive capital and a responsive public. None of these pairs should be collapsed into one quality score.

## 2. Companions: disagreement, usefulness and the limits of affection

The mainline dossiers document several kinds of relationship consequence. Origins allows commitments that a gift cannot always repair. DAII lets rivalry sustain an important relationship, but does not let affection prevent every crisis. Inquisition's Iron Bull case separates personal attachment from the community whose loyalty a prior decision reinforced. Veilguard connects resolved personal problems and suitable assignments to readiness, while retaining an authored sacrifice even in a well-prepared finale.

**Interpretation:** these are different answers to a recurring problem: a companion must be useful enough to rely on, particular enough to care about, and independent enough not to feel purchased. Increasing the number of affectionate scenes only solves one part. The game also needs to communicate what the person refuses, what they know, who else they value and what they can actually do.

Combat cooperation offers a parallel history. Ordered tactics let a player specify repeatable behavior, while primer/detonator combinations make the moment of cooperation visible. Veilguard retains that joint-action opportunity but reduces the symmetry between protagonist and companions. Neither “everybody is directly controllable” nor “companions act without micromanagement” by itself establishes autonomy. An independently simulated person would additionally need bounded knowledge, goals, authority and a coherent response to interruption. The evidence reviewed does not establish a general-purpose autonomous cognition system in any of these titles.

A useful small research experiment would give a helper one explicit routine and one conflicting personal obligation. The observation to record is not whether the helper produces natural dialogue, but whether the player understands why the routine paused, what remains possible and how the relationship can change the next decision. This is a proposed experiment, not an accepted OpenLegend feature.

## 3. Continuity is a curated history, not a transferred universe

Mark Darrah's August 28, 2013 Keep announcement explains the move toward a platform-independent historical world state while Inquisition changed engine and console generation. It explicitly discusses lost/corrupt saves, reconstruction of desired history, exploration of alternatives and a validator for conflicting choices. The announcement still describes direct save-file population as under investigation; that promise must not be mistaken for a verified shipped feature. [K1](#k1)

Keep later provided customization of heroes and management of recorded playthrough states. Its 2015 update describes deleting states and handling a finite state collection; the 2016 tapestry-printing feature even made a chosen history into a physical personal artifact. These are continuity and participation surfaces, not another playable full campaign. Neither a surviving public blog nor an unauthenticated landing page proves that every account/export path functions today. No authenticated Keep test was performed. [K2](#k2) [K3](#k3)

The Veilguard uses a much narrower recreation of selected Inquisition history instead of importing a complete Keep tapestry. Its parent dossier explains the choice and the resulting expectations gap. A player may remember dozens of decisions whose exact later flags are absent. That does not logically mean the person never had agency in the earlier game; it does mean the sequel should not promise a comprehensive historical response it cannot deliver.

**Interpretation:** there are at least four distinct kinds of continuity worth preserving:

1. **Event continuity:** what actually happened in the selected history.
2. **Knowledge continuity:** who witnessed or learned it, possibly incorrectly.
3. **Interpretive continuity:** how a person or institution understands it.
4. **Presentation continuity:** which parts a later experience can afford to acknowledge.

The mainline imports primarily carry selected authored facts, not every actor's knowledge and lived simulation state. A generative world should not confuse a summary with authoritative history or assume that remembering an event makes it public knowledge. Equally, a validator can reject a contradictory branch without claiming to know the player's intended emotional interpretation.

## 4. Journeys — condense a role-playing world into positional tactics

**Identity and release boundary.** EA2D's Ethan Levy described a Flash project developed with BioWare and independent creators, beginning in late 2008. The October 2009 introduction announced a three-chapter plan and the first installment, **The Deep Roads**. This supplement credits the released first installment, not three completed campaigns. Orzammar and the darkspawn supplied recognizable context around a much smaller presentation. [J1](#j1)

**Actual play.** Up to three heroes use a hex battlefield, character statistics, talents, equipment and progression. Levy's mechanics account describes movement, attacks, spells and potions. Iteration added enemy secondary attacks, obstacles with line of sight, positional bonuses, and movement that could be corrected within the allowed radius before committing an attack. These are documented developer decisions, not a personal playtest result or proof of the promotional claim that the combat achieved perfect depth. [J2](#j2)

**Constructed causal example:** a ranged hero's target is blocked; moving within the available radius changes the firing line but also the position from which enemies can approach. The player can reconsider the move before committing the attack. The interesting decision comes from geometry and commitment timing together, not simply from having many talents. The limit is the authored hex encounter: nothing here establishes a free-form physical city or persistent NPC life outside battle.

**Interpretation:** forgiving an uncommitted input does not have to remove tactical consequence. It can remove accidental misclick punishment while preserving the meaningful decision to attack from a chosen place. Enemy secondary actions also matter because they force a response to different threats; replacing every enemy with a larger health bar would retain progression but lose much of the spatial problem.

**Distribution and role in the franchise.** The browser format and unlockable Origins rewards made it an introduction and cross-promotion surface, rather than a requirement to understand the main campaign. The 2009 reward promotion documents the cross-game incentive, not how many buyers it converted. Its historical Flash deployment is not a tested modern access route. A currently surviving page, emulator listing or rehost should not be represented as the original maintained service without verification. [J3](#j3)

The strongest portable lesson is a deliberately bounded combat prototype with recognizable stakes. It can test positional verbs, feedback and reversibility without attempting all the main game's cinematic production. Reusing the world's motifs is not evidence that those motifs alone produce strategy.

## 5. Legends — friends as party resources, a castle as preparation

**Actual game.** The 2011 browser/social RPG let a created warrior, rogue or mage fight turn-based encounters, develop abilities and prepare at a castle. The Gamezebo review describes recruiting friends' characters for limited-frequency assistance and using servants to make supplies. Those borrowed characters need not have been controlled live by friends; “social party” must not be silently translated into synchronous cooperative play. [L1](#l1)

**Reception and economic tension.** Jeremiah Leif Johnson's substantive review praises its richer combat and production relative to surrounding social games, yet reports severe progression friction when friends are unavailable. Paying can shorten waits and compensate for those limits. His enthusiasm and criticism coexist: the game can have meaningful tactical improvements and still put an unwelcome price on access to them. This is one critic's experience, not a measured average willingness to pay. [L1](#l1)

**Constructed causal example:** the player loses because the available party lacks support; a stronger friend's character will return after a timer, while castle preparation offers another route to readiness. The next choice becomes wait, prepare, recruit socially or pay. The same apparent combat failure therefore belongs partly to roster design and partly to a scheduling/business model. A helper's unavailability is not necessarily a simulated personal obligation; it can be an external engagement gate.

**Interpretation:** social interdependence can express generosity, but becomes coercive when it primarily supplies friction relief. A design should ask whether a friend joins because the experience is enjoyable together or because a countdown punishes playing alone. The castle's useful relation to an expedition is worth separating from repetitive collection and return timers.

**Distribution and end of life.** BioWare announced a Google+ version in August 2011, extending the social-platform reach. Its June 19, 2012 notice confirms that the online app closed on **June 18, 2012** and points to a downloadable continuation. Contemporary reporting distinguishes that offline version from the social features and cross-game rewards it could no longer supply. This was a changed product, not an assertion that the original online game survived unchanged. The historical download was not installed or tested in this pass. [L2](#l2) [L3](#l3) [L4](#l4)

Contemporary closure reporting attributed the decision to insufficient sustainable commercial performance. No title-level revenue or profit total was recovered. In particular, the publisher's total audience for a broader free-to-play label is not a Legends player count. [L5](#l5)

## 6. Remix 01 — change the verbs, not just the content

EA2D and independent developer **Pixelante/Evan Miller** produced a hack-and-slash reinterpretation featuring Viscount Ravi. The developer-posted Kongregate page credits the collaborators, describes point/click/drag controls and a reward connection to the Facebook game. Its **May 24, 2011** date is the date on that distribution page, not proof that no earlier distribution existed. It is a separate action game, not a patch turning all Legends battles into real-time combat. [X1](#x1)

The directly inspected Kongregate comments provide unusually clear qualitative contrast. **Jaundice**, May 24, 2011, praises the controls and compact length. **Trarek**, July 14, 2014, enjoys the game but reports a Finesse strategy that undermines difficulty. Those comments identify both accessibility and a balance failure without constituting a representative player survey. The page's existence and play button do not establish successful execution on a modern browser. [X1](#x1)

**Interpretation:** this is a stronger example of a remix than “the same game with a different skin.” The intervention point changes from choosing commands in a social RPG to embodied timing through the mouse. A compact derivative can test a different kind of pleasure with shared identity and art direction. It can also reveal that the side project is more appealing to some players than the larger product it promotes. Neither result establishes a measured acquisition funnel.

**Constructed study question:** when the player changes from clicking an ability to drawing a movement/attack gesture, which information must become immediate? Target distance, direction, enemy preparation and feedback become more urgent than an elaborate strategic menu. The assets may remain familiar while camera, animation and timing requirements change substantially. Permission given to one commissioned collaborator does not license outside researchers to reuse those assets.

## 7. The Last Court — political texture through cards and language

**Premise.** Failbetter's November 2, 2014 announcement describes an impending visit to **Serault**, an outlying Orlesian fiefdom, and a ruler played as the Huntress or Scholar. Choosing a counselor, lover, accomplice and bodyguard connects distinctive people to the work of government. The ancient Shame, Sealed Chantry and Horned Knight make a small place feel older than the current problem. This is the developer's documented premise, not a claim that the game procedurally generates religions or civic institutions. [T1](#t1)

**Actual rules.** The original browser game uses cards, skill-dependent challenges and changing realm conditions. Seven Market Days lead toward Divine Justinia's visit; Secrets, Trophies and Viands are preparation resources. The action pool replenishes over real time, and purchased Dawn can restore actions. Dignity, Freedom and Prosperity are not identical to Peril, Twilight and Rumors of Revolution. Different pressures can open opportunities as well as create danger. Completion originally unlocked specific Inquisition war-table/customization rewards; its selected story choices were not a comprehensive plot import into Inquisition. [T2](#t2)

**Constructed causal example:** a ruler wants a dignified reception but also needs a particular adviser. Obtaining that person's favor changes the available skills and consumes a resource that could have improved the reception directly. Another card presents a risky opportunity while a threat is elevated. The next decision is not simply to maximize every bar: time, dependencies and acceptable risk compete. The case illustrates the documented resource grammar, not a claim that a particular unverified card guarantees an exact ending.

**Interpretation:** named currencies and offices can make abstract state feel social. A favor owed by a particular person carries a different imagined meaning from generic points even when both have simple numerical implementations. The writing's suggestiveness also lets a small map imply a surrounding world. The danger is that sufficiently opaque prose makes the player unable to distinguish mood from actionable conditions. In a new system, flavor should enrich a rule without concealing its cost.

**Retirement and preservation.** The original service closed in **November 2020**; the community chronology records November 17, and contemporary reporting documents the closure and a screenshot-preservation effort. This is not merely cessation of new content. A record of prose and branches can preserve research access without restoring the game's original timing and interaction. [T2](#t2) [T3](#t3)

There is now a distinct fan project, **DATLC's The Last Court: A Dragon Age Adaptation**, whose inspected creator page offers browser and offline play. It explicitly changes the episodic card format into a gamebook and identifies Twine/Harlowe. The page lists a **2.9 offline package** and an **August 28, 2026** update. The creator's description, not a firsthand execution test, is the source. This is neither an EA relaunch nor an identical restoration of the original mechanics. Its assertions about rights are the creator's position, not a legal conclusion endorsed by this research. [T4](#t4)

That distinction is valuable in its own right: preserving text, preserving rules and preserving a service's social/economic context are three different projects. A faster gamebook can make the prose accessible while intentionally removing the original waiting model.

## 8. Heroes of Dragon Age — collection and preparation replace direct combat

**Identity and actual play.** Capital Games' 2013 mobile game represents characters as collectible figures. A selected five-unit force, formations, abilities and support effects determine automated battles. Its scenarios revisit or remix episodes from wider Dragon Age lore rather than supplying another customizable mainline protagonist whose entire campaign becomes imported canon. Development, platform-specific rollout and later additions should not be reduced to a single launch screenshot. [H1](#h1)

An official June 20, 2014 EA update documents additional unit-strengthening and social-code features. This matters because a launch review cannot establish the final collection economy. The community record distinguishes combining, consuming and fortifying units; similar names do not imply identical actions or that every duplicate has only one use. [H2](#h2) [H1](#h1)

**Constructed causal example:** a player repeatedly loses an automatic encounter. Rearranging or replacing units can change the result, while consuming an expendable figure improves another. The meaningful action happens before battle, so the post-fight explanation must make composition and targeting understandable. Without that explanation, losing can feel like a demand for a rarer purchase rather than a reason to form a better plan.

**Reception evidence.** The accessible Metacritic page contains direct player text, not only aggregate scores. **Necrolexicon**, November 30, 2016, values roster management and lore collection. **TheQuietGamer**, November 12, 2014, describes an enjoyable collection loop interrupted by difficulty walls and grind. Neither account establishes the statistical drop rates or that all non-paying players reach an identical wall. Eurogamer and 148Apps direct-review attempts failed; their short aggregator excerpts are not counted as full reviews read. [H3](#h3)

**Interpretation:** automation can relocate agency into preparation, but only if preparation offers legible alternatives. A beautiful battle animation is not a replacement for useful causal feedback. In a collection economy, rarity, compatibility, role and level should remain distinguishable; otherwise the system may appear strategically broad while functionally demanding a stronger draw. Likewise, reproducing a named person many times is coherent as a figurine collection but should not be represented as a literal population of identical people in a living world.

**Retirement.** A sunset notice reproduced by a community contributor on EA's forum separates three dates: new purchases ceased **October 26, 2022**; distribution/support retirement was **November 23, 2022**; the remaining in-game currency could be used until the **January 24, 2023** shutdown boundary. The community game reference corroborates the January 2023 closure. The forum contributor is not misidentified as an EA employee, and the original staff notice was not independently recovered. Do not substitute January 2024, or claim an official offline edition exists. [H4](#h4) [H1](#h1)

## 9. Franchise production and distribution lessons

The mainline dossiers preserve the separate production accounts: Origins' authored backgrounds, DAII's concentrated city/time approach, Inquisition's larger organization and engine/platform transition, and Veilguard's documented exploration of other concepts before single-player refocus. These are not all attributable to one timeless creative plan or one engine. Audience expectations accumulate while the creators' practical constraints and chosen experience change.

The smaller games reveal a parallel experimentation structure. EA2D worked with independent Flash creators; Legends explored social distribution and cross-game rewards; Remix changed the action grammar; Failbetter brought its own text/card sensibility; Capital Games organized lore around collectible figures. The world supplied a shared imaginative vocabulary, but each project selected a different player activity. The key research question is which activity can stand on its own when the cross-promotion disappears.

There are also different end-of-life outcomes: an offline conversion for Legends, closure and archival/fan adaptation for The Last Court, and a server-dependent shutdown for Heroes. A surviving reward, a surviving image archive and a playable standalone game preserve different amounts of value. None is equivalent to perpetual service continuity. The study found no defensible comparable revenue series across these smaller games, so it does not rank their profitability or infer failure merely from eventual retirement.

For OpenLegend, the strongest proposed takeaway is **portable meaning with explicit mechanical ownership**. A world can support different experiences without pretending they share identical rules. A card game's favor, an action game's detonation and a tactics game's flank can all express cooperation, but the state and interaction contract should remain clear. A shared setting is a source of coherence, not permission to blur authority, economics, perception or control.

### Coverage and preservation check

The comparison covers protagonist identity, tactical/direct control, progression and crafting, relationships, place and time, imported continuity, production, audience disagreements and business/service boundaries across all four mainline games. Each requested smaller game has its own actual player activity, progression/interaction model, distribution and continuity/end-of-life treatment. Mainline DLC remains in the parent dossiers rather than being reduced to a product list here.

This supplement preserves and links all four mainline owners; it does not overwrite the earlier packet, independent critic summaries or mechanics studies. Cross-game synthesis and constructed examples are explicitly interpretations, not claims of firsthand play. Source and internal-anchor review was textual; no automated repository-wide link crawl, executable preservation audit or service-login verification was performed. Completion of this supplement does not imply completion of the whole 130-subject roster.
