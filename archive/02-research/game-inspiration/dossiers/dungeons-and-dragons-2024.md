# Dungeons & Dragons: the 2024 rules revision / 5.5e

Research date: 2026-09-27. Scope: the revised fifth-edition tabletop game, not Baldur’s Gate 3, an actual-play show, a virtual tabletop, or every D&D supplement. This is reference research, not an accepted OpenLegend rules specification.

Navigation: [D&D research hub](../games/dungeons-and-dragons.md) · [3.5e comparison](dungeons-and-dragons-3-5.md) · [GM adjudication and simulation lessons](../mechanics/dungeons-and-dragons-adjudication.md) · [worldbuilding study](../../worldbuilding/worlds/29-dungeons-and-dragons.md).

## 1. Identity, editions, and the actual promise

The current core revision began with the 2024 Player’s Handbook and Dungeon Master’s Guide and the 2025 Monster Manual. Wizards’ current SRD page distinguishes 2014 **5e** from revised **5.5e**; its latest listed English rules reference is **SRD 5.2.1**, published May 1, 2025. “2024 D&D,” “revised fifth edition,” and “5.5e” therefore describe the studied lineage, not three separate games. “One D&D” was development-era terminology, not a second shipped ruleset to mix into the evidence. [S1–S2]

“Latest” does not establish “most played.” This research did not recover a representative census separating ongoing 2014 tables from revised-rule tables. Nor does the contrast with 3.5e imply that every earlier D&D edition was more complicated. The useful comparison is particular: modern consolidated checks, proficiency, and advantage versus 3.5e’s ranks, typed modifiers, prerequisites, and more elaborate action interactions.

The player promise is an adventurer whose choices can matter in a collaboratively imagined world. One participant, the Dungeon Master, presents situations, portrays nonplayer characters, applies rules, and resolves uncertainty; the other participants normally control their own characters. The basic loop is description → player intention → resolution → changed situation. Combat is only one of three named pillars alongside exploration and social interaction. The game explicitly permits actions not listed on the character sheet. It does **not** promise that every described action is possible or that every possibility needs dice. [S3; SRD pp. 5–18]

A first session can begin with a small location, an immediate problem, a character sheet, dice, and conversation. Established campaigns accumulate equipment, allies, obligations, discoveries, levels, and memories. The persistent artifact is partly the character sheet and partly the group’s shared account of what happened. There is no mandatory server simulation of every resident while the table is absent.

## 2. Character formation and progression

Character construction combines class, background, species, ability scores, proficiencies, equipment, and personal description. In the revised rules, background supplies ability-score increases and an Origin feat; species supplies features such as size, speed, senses, and particular capabilities. This separates some learned life history from inherited features, but a fixed background package can still constrain an unconventional concept. The rules support standard-array, point-buy, or rolled scores; these are different agreements about initial variance, not interchangeable balancing assumptions. [S4; SRD pp. 19–27, 83–88]

The six abilities are Strength, Dexterity, Constitution, Intelligence, Wisdom, and Charisma. Their modifiers, rather than scores directly, usually enter checks. Skills describe applications of abilities: Athletics, Stealth, Arcana, Investigation, Insight, Persuasion, Survival, and others. Proficiency represents training; Expertise increases its contribution. A proficient low-level specialist is not automatically incapable of failure, and an untrained character is not automatically barred from every related action. Particular activities can nevertheless require training or appropriate tools. [S3–S4]

The twelve core classes create different recurring decisions:

| Class | Character fantasy and recurring resource/decision |
|---|---|
| Barbarian | Commit to close danger; manage Rage and its interaction with attack and defense. |
| Bard | Mix spellcasting, skills, and Bardic Inspiration; decide whose uncertain action to support. |
| Cleric | Prepare divine magic; combine healing, protection, Channel Divinity, and chosen domain. |
| Druid | Balance prepared magic with Wild Shape and environmental or animal capabilities. |
| Fighter | Exploit weapons, masteries, repeated attacks, Second Wind, and Action Surge. |
| Monk | Spend Focus Points on mobility, defense, and special attacks rather than merely repeat damage. |
| Paladin | Combine martial commitment, spells, healing, auras, and oath identity. |
| Ranger | Combine weapons, tracking/exploration capabilities, spells, and target-focused choices. |
| Rogue | Position for Sneak Attack, use Cunning Action, and later trade damage for Cunning Strike effects. |
| Sorcerer | Shape a limited spell repertoire through Sorcery Points and Metamagic. |
| Warlock | Build around a patron, Pact Magic, and selected Eldritch Invocations. |
| Wizard | Expand and prepare a spellbook; trade preparation and information for broad solutions. |

These are analytical descriptions of the core class chapters, not a ranking. Subclasses now begin at class level 3 in the revised core classes. Later features and feats create build identity; multiclassing adds prerequisite and progression tradeoffs. The SRD contains one subclass per core class, not the complete commercial book’s selection. Artificer is not a thirteenth core PHB class and is outside SRD 5.2.1. [S1, S4; SRD pp. 28–88]

Leveling normally runs from 1 through 20. Advancement increases durability and unlocks qualitatively different permissions as well as numbers: extra attacks, transformative magic, improved mobility, or new ways to spend class resources. Experience-based and DM-directed advancement support different pacing choices. Early play foregrounds local risk and scarce options; middle play adds reliable specializations and wider travel; high-level magic can change which kinds of obstacle remain meaningful. There is no universal MMO-style endgame, daily login reward, or infinite equipment tier. A campaign can conclude, change scale, or retire characters. [S4; S7]

## 3. Resolution: more than rolling Charisma

For a typical ability check, roll a d20, add the relevant ability modifier and applicable proficiency and other adjustments, and compare the total with a Difficulty Class. Attacks instead target Armor Class; saving throws resist a specified threat. Typical generic DC landmarks run from 5 to 30. A natural 20 is not a universal ability-check success, and a natural 1 is not a universal ability-check failure. Attack-roll critical rules are a separate rule. [S3; SRD pp. 6–8]

Advantage rolls two d20s and keeps the higher; disadvantage keeps the lower. Multiple sources do not accumulate extra dice, and the presence of both cancels them. This is a deliberate compression of circumstances, not an accurate additive model of all physical advantages. Heroic Inspiration is a distinct immediate die reroll with a one-instance storage limit. [S3]

The DM can select a different ability for a skill when the approach warrants it: a physical display might use Strength with Intimidation proficiency. But the player cannot obtain their best statistic simply by renaming an unchanged action. A request first needs a meaningful approach and a relevant target. Searching the wrong room cannot discover a hidden object elsewhere merely because the roll is high. [S3]

The revised **Influence** action is unusually relevant to OpenLegend. The DM first decides whether the NPC is **willing, unwilling, or hesitant**. Willing characters can comply without a check, in a manner they prefer; an unwilling character can refuse without a check. Only the hesitant case calls for the prescribed uncertainty resolution. Its default DC is the higher of 15 and the target’s Intelligence score. Friendly and hostile attitudes affect the check through advantage and disadvantage. A failed attempt cannot simply be repeated in the same way immediately; the default wait is 24 hours, subject to the DM’s duration. [S5; SRD p. 184]

That is not a complete theory of persuasion. Intelligence as a default resistance target is a game abstraction; relationships, values, authority, and stakes still require judgment. A guard may agree that refugees deserve help but lack authority to open the fortress. A ruler’s friendliness does not make abdication a reasonable request. Charming someone is not automatically controlling their actions. The general lesson is **eligibility before probability**, with the actor’s actual desires and authority retained.

## 4. Action economy, movement, and combat

Combat organizes roughly six-second rounds into initiative-ordered turns. A turn supplies movement and an action; a Bonus Action is available only when a feature permits that use. A Reaction responds to a trigger and has its own refresh timing. Attack, Dash, Disengage, Dodge, Help, Hide, Influence, Magic, Ready, Search, Study, and Utilize are useful standard verbs, not an exhaustive set of fictional possibilities. Speech does not grant unlimited free persuasion, and searching, helping, and manipulating objects can compete for time. [S3; SRD pp. 9–18]

Position creates decisions about cover, reach, line of sight, opportunity attacks, difficult terrain, allies, and area effects. Movement can be divided around actions. Climbing, swimming, crawling, jumping, flying, mounts, and vehicles have different constraints. A running long jump has an explicit Strength-based baseline; the DM need not roll for every ordinary gap. A leap beyond that baseline is a different adjudication question. Forced movement, teleportation, and voluntary movement must not be treated as identical triggers. [S3, S5]

Combat offers more than reducing hit points: defend an exit, move an enemy, grapple, shove, protect a concentration caster, rescue an ally, retreat, negotiate surrender, or exploit terrain. Revised unarmed strikes can deal damage or use grapple/shove options, with their specified eligibility and saves. Conditions such as Grappled, Prone, Restrained, Frightened, Incapacitated, and Unconscious are distinct bundles; “disabled” is not one interchangeable state. [S5; SRD glossary]

Hit points are an abstract survival resource, not a detailed wound map. At zero HP, player characters usually enter the death-saving-throw process unless an exception applies; massive damage and particular effects can change that outcome. Healing can restore a fallen ally, temporary HP are not ordinary healing, and damage resistance, vulnerability, and immunity are different operations. The current nonlethal melee rule can leave a qualifying target at **1 HP and Unconscious**, rather than importing the 2014 zero-HP wording. [S3, S5]

A Short Rest supports recovery through Hit Point Dice and applicable class features. A Long Rest has time, sleep/activity, interruption, and eligibility rules; current completion restores all spent Hit Point Dice as well as HP. It is not a free reset available after every exchange. Death and resurrection depend on particular spells, bodies, souls, components, and campaign circumstances, not simply paying an arbitrary generic respawn fee. [S5; SRD pp. 17–18 and spell/glossary entries]

## 5. Items, weapons, armor, materials, and combinations

Equipment matters both as a rules entry and as an object in a situation. A ladder changes reach; rope supports a descent; a pole lets someone touch a suspect mechanism from a distance; a lantern changes what observers can see; a crowbar or tools can make a different approach possible. Inventory, carrying capacity, ownership, available hands, retrieval time, ammunition, consumables, and environmental exposure are separate constraints. Core D&D often abstracts mundane weight until it becomes important; this is not evidence of an item-by-item logistics simulation. [S3, S6]

### Weapon families and mastery

Simple versus martial proficiency, melee versus ranged use, damage type, reach, handedness, finesse, ammunition, loading, thrown range, and weight distinguish weapons. A dagger, spear, quarterstaff, greatsword, rapier, pike, whip, longbow, and crossbow are not merely ascending damage tiers. The core equipment reference also includes a pistol and musket; their inclusion does not establish availability in every setting. [S6; SRD pp. 89–92]

Weapon Mastery gives eligible characters additional tactical properties; merely owning the weapon does not unlock mastery. The eight families are Cleave, Graze, Nick, Push, Sap, Slow, Topple, and Vex. Examples: a greatsword’s Graze can matter on a miss; a quarterstaff’s Topple can force a save against becoming Prone; a pike’s Push can reposition a qualifying target; a dagger’s Nick changes the timing of the Light-property extra attack. These are constrained effects with triggers, sizes, timing, and frequency limits, not permission to invent any associated outcome. [S6; SRD pp. 90–91]

**Constructed interaction:** a fighter tries to keep a pursuer away from a wounded ally. A qualifying Push hit can move the enemy and change the next path or reach decision. It does not guarantee a fall into any nearby pit: direction, distance, intervening geometry, and the enemy’s actual position still matter. The next choice is whether to hold the passage, retreat together, or spend another resource.

Light, medium, and heavy armor trade AC calculations against Dexterity contribution, cost, weight, requirements, and sometimes stealth disadvantage. Shields occupy equipment capacity and provide their specified defense. Alternative base-AC formulas do not stack freely. Wearing equipment is not the same as being trained to use it effectively. [S3, S6]

### Acquisition, crafting, and persistence

Items can be bought, found, gifted, stolen, crafted, recovered, expended, broken, or lost. Trade goods, gems, art objects, coins, and service payments support different fictional transactions. Item rarity and an adventurer-facing price are not a fully modeled regional economy. A price list does not guarantee that the nearest village stocks every item. [S6–S7]

For ordinary revised crafting, the character needs the relevant tool proficiency and tools, raw materials costing half the item’s purchase price, and workdays based on price divided by 10 GP, rounded up; an ordinary workday is eight hours. Availability remains a DM/world question. Qualified assistance can reduce time. Thus the printed 1,500 GP plate-armor price implies 750 GP of materials and 150 solo workdays, not one successful roll during combat. A 50 GP heavy crossbow implies 25 GP and five days. These are calculations from the rule, not observed campaign economies. [S6]

Tool identity is useful rather than cosmetic: carpentry can produce wooden equipment; smithing and leatherworking support different objects; herbalism supports healing-potion brewing; calligraphy or Arcana proficiency can qualify a character for scroll work. Skill proficiency plus applicable tool proficiency can give advantage while adding proficiency only once. The healing-potion recipe requires its own kit, materials, and day of work. Scrolls require an appropriate prepared spell and specified components and production time; exact spellcasting parameters are not arbitrary. [S6]

The SRD does not provide a comprehensive metallurgy, agricultural production, spoilage, repair, and supply-chain model. Named special materials and magic-item properties must be checked in the relevant item rules. It would be a mistake to infer that an iron sword, adamantine armor, and a magically enhanced weapon are different grades on one universal material-upgrade tree.

Magic items add activation methods, attunement where required, charges, recharge conditions, curses, and sometimes personality. Attunement limits prevent unlimited stacking of selected powers. Potions and scrolls differ from permanent items; finding an item, identifying it, safely using it, and knowing all of its consequences are different events. Bags or other extraordinary storage devices have their own limits rather than eliminating all mass, space, or retrieval questions. [SRD, Magic Items, pp. 202 onward]

## 6. Magic as a vocabulary of bounded exceptions

Spell level, character level, and slot level are different quantities. Preparation determines available options; slots constrain repeated use; rituals trade time for a slot under their rules; cantrips have their own scaling. Concentration limits simultaneous maintained effects and can be broken. Verbal, somatic, and material requirements make silence, restraints, free hands, costly ingredients, and visible casting consequential. Higher-level slots do not automatically strengthen every spell. [S8; SRD, Spells]

The revised rule limits expending spell slots to cast spells to one per turn; it should not be replaced with a remembered 2014 bonus-action-spell shortcut. A reaction on another creature’s turn is a separate timing question. Effects must also satisfy range, valid-target, perception, duration, and area rules. Invalid targets need not reveal all their hidden properties to the caster; some attempts fail while still consuming resources. [S8]

A useful spell catalogue groups **functions**, not just damage:

| Function | Examples to inspect | Consequence beyond combat |
|---|---|---|
| Access and movement | Knock, Fly, Misty Step, Spider Climb, Teleport | Architecture, security, travel, escape, and privileged access. |
| Information | Detect Magic, Detect Thoughts, Speak with Dead, Scrying | Evidence gathering; strict limits on what a target knows or a spell reveals. |
| Communication | Sending, Tongues, Comprehend Languages | Distance and translation change coordination without guaranteeing trust. |
| Concealment and illusion | Invisibility, Minor Illusion, Disguise Self, Silence | Different effects on sight, sound, recognition, and physical contact. |
| Material and environmental change | Mending, Fabricate, Wall of Stone, Control Water | Repair, production, construction, transport, and hazards, with explicit scope. |
| Care and survival | Cure Wounds, Lesser Restoration, Goodberry, Revivify | Recovery and provisioning; each effect addresses particular deficits. |
| Social coercion | Charm Person, Suggestion, Dominate Person | Separate magical permissions, saves, durations, and boundaries—not ordinary persuasion. |

The names identify reference entries, not proposed OpenLegend content. Read the revised individual spell before importing a familiar interaction. For example, Knock opens or suppresses a qualifying barrier but creates an audible knock up to 300 feet away; multiple locks are not all removed at once. An illusion of a bridge is not a load-bearing bridge. A corpse’s answers do not automatically become omniscient history. These distinctions make utility magic interesting because bypassing one constraint can expose another. [SRD, individual spell entries]

## 7. Exploration, social life, quests, and management

Light, darkness, obscuration, special senses, travel pace, marching order, terrain, traps, falling, food, water, and suffocation can all matter. A slow journey can improve observation but consume time; a faster route may increase exposure or reduce opportunities to notice things. A loud, bright solution to a door problem may be worse than a slower quiet one. The level of procedural detail is a table choice: the DM can summarize uneventful travel instead of checking every mile. [S3, S5]

Stealth is not a universal untargetability switch. The revised Hide action has positioning and visibility requirements, a DC 15 Stealth check, a discovery threshold, and ending conditions. Its use of the Invisible condition while hidden should not be narrated as literal magical transparency. Searching, hearing, seeing, and identifying the source are distinct questions. An NPC does not know all player intentions merely because the DM does. [S5]

NPCs can have desires, fears, social positions, faction loyalties, routines, and relationships, but the tabletop rules do not execute an autonomous psychology engine. The DM supplies relevant behavior. Parties divide roles and negotiate shared plans and treasure. Allies, retainers, mounts, familiars, and other companions have different control and risk arrangements; “companion” is not one common implementation. Romance and friendship are possible fiction, not a mandatory universal approval-meter or romance-minigame system. [S3, S7]

Quests can come from patrons, rumors, personal goals, faction demands, discoveries, and consequences. The DM can prepare an adventure, but no player is required by the core mechanics to follow a video-game quest marker. Renown, rewards, favors, and institutional authority can extend progression beyond level. Crafting, investigation, performances, competitions, social events, and stronghold activity provide downtime; there is no single obligatory minigame for each. [S7]

The 2024 DMG adds **Bastions**: player-controlled bases with facilities, orders, turns, hirelings, and events. They connect advancement to a home and recurring off-adventure activity. Official public contents and product descriptions establish the structure; the paid chapter redirected to the marketplace during this research, so this dossier does not pretend to audit every facility or reproduce its exact output table. Russell’s review usefully questions whether some defensive investments have meaningful consequences and whether facility eligibility favors spellcasters. That criticism is one reviewer’s judgment, not a demonstrated population-wide failure. [S7, R5]

## 8. Worked situations

These are constructed illustrations, not claimed actual-play transcripts. Exact DCs below are example rulings unless identified as published rules.

**A. Quiet entry or reliable entry.** The party needs a ledger behind a locked door before a patrol returns. The rogue has appropriate tools and a chance to work; the wizard has Knock prepared and a slot. The DM clarifies whether the goal includes remaining unnoticed. Tools can require an uncertain check with time/noise consequences; Knock has its defined effect and sound. Opening the door does not complete the theft, erase the patrol, or resolve a second lock. The next choice concerns exposure, timing, and the escape route. [S6; SRD Knock]

**B. Permission from a guard.** A guard wants to keep people safe but has orders not to admit strangers. Producing a valid permit may make compliance routine. Asking for an exception may leave the guard hesitant; demanding treason may be refused. A bribe can change the situation, expose a new risk, or offend this particular guard. A high Persuasion result cannot create authority the guard lacks. The next approach might be finding the actual decision-maker. [S3, S5]

**C. A chasm and real equipment.** A character checks the gap, run-up, load, and landing. A jump within the explicit baseline may need no roll; unstable footing or a farther leap changes the problem. Rope anchored on one side is not magically secured on the other. A flying character can help establish the line, but concentration, carrying limits, and exposure remain relevant. The next decision is who crosses first and who protects the anchor. [S3, S5; SRD Fly]

**D. A rescue under pressure.** An ally is down while a threat controls the approach. Healing, dragging, blocking sight, Disengaging, or forcing the enemy away are different actions with different costs. A successful heal does not teleport the ally to safety. An attempted nonlethal attack must qualify under the melee rule rather than being declared after any arbitrary lethal effect. The next decision is recovery versus withdrawal. [S3, S5]

**E. An industrial commission.** The party commissions plate armor before a dangerous expedition. Money covers only one constraint; tools, proficiency, material availability, work time, and assistance still matter. Waiting may let a faction act. A promise of instant delivery requires a separately supported magical capability, not a high generic Craft roll. The next choice is cheaper available armor, postponement, borrowing, or a new procurement task. [S6]

**F. Information without certainty.** Investigators find a witness who sincerely repeats a false rumor. Insight can inform a reading of intention; it is not a universal factual truth detector. Magical interrogation also has specific limits. Several independent evidence routes are more robust than one indispensable roll. The next decision is whether to trust, corroborate, or act while uncertain. [S3; interpretation; see adjudication study]

## 9. Presentation, production, access, and commercial context

The physical interface is a book, character sheet, spoken description, and dice; maps, miniatures, virtual tabletops, voice acting, music, and sound effects are optional layers. Unlike a video game, D&D has no mandatory camera, animation system, or score soundtrack. A map can make positioning legible while also making players assume unpictured details do not exist. Speech can convey unlimited possibilities but impose memory, hearing, turn-taking, and shared-attention costs. Written summaries, explicit rules references, descriptive rather than performed dialogue, and readable sheets support different participants. [S3–S4; interpretation]

The revised PHB’s tutorial-first ordering, summaries, class-local spell lists, glossary, and extensive illustration make it a meaningful interface study. Storyscribe values the usable tool descriptions and clearer organization; Hoffer sees improved presentation but little conceptual novelty; Russell identifies remaining wording and creation-order friction. These are independently attributed assessments, not measurements proving improved retention. [R1–R4]

Wizards of the Coast publishes the revision. Its public development route included playtest material, feedback, and a deliberately compatible revision of fifth edition rather than replacement of every existing adventure. The core release was staggered across 2024–2025. The freely accessible rules and SRD are deliberately smaller than the commercial core books. Physical books, digital editions, bundles, optional platform subscriptions, adventures, and supplements are distinct purchases or services; paying for a tool is not necessary to play at a physical table. [S1–S2, S7]

Distribution through bookstores, hobby stores, digital storefronts, and third-party tools gives different entry paths. A character build, striking illustration, unusual ruling, memorable scene, or campaign anecdote can be a shareable object. Actual-play shows can demonstrate conversation and make character relationships emotionally accessible. These are plausible dissemination mechanisms, not a measured attribution model for D&D growth. Existing groups, scheduling, and the availability of someone willing to DM remain adoption constraints even when the rulebook improves.

No verified edition-specific active-player total, revised-edition retention rate, total paid-table count, or profitability figure is claimed here. Large franchise reach, a book launch, and a digital account count would measure different things. A home table can keep using its purchased books after a publisher changes its product line; a hosted character builder has continuing version and access dependencies.

## 10. Reception: five substantive written pieces

The five pieces below were read as text. They cover the revised game through four PHB reviews and one DMG review, from four publications and four named/bylined voices; the two Wargamer pieces share a reviewer and are **not** five independent observers. No Steam review surface applies to this tabletop ruleset. R1, R3, and R4 also contain direct accounts of play or switching a group, rather than store copy.

| Source | What it values | What it questions; evidence boundary |
|---|---|---|
| R1: Mollie Russell, Wargamer, PHB review, updated July 28, 2025 | Beginner-facing structure, stronger class options, useful tools, Weapon Mastery; reports its impact in a session. | Ambiguous wording, constrained backgrounds, reduced setting flavor, homogenization, unresolved balance at the time of review. Early-session evidence, not a long campaign study. |
| R2: Christian Hoffer, ComicBook.com, August 1, 2024 | Cleaner rules, layout, art, and an improved entrance to the game. | Conservative improvements do not particularly excite this veteran DM; comparisons with previous supplements are critical interpretation. |
| R3: Storyscribe / cboliver22, September 30, 2024 | Teaching before character creation, local spell lists, concrete tool uses, martial choices after the group’s switch. | Delaying every subclass to level 3 can postpone identity that previously existed immediately. Its popularity claim is not adopted as a census. |
| R4: Doug, Gleanings in Buddha-Fields, September 28 / October 31, 2024 | A family could return to familiar play, reuse setting material, and enjoy new character features. | Notes subclass-selection contraction and the need to reread changed spells; broader views of other systems and Hasbro remain personal experience, not neutral market evidence. |
| R5: Mollie Russell, Wargamer, DMG review, October 15, 2024 | More practical GM instruction, tracking sheets, usable preparation support. | Questions Bastion defense incentives, facility asymmetries, and a lore glossary that gives newcomers names without reasons to care. A book review rather than a full facility-by-facility playtest. |

The disagreement is instructive: accessibility can improve without novelty improving; standardization can simplify learning while weakening some identities; more lore can create less orientation. Those are tradeoffs to investigate, not reasons to declare the revision universally better or worse.

Access limitations: the attempted Polygon review fetch failed; Rascal’s critical article exposed only its introduction before a subscriber boundary. Neither is counted as a fully read fifth review. Complete paid PHB, DMG, and Monster Manual texts were not accessed. This pass uses the licensed SRD, public rules, official publication descriptions, and the explicitly identified criticism instead.

## 11. Transferable inspiration and limits

The most promising transferable pattern is a stable negotiation between open intentions and bounded consequences. A small common resolution vocabulary lets a player attempt unusual things, while equipment, knowledge, relationships, and circumstance determine which attempts make sense. The [adjudication study](../mechanics/dungeons-and-dragons-adjudication.md) develops this as research rather than assuming that an unconstrained language model can replace every DM responsibility.

Useful candidates include visible action costs; techniques for accepting routine success; contextual permission before checks; reusable effect families with exact exceptions; equipment that changes available approaches; persistent consequences; and short, understandable explanations of why an attempt works or fails. Important cautions are arbitrary DC invention, excessive rolls, charisma as mind control, unbounded improvisational power, six-second turns treated as literal physics, and importing a combat-first class system as a universal ontology for every inhabitant.

D&D’s social negotiation is also part of its machinery. A human can notice confusion, repair expectations, consent to a retcon, and agree to skip an uninteresting detail. A persistent multi-agent simulation needs explicit policies for those choices, particularly when different players share consequences. The human experience is a valuable source of design patterns, not evidence that those patterns already solve automation.
