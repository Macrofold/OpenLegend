## 8. Production and platform pitfalls

### The recurring traps

| Trap | Why it is dangerous | Practical response for OpenLegend |
|---|---|---|
| Building the framework before the experience | Interfaces accumulate without evidence of a satisfying loop | Keep an end-to-end playable scenario as an acceptance gate |
| Measuring possibility instead of consequence | New nouns/prose look varied while decisions stay the same | Track changed actions, relationships, and viable alternatives |
| Assuming generation eliminates content design | Tone, pacing, constraints, and quality still require selection | Author a strong default and use generation selectively |
| Treating more realistic needs as more fun | Maintenance can crowd out discovery and relationships | Test attention cost and whether autonomy preserves meaningful choices |
| Building a creator platform without non-creators | Supply grows without demand or useful feedback | Ship playable examples and measure adoption/play separately |
| Mistaking community affection for operating viability | A small loyal audience may not cover persistent service costs | Match hosting and AI cost to a modest viable audience |
| Making every feature require every other feature | Nothing ships until a universal system exists | Stage interfaces around complete vertical experiences |
| Ignoring onboarding because experts cope | Expert demonstrations hide newcomer failure | Observe uncoached first sessions, not only creator playtests |
| Shipping a technical error as fictional consequence | Trust in the world’s rules collapses | Distinguish native failure, unsupported action, and system failure |
| Overpromising a rare demo | Players expect the exceptional case as a normal guarantee | Market representative reproducible play |
| Requiring constant connectivity/hosting unnecessarily | Persistence and shutdown risk become product-wide | Keep the simplest authority model consistent with the promise |
| Optimizing away all expression | A technically efficient game can become emotionally generic | Preserve distinctive art, sound, character, and small moments |

These recommendations combine the contrasts above, especially EverQuest Next, Worlds Adrift, Dreams, Spore, and the Hades interviews. ### Three production lessons especially relevant to AI-assisted development

**More code is not the same as more validated play.** An AI can add many mutually compatible interfaces without proving that a player understands the resulting activity. Treat generated implementation capacity as a way to run more good experiments—not as justification to postpone selection.

**A feature’s integration cost can exceed its local implementation cost.** Wube’s retrospective is a concrete reminder that distributed dependencies make change and regression management expensive. OpenLegend’s module boundaries are valuable insofar as they reduce repeated work and preserve coherent behavior. They should not become abstractions without consumers. [FA2](../references/sources-01.md#source-fa2)

**A service-dependent mechanic has an ongoing experience cost.** Latency, budgeting, interrupted generation, and variable output quality are part of the product. Native actions and useful character routines should continue where appropriate without a fresh model call. A fallback must remain truthful; it should not fabricate completion or secretly change the rules.

### Community and creator trust

A future pack ecosystem introduces expectations about credit, permissions, compatibility, retained work, and discovery. Source availability alone does not make a pack understandable or maintainable. A polished authoring chat does not solve dependency or moderation problems.

Roblox’s disclosed operating scale is a reminder that platform success includes substantial infrastructure and trust work. Do not interpret this as an instruction to build that full apparatus now. Instead, keep early sharing bounded, make rights and activation clear, and avoid promising a creator livelihood before there is evidence of consumer demand. [RB1](../references/sources-01.md#source-rb1)

### Avoid the “failed game” monoculture

The cautionary cases have different outcomes:

- **Canceled before release:** EverQuest Next.
- **Service viability failure:** Worlds Adrift; online closure for Project Spark.
- **Live-development support ended while creation/play remained:** Dreams.
- **Mixed creative-versus-strategic reception:** Spore.
- **Launch disappointment followed by substantial repair:** No Man’s Sky.
- **One subsystem discarded:** Ultima Online’s ecology.

The classifications follow the case sources. They do not justify one explanation for all of them. In particular, do not infer that ambition is bad, players dislike creativity, or simulation cannot work. The useful question is which promise failed to connect to a sustainable, understandable experience.
