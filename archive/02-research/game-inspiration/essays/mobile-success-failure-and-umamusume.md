## The question the comparison needs to answer

The useful question behind the quoted $2.5 billion is how a game can remain worth playing and paying for across many years. A recognizable premise can attract attention, but a sustainable service also needs satisfying repeated play, reasons for a player to value its offers, and an organization capable of supplying the experience at a viable cost. These are separate problems. Solving the first does not establish that the others have been solved.

Our working explanation for Umamusume is that character attachment, repeated improvement, persistent ownership and renewed goals can reinforce each other. The dossiers document the relevant mechanisms; the strength of each mechanism's contribution to revenue remains an inference. Public spending estimates cannot tell us how much income came from affection for a character, competitive ambition, convenience, an expiring offer or another motive. The final comparison therefore distinguishes the observable product from the proposed explanation of its results.

## A large total can come from very different audiences

An illustrative accounting identity is:

**Cumulative player spending = the sum, across periods, of paying players × average spending per paying player.**

This is a way to organize the question, not an estimate of Umamusume's actual payer population. For example, both hypothetical businesses below would collect $2.5 billion:

| Hypothetical monthly payers | Hypothetical average monthly spending per payer | Duration  | Cumulative spending |
| --------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------- | --------- | ------------------- |
| 1,000,000                   | $50                                             | 50 months | $2.5 billion        |
| 250,000                     | $200                                            | 50 months | $2.5 billion        |

Real populations fluctuate, individuals spend different amounts, and a person can move between paying and nonpaying periods. The illustration simply explains why a free game does not need billions of purchasers to accumulate billions in spending. It also explains why downloads alone cannot answer the business question. A download does not specify continued use, whether the person pays, how often they pay or what serving that person costs.

For the publisher, several further quantities matter: amounts retained after store arrangements and taxes, contractual revenue shares, development and ongoing production, marketing, infrastructure and support. Gross customer expenditure cannot be substituted for profit. Nor can a corporate segment's profit be assigned to one game without an allocation that the company actually discloses.

These questions are analytical readings of the documented cases, not claims that one variable explains their outcomes. A familiar brand, elaborate mechanics, positive reviews or a devoted community can all coexist with an ended service. Conversely, a financially successful game can still produce fatigue, frustration or criticism. Enjoyment, continued use, customer spending and profitability should be evaluated separately.

## Comparing the promise with the work required

A useful analysis begins with the experience a player wants, then asks what work stands between that desire and a satisfying result. Some work creates the pleasure: recognizing a pattern, choosing a plan, taking a risk, coordinating with someone or discovering a useful interaction. Other work exists mainly because a player must clear it before reaching the part they came for.

The distinction cannot be settled by counting systems or minutes. A long session may be absorbing; a brief session may feel empty. A demanding preparation chain may make an achievement meaningful for one audience and obstruct it for another. A repeated action can provide mastery when its circumstances change, or feel like an obligation when the decision has disappeared.

This suggests separating four questions in further research:

1. **What result does the player care about?** Identify a person, object, place, discovery, contest or social contribution rather than only a reward currency.
2. **Which decisions make reaching that result enjoyable?** Explain what the player notices, chooses and learns.
3. **What survives the session?** Distinguish durable ownership, improved understanding, social history and temporary numerical gains.
4. **Why would another attempt be interesting?** A fresh decision is a stronger explanation than the mere availability of another reward.

The answers should remain specific to the intended audience. A design that requires coordination, uninterrupted attention or comfort with a large interface asks for a different commitment than a short solitary puzzle. Neither commitment is inherently superior; the commercial question is whether enough of the intended audience values the actual experience under real conditions.

## Questions for OpenLegend, with evidence that could change our minds

These are research questions suggested by the comparison. They do not authorize feature implementation.

| Question                                                     | What would support the idea?                                                                                                      | What would challenge it?                                                             |
| ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Can a concrete attachment make improvement matter?           | Players independently refer to a particular character, possession, place or relationship when explaining why an outcome mattered. | The attachment is nominal; only the numerical reward explains their choices.         |
| Does another attempt create a new decision?                  | Players can describe what they learned and voluntarily choose a different plan.                                                   | They repeat the same inputs because a meter or calendar requires it.                 |
| Does persistence preserve something worth revisiting?        | Earlier choices continue to affect useful options or personal meaning.                                                            | Accumulated items have little purpose, or maintaining them feels like avoiding loss. |
| Does specialization create useful cooperation?               | A player can make an understandable contribution that another person values.                                                      | Cooperation mainly means waiting, mandatory attendance or dependence without agency. |
| Is the experience enjoyable before a payment prompt?         | Players can explain the appeal without referring to purchase relief.                                                              | The strongest reason to pay is to escape an unenjoyable requirement.                 |
| Is the production burden proportionate to the value created? | A bounded addition enables several worthwhile situations, and its support burden is understood.                                   | More content creates mostly maintenance while the central play remains weak.         |

The order matters: first understand the enjoyable activity and the player's reasons to care. Then examine whether repetition, persistence and social structure strengthen it. Commercial structure should be evaluated against that experience rather than taken as proof that the experience works.

## Evidence and scope

The dossier set includes major mechanics inventories, concrete constructed interactions, fictional economies, narrative and creator-tool boundaries, production and distribution, historical purchases, commercial evidence, independent written reviews and original player accounts. Early builds, later updates, original games, adaptations and unofficial reconstruction are explicitly separated where the evidence requires it.

Written reviews provide situated accounts, often with limited hours or a particular device. Player posts provide reasons someone gives for staying, paying or leaving. Neither is a representative retention study. Publisher and developer statements establish their reported intent or explanation; vendor revenue models estimate defined slices of the market. None alone establishes a causal model of success.

The final essay will retain missing quantities as missing: title-specific profit, complete cost structures, payer distributions, acquisition costs, cohort retention and the financial contribution of individual mechanics cannot be manufactured from the recovered public evidence.
