# Fun, engagement and compulsion — research foundations

**Research reference, October 14, 2026.** This document owns the terminology and selective outside-research foundation for the [canonical cross-dossier synthesis](../essays/fun-and-sustainable-engagement.md). It is not a clinical assessment, an exhaustive literature review, a neuroscience model, or an approved OpenLegend implementation specification. Game-specific evidence belongs in the dossiers and the [two-volume evidence map](fun-and-engagement-dossier-evidence.md).

## The answer: related, but not the same thing

For design work, ask four separate questions: **Is this enjoyable now? Is it worthwhile afterward? Do I want to return? Can I comfortably stop?** A single playtime measure cannot answer all four. Our working objective is a game people value participating in and freely choose to revisit, not a game that maximizes difficulty leaving.

This distinction is consistent with research separating harmonious from obsessive engagement and with the clinical requirement for impairment rather than mere enthusiasm. It does not imply that an enjoyable game cannot be overused, or that every intense session is harmful. Nor does it make uneventful comfort, a frightening challenge, or a moving finite story inferior to a continuously exciting reward loop. The empirical findings supporting these distinctions, and their limits, are recorded below. [F2](#f2) [F3](#f3) [F5](#f5)

## Working vocabulary

These are operational definitions for this research library unless explicitly attributed to a clinical source. They are not interchangeable diagnostic scales.

| Term | Meaning here | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Fun / enjoyment** | A positively valued experience of playing: for example amusement, satisfying action, discovery, absorption or comfortable routine. Ask what part is enjoyable rather than assuming one universal sensation. | Long duration, commercial success, or the absence of every negative emotion. |
| **Appreciation / meaning** | Valuing what an experience expresses, reveals or makes one care about, including experiences that are sad, tense or challenging. | That the controls, repeated tasks or every moment are enjoyable. |
| **Engagement** | Attention, effort or involvement in an activity. It can be cognitive, practical, emotional or social. | Why the person is involved, whether involvement is welcome, or whether it causes impairment. |
| **Retention / return** | Observable resumption of play under a stated time window and population. | Enjoyment, loyalty, addiction, or causation by the last feature changed. |
| **Habit** | For this analysis, a recurring pattern prompted by a familiar situation or cue. | Impaired control or harmful dependence. A chosen social routine and an unwanted checking routine need separate evaluation. |
| **Compulsive persistence** | A non-diagnostic design concern: continuing or returning despite wanting to stop, especially when leaving feels costly or continued play conflicts with other priorities. | A diagnosis, a universal response to a mechanic, or proof that enjoyable content is absent. |
| **Gaming disorder** | WHO's clinical category involving impaired control, gaming taking priority, and continuation despite negative consequences, with significant functional impairment and a pattern normally evident for at least twelve months. | A synonym for a favorite game, a long session, an invested community or a high lifetime hour count. [F5](#f5) |

When a source says **addictive**, preserve the source's intended language, then identify what it actually observed: wanting another attempt, enjoying a session, repeatedly checking a shop, regretting continued play, or something else. Do not silently translate enthusiasm into disorder or soften a report of unwanted persistence into harmless fun.

## What the selected primary research supports

### F1 — Competence, autonomy and connection are useful motivational lenses

<a id="f1"></a>**Ryan, Rigby and Przybylski (2006), _The Motivational Pull of Video Games: A Self-Determination Theory Approach_.** [Publisher abstract and record](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8), DOI `10.1007/s11031-006-9051-8`.

Across four studies, the authors report relationships between perceived competence/autonomy and enjoyment, preferences and aspects of well-being. Their multiplayer-community study also relates autonomy, competence and relatedness to enjoyment and future play. The abstract connects intuitive controls with competence/autonomy and presence.

**Use:** ask whether players feel effective, endorse their actions, and experience wanted connection. **Limit:** these are psychological experiences, not a recipe requiring maximum difficulty, maximum choices or mandatory multiplayer. The publisher abstract was inspected; this pass does not reproduce the full methods, effect sizes or validate a particular OpenLegend feature.

### F2 — Wanting to play and feeling driven to play can differ

<a id="f2"></a>**Przybylski, Weinstein, Ryan and Rigby (2009), _Having to versus Wanting to Play: Background and Consequences of Harmonious versus Obsessive Engagement in Video Games_.** [Primary article record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19772442/), DOI `10.1089/cpb.2009.0083`.

The reported results distinguish harmonious passion, associated with enjoyment and energy after play, from obsessive passion, associated with more play, more tension and less enjoyment. Daily psychological-need satisfaction also matters; the unique contribution of passion to well-being was small after accounting for everyday need satisfaction.

**Use:** measure reasons for continuing, not just duration. **Limit:** these associations do not diagnose a player, prove that a particular reward causes compulsion, or establish that ordinary life circumstances are irrelevant. The bibliographic abstract, not a new analysis of participant data, is the evidence used here.

### F3 — Meaning is not reducible to immediate pleasure

<a id="f3"></a>**Oliver, Bowman, Woolley, Rogers, Sherrick and Chung, _Video Games as Meaningful Entertainment Experiences_.** [Author-institution record, dated 2015](https://digitalcommons.butler.edu/ccom_papers/145/), DOI `10.1037/ppm0000066`.

In a study of 512 participants, people were randomly assigned to recall a particularly fun or meaningful game and then describe it. Both conditions produced high enjoyment; appreciation was higher for meaningful recollections. Gameplay, competence and autonomy were more associated with enjoyment, while story, insight and relatedness were more associated with appreciation.

**Use:** evaluate satisfying participation and meaningful experience separately. **Limit:** random assignment concerned the recall prompt, not assignment to different game mechanics. This retrospective study does not show that adding story necessarily improves a game. The institution's abstract and post-print metadata were inspected, not a claimed reproduction of the copy of record.

### F4 — Hours are an insufficient welfare proxy

<a id="f4"></a>**Vuorre, Johannes, Magnusson and Przybylski (2022), _Time Spent Playing Video Games Is Unlikely to Impact Well-Being_.** [Published article record and abstract](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35911206/), DOI `10.1098/rsos.220411`.

The published study linked six weeks of objective play data from 38,935 players, supplied by seven publishers, with three waves of self-reported well-being. It found little to no evidence for a causal connection between playtime and well-being in that analysis; motivations appeared relevant. The authors describe average effects of time as probably small.

**Use:** do not substitute total time for experienced value or harm. **Limit:** observational modeling is not randomized assignment. This window and population cannot establish safety for every player, child, game, monetization system or longer-term pattern. The inspected abstract and record support this bounded finding, not an independent statistical audit.

### F5 — Clinical addiction requires a different evidentiary standard

<a id="f5"></a>**World Health Organization, _Gaming Disorder_ FAQ.** [Official definition and identification guidance](https://www.who.int/standards/classifications/frequently-asked-questions/gaming-disorder), checked October 14, 2026.

The clinical boundary in the vocabulary table is deliberately narrower than everyday “addictive.” A transcript, review or session log does not establish its required impairment and behavioral pattern. This library therefore does not diagnose the narrator, their friends, or any game's player population.

## From sources to mechanisms: hypotheses, not brain claims

The following framework is **our interpretation of the dossier evidence**, not an additional finding of F1–F5. Mechanisms should be described at the level actually observed.

**Activity value:** performing an action feels good, a problem becomes understandable, a plan works, an expressive object takes shape, a companion reacts, or the next authored scene creates curiosity. Rewards may help communicate that value or unlock more of it. [Evidence map, G01–G80](fun-and-engagement-dossier-evidence.md)

**Anticipated value:** a player continues toward a desired discovery, useful capability, collection, social occasion or ending. Delayed payoff is not inherently bad: preparing a voyage and finishing a story can both be worthwhile. The question is whether the path and destination still justify the player's effort. [Evidence map, G81–G148](fun-and-engagement-dossier-evidence-g81-g148.md)

**Pressure to continue:** an expiring opportunity, vulnerable possession, social obligation or repeated chance at a coveted result may make stopping feel costly. That is a mechanism to investigate, not proof of disorder or proof that everyone dislikes the system. The supplied Roblox account gives a concrete self-report of remaining for shop restocks, while other cases describe pleasant routine and creative play. [Roblox transcript study](roblox-addictiveness-video.md)

The distinction is not “rewards versus no rewards.” Bee Swarm's randomness exists alongside planning; Balatro's uncertain offers create strategic choices; a finite authored surprise can hold attention without a sandbox. Conversely, a rewarding-looking event can remove the decisions that made play worthwhile. Classify **the complete activity, the alternatives available, and the player's experienced motive**, not an isolated feature name. [Roblox study](roblox-addictiveness-video.md) · [Balatro](../dossiers/balatro.md)

The transcript's “dopamine maxing,” claims of universal attention-span decline, and claims that brains adapted to FOMO are not neurochemical evidence. This research contains no dopamine measurements and supplies no optimal reward frequency, universal threshold for addiction, or proof that creativity never becomes stale. Those unsupported claims remain explicitly attributed in the source study rather than becoming foundations for design.

## How to use the distinction

A proposed review should pair behavior with the player's account: what they intended to do, which moments they enjoyed, what felt worthwhile afterward, why they continued, and whether they could leave without an unwanted penalty. Someone completing a short story and departing satisfied should not automatically score worse than someone repeatedly checking a timer while wishing to leave.

Test changes against their intended value. Reducing interruption cost is different from making combat easier. Explaining a rule is different from solving a mystery. Accelerating repetitive catching-up is different from granting power that erases the next challenge. Removing an attendance penalty is different from removing a social occasion. These distinctions are supported as design comparisons by the dossier corpus, not yet established as outcomes for OpenLegend.

The [canonical synthesis](../essays/fun-and-sustainable-engagement.md) owns the cross-game principles, counterexamples, OpenLegend hypotheses and proposed evaluation questions. Future research should revise these interpretations when player evidence disagrees, preserve edition and audience differences, and distinguish a design recommendation from a demonstrated causal result.
