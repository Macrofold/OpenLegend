# Where the Water Tastes Like Wine — launch and commercial dossier

[Collection index](../README.md) · **Read and written: 2026-10-15** · **Cohort: unsuccessful commercial launch; artistic achievement explicitly distinguished**

## Outcome and team-size qualification

On March 30, 2018, creator **Johnnemann Nordhagen** described the early commercial result as disastrous: he had received **US$0 personally**, after spending approximately **US$140,000 on collaborators**. This is not a statement that the game generated zero gross sales. He explicitly objected to headlines calling the whole creative project a failure and planned continued improvements. [S1]

This was a solo-led Dim Bulb project with a **much larger external production network**, including Serenity Forge, numerous writers, artists and performers. It is included as a qualified small-core example, **not** as a game with fewer than ten total contributors. Steam credits Dim Bulb and Serenity Forge as developers and Good Shepherd as publisher. [S1], [S3]

## Funding, collaborators and how connections happened

No Kickstarter route is established. The documented financing includes the creator's spending and a publisher relationship. **Good Shepherd** was encountered at **SXSW** and supplied marketing/PR, localization and QA. A meeting with **Z from Serenity Forge at IndieCade** led to crucial production help after losing key artists. The founder regretted bringing editor Laura Michet in late. [S1]

These are concrete industry-contact routes, not evidence that a single marketplace supplied the entire team. The dossier does not infer that attending an event guarantees a publisher or affordable production partner.

Dias provides a complementary contractor perspective: he wrote about 30 of the game's 200-plus vignettes. Writers received artwork as a starting brief; individual pieces were ideally under 400 words. He was paid as a contractor and had no direct stake in sales. He also describes limited cross-pollination between workstreams. That is useful evidence about the coordination demands hidden inside a content-heavy “solo” project. [S2]

## Launch reception and diagnosis

The Steam page establishes a narrative/interactive-fiction product and its release route. Current reviews are not used to reconstruct original sales. [S3]

The founder identifies late full-game playtesting, weak pacing and insufficient specialist knowledge as production problems. A successful short showcase could therefore fail to represent the eventual complete experience. That explanation is attributed to the creator; it is not an independently tested estimate of how much revenue better pacing would have produced. [S1]

Dias's account separately cautions that this kind of anthology can continue absorbing content indefinitely. His observations concern the writing process, not privileged knowledge of commercial analytics. [S2]

## Case-specific observations and limits

**Positive:** networking produced specific publishing and production relationships; specialist work was paid rather than universally expected on speculative revenue share. **Negative:** professional marketing and recognized collaborators did not ensure a commercially sustainable launch.

The useful unresolved issue is how early complete-experience testing, editorial ownership and a coherent market proposition could have altered scope or spending. The available evidence supports asking that question, not asserting a counterfactual profitable version existed.

Lifetime recovery, per-platform receipts, publisher recoupment, exact back-end terms, contractor rates, launch wishlists, campaign referral analytics and social growth remain unverified. The classification deliberately stops at the early 2018 commercial window and preserves the creator's distinction between artistic and financial value.
