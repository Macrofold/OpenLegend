# What's the Password? — launch and commercial dossier

[Collection index](../README.md) · **Read and written: 2026-10-16** · **Outcome: small reported cash-cost recovery at launch; later mixed reception; founder-labor profitability unknown**

## Origin, product and inherited advantages

This is TrampolineTales' **2026** password-decoding puzzle game, not another similarly named title. Steam and the creator's itch.io page date release to **May 28, 2026**. The compact proposition is over one hundred four-digit puzzles, sold as an experience of roughly two hours. Steam lists TrampolineTales as developer/publisher; itch.io purchases include a Steam key. Those channels must not be counted as independent additive customers without sales records. [S2, S3]

Dan DiIorio already had Luck be a Landlord and Maze Mice. The soundtrack credits **Vincent Colavita**, a returning collaborator. The production therefore reused business and creative relationships rather than proving that a new creator can immediately obtain the same reach. [S2, S4]

## Launch economics and sequencing

The creator's **May 30, 2026** report gives **US$3,750 external budget**: key art US$1,000, music US$500, additional art US$250 and localization US$2,000. Around **1,000 Steam and 200 mobile sales** produced approximately **US$4,500 after the described deductions**, leaving **US$750 above that budget**. Programming/design labor was excluded. [S1]

He brought release forward from a proposed August date to coincide with **Cerebral Puzzle Showcase**. About **500 creators received prerelease keys three weeks before launch**. He reports approximately **5,400 wishlists when Popular Upcoming placement arrived**, estimates at least 1,000 from creator coverage, and identifies two Chinese-language videos totaling roughly 400,000 views. No paid sponsored coverage was used. These are attributed observations and estimates. [S1]

The newsletter retains a **May 2, 2026 public-demo announcement**, followed by launch messaging. That establishes playable exposure before payment, but not the first prototype's age or an exact prelaunch demo completion rate. [S5]

## Case-specific observations

**Interpretation:** a carefully bounded project can recover cash expenditures with a small number of sales. That does not make those expenditures the full economic cost or the developer's labor free. Existing catalog bundles and creator relationships also limit comparison with a debut.

This case also challenges the idea that a specific wishlist target should always determine release timing. The source describes choosing a relevant audience event instead. It does **not** establish a universal 5,400-, 7,000- or 20,000-wishlist threshold; timing, competing products and storefront context remain uncontrolled.

The outlier worth retaining is not that reviews do not matter, but that limited upfront cash exposure, immediate receipts and audience satisfaction can move independently. A later drop in reception is not evidence that the original reported cash recovery was fabricated.
