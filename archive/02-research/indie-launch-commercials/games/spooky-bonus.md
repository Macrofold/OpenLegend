# Spooky Bonus — launch and commercial dossier

[Collection index](../README.md) · **Read and written: 2026-10-16** · **Outcome: successful casual-portal product; comparatively modest later Steam sales**

## Origin and the hidden preparation behind a short project

Jake Birkett describes Spooky Bonus as his **seventh match-three game**, made in **three months** with an existing engine. In January 2018 he estimated nearly **US$400 earned per development hour**, explicitly excluding time spent on earlier games and their reusable technology. This is a creator's project-level estimate, not a beginner's attainable hourly wage or an audited lifetime profit figure. [S1]

That prior work matters to the comparison: three calendar months does not mean three months from first learning to make games. The dossier does not establish a complete contemporaneous core headcount or identify every specialist as an employee.

## Distribution sequence and maturity

| Stage | Record |
|---|---|
| 2013 | Commercial casual-portal release; the iWin listing records October 10, 2013. Its retained description presents a complete, downloadable match-three game with relaxed and timed modes. [S2] |
| 2015 | Steam release followed a lengthy Greenlight process, with achievements and trading cards added. The creator says the game was already the studio's best seller, but was not a major Steam seller. [S3] |
| October 15, 2015 | Official Steam release date, with Grey Alien Games as developer and publisher. [S4] |
| January 2018 | The project-level return estimate above, years after original release rather than launch-week income. [S1] |

No Kickstarter campaign, fundraising target, opening/closing dates or pledge total is established. Its commercial route was selling a finished game through existing distribution channels, not a recovered crowdfunding campaign or a newly created social platform.

## Community and promotional evidence

The developer's December 2015 retrospective discusses the existing product catalog and the work needed for the Steam edition. [S3] The surviving Steam page offers a demo and bundles with other Grey Alien products. That demonstrates a present sampling/cross-selling route, **not** proof of demo availability on the first 2013 release day or historical bundle conversion. [S4]

The sources do not reconstruct first forum posts, a prelaunch email list, YouTube account creation, posting cadence, historical followers or Discord membership. They therefore do not support the claim that a large developer-led social audience preceded the original success. Nor do they prove that no such promotion occurred.

The important evidence is distribution-specific: the creator had a product that sold well outside Steam before a less impressive Steam debut. That is different from a game with no customers anywhere.

## Collaborators and evidence boundaries

The record establishes repeated in-house production experience and outside distribution, but not artist-recruitment websites, individual fees, contracts or a dedicated marketing employee. The Steam developer/publisher label should not be expanded into a claim that all art, audio and testing were performed by one person.

No later complete revenue ledger, tax calculation, sales by individual portal or controlled advertising experiment was recovered. The current favorable Steam reviews are not used to back-calculate unit sales. iWin's historical page was indexed, but direct retrieval failed during this pass; its date remains a distributor metadata observation.

## Case-specific observations

**Interpretation:** an unglamorous, well-targeted product can be commercially important even when it is not a famous Steam hit. The useful questions are which customers it served and what prior work made its scope economical—not whether the developer attracted millions of video views.

This case and [Regency Solitaire](regency-solitaire.md) come from the same studio and cannot be counted as independent statistical confirmation of a universal formula. The comparison with [Shadowhand](shadowhand.md) is useful precisely because a bigger, better-publicized product can produce a different return on labor.
