# Monkey Labour — launch and commercial dossier

[Collection index](../README.md) · **Read and written: 2026-10-15** · **Cohort: unsuccessful commercial return; small iOS launch and failed desktop extension**

## Team, origin and commercial boundary

Dawn of Play originated within R&D company Razum. Contributor **Matej Jan** worked as an independent contractor through Retronator. His account identifies five developers/contributors plus two outsourced audio specialists. The parent business helped sustain further work when game sales did not cover production costs. [S1]

The game is an intentionally small, retro handheld-style arcade title, not a large content-heavy adventure. Its official site emphasizes iPhone/iPad/iPod Touch availability, achievements, Game Center scores and nostalgia for electronic handheld games. The compact design makes it a useful counterexample to assuming small scope alone guarantees adequate commercial return. [S2]

## Release and measured outcome

| Window | Developer-reported result |
|---|---|
| January 2011 iOS debut | 52 copies on day one; 790 in the first month. |
| Update/relaunch | 215 copies in the first week, 24 the following week. |
| Later iOS period | Approximately 4,000 copies mentioned around award recognition. |
| Windows / IndieCity | Seven copies after almost two months. |
| Overall retrospective | Production costs not recovered. |

All figures are Jan's June 27, 2012 retrospective, not current sales or independently audited accounts. The desktop result is a separate platform experiment, not the entire game's lifetime unit count. [S1]

## Development, marketing and timing

The reported initial production involved roughly two months of team work. A later update prompted a larger publicity effort, including a new trailer, promotional webpage, review emails, giveaways, forum replies and a livestream. Jan recorded **113 hours** on that marketing effort. The promotion reached over **200 combined social-network followers**; a Reddit-linked video drew roughly **5,000 YouTube views**. Those are distinct measures, not evidence of thousands of paying fans. [S1]

The game was already released when the best-documented social campaign occurred. The reviewed record does not establish a substantial preproduction community or a years-long creator channel preceding development.

## Publishing, contracts and platform extension

The official page shows the studio selling a completed app directly through the App Store, with a trailer and review-led presentation. It supplies evidence of packaging and distribution, not of a separate publisher financing acquisition. [S2]

Jan's account describes the desktop port as a further experiment and records rejection by Steam, Desura and Big Fish. That concerns their historical gatekeeping, not today's application rules. The marketing expense here included the developer's own labor; no hired PR agency is established. [S1]

The approximate US$50,000 production valuation in the original essay is a hypothetical professional charge, **not a disclosed cash budget**. Artist/audio recruitment dates and contract rates remain unresolved.

## Case-specific observations

**Positive interpretation:** the team shipped, measured outcomes and documented a small-audience campaign instead of publishing only celebratory download numbers. Porting also produced technical knowledge, which should be assessed separately from commercial return.

**Negative interpretation:** the evidence does not support expecting a second storefront or a new trailer to rescue weak demand automatically. It is possible to improve promotional execution and still earn too little to sustain production.

The informative comparison is not “seven sales means no one ever liked the game.” There were mobile buyers and favorable reviews. The question is whether the returns justified repeated additional work on this particular product. An exact answer would require all labor costs, receipts and the value of alternative projects, which are not publicly itemized here.

## Remaining gaps

No historical mailing-list, Discord, wishlist or paid-ad records were recovered. The earliest announcement, first game-blog post, original social-account dates and art recruitment process are not established. The outcome is grounded in a named contributor's cost-recovery statement rather than an inferred revenue estimate from ratings.
