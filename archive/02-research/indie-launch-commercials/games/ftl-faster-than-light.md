# FTL: Faster Than Light — launch and commercial dossier

[Collection index](../README.md) · **Read and written: 2026-10-15** · **Cohort: commercial success**

## Outcome and scope

A two-founder game with a successful Kickstarter and a successful paid release, not merely a popular campaign. In a tenth-anniversary interview, Justin Ma recalled **100,000 copies sold within a month or two** of release. That is a retrospective, approximate founder statement, not a precise first-month audit. No lifetime profit figure is established here. [S5]

The relevant sequence is prototype and competitions in 2011, crowdfunding and commercial launch in 2012, and later reflection. This is a historical Steam-curation/early-Kickstarter case, not evidence that the same platform access is automatic today.

## Origins, team and development

Justin Ma and Matthew Davis were the core developers. Their GDC account describes beginning with the desired experience of being a spaceship captain rather than with a fixed feature specification. They discarded concepts that did not support that experience, including multiplayer. The project originally had a much smaller expected scope and schedule than the eventual game. [S4]

Their own postmortem puts total development at roughly **18 months**. They had left their jobs in early 2011, experimented before finding the structure, and were approaching the end of their savings around the crowdfunding period. [S2]

## Collaborators, publishing and marketing work

The founders handled publicity and community work themselves and later wished they had obtained help with PR and website management. Kickstarter money enabled a larger soundtrack from **Ben Prunty** and additional writing from **Tom Jubert**. These were specialist contributions around a two-person core, not evidence that two people supplied every asset and service. Exact contract rates and recruitment terms are not public in these sources. [S2]

The commercial release was self-published on Steam. Platform distribution is distinct from having a publisher finance development or run a campaign. [S6]

## Reception and bounded observations

**What worked in this case:** the externally playable prototype and competition history preceded the large funding audience. The campaign then supplied both finance and a reachable testing population. The documented order is more specific than “start a Discord” or “post for years.” [S1], [S3]

**What became difficult:** the founders' postmortem identifies fan-management work, increased expectations and the burden of simultaneous platform support. Its praise for crowdfunding should be read alongside those production costs. [S2]

**Interpretation:** the useful question for a solo developer is what evidence convinced strangers before they were asked to fund the game. FTL supplies evidence of an enjoyable playable proposition and third-party validation. It does not supply a repeatable formula for buying a particular number of followers.

## Evidence cautions and remaining gaps

The 2013 mini-postmortem refers to nearly 3,000 beta testers; the September 2012 interview refers to roughly 4,000. These may describe different populations or moments, but that reconciliation is not established. Preserve the discrepancy rather than averaging it. [S2], [S3]

Unknowns include initial social-account dates, historical follower curves, mailing-list growth, press-email response rates, campaign referral shares, contractor sourcing details and net profitability. The qualitative account is strong; attribution analytics are not.
