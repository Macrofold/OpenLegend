# PONCHO — launch and commercial dossier

[Collection index](../README.md) · **Read and written: 2026-10-15** · **Cohort: failed Kickstarter, then publisher-assisted release with unsuccessful developer returns through January 2017**

## Origin, artist recruitment and publicity

Dan Hayes and Jack Odell began in winter 2011. In 2013, roughly **50 applications** to pixel-art-site advertisements led to recruiting **Matthew Weekes**, using day-job savings. [S1]

Marketing waited until 2014: a trailer, then two pre-campaign months targeting approximately **1,000 press/YouTuber contacts**, three press releases and several weekly social/blog posts. [S1]

A **September 20, 2014 Unity forum post** introduces the shifting-parallax game, links Kickstarter and invites readers to EGX. Its author identifies himself as composer/co-designer. This is a directly inspectable developer-community invitation, not evidence of the first-ever mention or a measured source of sales. [S2]

## Publishing, release and reception

**Rising Star Games** funded continued development after the campaign. November 2015 reporting confirms Steam/Humble distribution and a reviewer who enjoyed the game while identifying problems. This establishes continued production and some favorable reception, not profitability. [S5]

Hayes dates Steam/PS4 release to **November 3, 2015** and reports sales below pessimistic expectations. By January 2017 he said the game had provided no financial return. [S1] That claim is not an audited worldwide revenue statement or evidence that no production advances had ever been paid.

A contemporary analysis reproduces competing developer/publisher accounts of missed milestones, payment expectations and promotion. The contract was not independently audited; this dossier does not resolve those disputes or identify the publisher as the sole cause of poor sales. [S6]

## Case-specific observations

**Interpretation:** a playable demonstration makes a pitch inspectable; it does not establish the scale of demand. Likewise, an agreement that enables continued production is not evidence that the earlier crowdfunding campaign succeeded.

The dispute exposes separate questions about financing timing, delivery responsibilities and promotional expectations. Without the full agreement and both parties' records, the case cannot establish which specific intervention would have changed the commercial result.

The creator's broad views about release months, publishers and key requests remain historical opinions, not current platform rules. No later lifetime recovery is established here.
