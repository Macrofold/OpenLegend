# PONCHO — launch and commercial dossier

[Collection index](../README.md) · **Read and written: 2026-10-15** · **Cohort: failed Kickstarter, then publisher-assisted release with unsuccessful developer returns through January 2017**

## Origin, art recruitment and first publicity

Dan Hayes dates the project to winter 2011 with Jack Odell. In 2013 they recruited pixel artist **Matthew Weekes** after advertising on pixel-art sites and receiving roughly **50 applications**, using money saved from a day job. This is a documented specialist-recruitment route, not merely a credits list. [S1]

Hayes says they deliberately deferred marketing until 2014. Their plan was a trailer followed by two months of promotion before Kickstarter: approximately 1,000 press/YouTuber contacts, three press releases and social/blog updates several times weekly. [S1]

A contemporary September 2014 creator post on Unity's forum introduces the game, explains its shifting-parallax premise, links the campaign and invites people to meet the developers at EGX. The author identifies himself as composer/co-designer. This provides a dated, directly inspectable example of developer-community outreach, but not the first-ever post or a measured source of customers. [S2]

## Crowdfunding and playable material

| Field | Evidence |
|---|---|
| Goal | **£22,500**, intended to support three people for another six months. [S1] |
| Campaign opening | Hayes recalls **September 2, 2014**; some historical listings differ by a day. [S1] |
| Closing | Contemporary coverage gives the night of October 1 / October 2, 2014; approximately a month. Exact platform-displayed duration was not recovered. [S4] |
| Outcome | Missed the goal; precise final pledged amount and backer count remain unverified here. [S1], [S5] |
| Demo | Creator-uploaded **Poncho Alpha** on Kongregate is dated **September 19, 2014** and explicitly asks for Kickstarter support. [S3] |
| Event | A playable EGX showing occurred during the campaign period. [S2], [S4] |

The campaign therefore had playable material during fundraising, not only concept art. The surviving Kongregate listing does not prove it was available on opening day, and this research did not execute its old browser build. Contemporary coverage also describes years of prior development. [S3], [S4]

## Publisher, release and commercial result

After the failed Kickstarter, **Rising Star Games** funded continued development. Release reporting confirms a November 2015 Steam/Humble launch rather than permanent cancellation. [S5] Hayes records **November 3, 2015** for Steam/PS4 and says sales fell below even pessimistic expectations; by January 2017, he reported no financial return from the game. This is a developer account, not audited worldwide gross revenue or proof that no production advances were ever paid. [S1]

The creator's postmortem contains serious complaints about the publisher relationship. A contemporary analysis records competing views about milestone delivery and payment. This dossier does **not** adjudicate those contractual disputes or attribute the entire sales result to the publisher. [S6]

Reception was not uniformly negative: the launch reviewer in GamingOnLinux enjoyed the game while identifying problems. An enthusiastic individual review is reception evidence, not financial validation. [S5]

## Case-specific observations

**Positive interpretation:** the history is useful precisely because a specific visual specialist, a playable alpha, regular communication and event exposure all existed. It is not an example of releasing an unseen concept without trying to build interest.

**Negative interpretation:** those activities achieved different outcomes. Recruiting an artist improved the production, the demo made the pitch testable, and publicity helped the project continue; none alone demonstrates a large paying audience. A later publishing agreement should not be retrospectively counted as a successful Kickstarter.

The creator's broad retrospective rules about release months, publishers and key requests remain personal conclusions, not universal rules or current Steam policy. No later lifetime recovery is established by the sources reviewed here.
