# MD proposal: Threewater March

[Library](../README.md) · [Labels](../README.md#reading-the-labels) · [Selection and scale](../selection-and-scale.md)

**Optional setting proposal, not canonical world history or implemented content.** Threewater March asks: _what kind of home can people make where rivers move, winters matter and nobody can solve everything alone?_ Its strongest loop is preparation → a useful expedition → a changed settlement → time to enjoy it. It can be gentle cooperative homesteading or demanding survival, chosen explicitly by the world creator.

The material palette takes a loose regional inspiration from medieval societies around 1000–1200: timber, stone, iron, sail, water power, swords, spears and bows. This is an invented region whose starting play omits firearms, not a claim that the entire historical world was then before gunpowder. Technology can advance through knowledge, resources and institutions. The starting period is an aesthetic and capability choice, not an immutable prohibition on invention.

**Estimate:** Play/Blend, High, **2/Extend** for a bounded settlement using supported ordinary actions; **3/New** for hydrology, structural loads, ecology or detailed weather not yet provided. Geographic and character descriptions are **1/Compose** authoring candidates. All described behavior still depends on supported capabilities.

## A place worth surviving in

Three rivers meet around a low wooded ridge. The upper river is cold and fast, the middle river powers mills, and the lower river spreads through reed marshes before reaching the sea. Flooding is both danger and fertility. A place can be beautiful without being benign: ice sings under bridges, cranes gather in wet fields and autumn smoke lies between the hills.

| Place or polity                                           | Proposed life and tension                                                                                                       | Build path                                           |
| --------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| **Bellwold**, a market village around a public oven       | A manageable starting home with a landing, forge, orchard and room for newcomers; the oven's fuel competes with winter reserves | 2/Extend: shared work and stock reservations         |
| **Ternmere**, downstream reed settlements                 | Boat-builders and fishers govern landing rights through assemblies; they resent upstream sediment and admire upstream tools     | 2/Extend: local rights and bounded trade             |
| **The Cairnward Holds**, upland hamlets                   | Sheep, stone and ironworking support scattered households; some favor toll-taking patrols, others guide travelers freely        | 2/Extend: distinct memberships and route commitments |
| **The Veyl Compact**, a neighboring league of three towns | Its road repairs and standardized measures are useful; its tax collectors want more authority than locals agreed to             | 2/Extend: limited services and negotiations          |
| **The Old Reed Road**, partly abandoned                   | Raised causeways lead to ruined storehouses now used by beekeepers and seasonal herders; history overlaps current claims        | 2/Extend: routes, occupancy and evidence             |

These are political communities, not fixed ethnic temperaments. A Ternmere person can hate boats; a Cairnward family can be hospitable or exploitative for reasons particular to them. Migration, marriage, apprenticeship and trade cross the map.

## History that leaves useful problems

**Seventy years ago: the Three Winters.** Consecutive difficult harvests led several villages to share seed stores. Some remember the pact as generosity, others as the moment large households gained leverage over smaller ones. The surviving ledgers can guide present reserves without settling the moral history.

**Twenty-six years ago: the Reed War.** Competing landing tolls escalated into a short, destructive conflict. Many people were displaced; others profited from repair contracts. The war matters through missing records, abandoned orchards and relationships, not an obligation to make every character traumatized or vengeful. Its peace agreement protects crossings but leaves maintenance responsibility vague.

**Eight years ago: the south river moved.** A flood made Bellwold's old mill nearly useless and created a new ferry opportunity. The celebrated rescue of trapped families involved a ferryman, several washerwomen and two rival soldiers; the public plaque names only the ferryman. That omission is one possible historical dispute.

**Now: a mild autumn and a worrying bank crack.** There is time to prepare. The opening need not begin with starvation or an attack. A newcomer can help survey the bank, build a home, join the orchard work or simply make supper with neighbors.

Alternative history: replace the war with a failed grand canal and a generation of debt. The same displaced households, disputed records and infrastructure questions remain, with a less martial tone. Both versions are **1/Compose** lore; modeled long-term demographic or military consequences would be separate **3/New** work.

Technology advances through projects: a better mill bearing, a foot-operated loom, a dependable kiln, a shared repair standard. A breakthrough should spread through teaching and demonstration rather than instantly granting every resident a new technology tier. Future firearms could be a separately selected development with material and social consequences; they are outside this starting proposal.

## Customs, belief and names

The **Keepers of the Open Table** understand hospitality as leaving room for a traveler, but households contribute differently. The **River Witnesses** maintain flood marks and remembrance stones; some speak of river spirits, others regard the same practices as duties to future neighbors. Neither tradition has to be mechanically supernatural. A minority of residents practice neither and still participate in festivals.

Bellwold names favor short stress-first forms and practical compounds: **Nella, Oren, Tavin; Reed, Venn, Bellwold, Ashford**. Downriver names allow open syllables and flowing sequences: **Sela, Iven, Neris; Ternmere, Velin**. Upland place names tend toward compact consonants and descriptive endings: **Darn, Kel, Rusk; Cairnward, Stonefold**. These are invented sound palettes, not claims to reproduce a real language. Personal, household and occupational names can differ; speakers use aliases rather than forcing one official form.

An old root **ter** can mean a bank or edge in one proposed local language, appearing in places now far from water. Newcomers might reinterpret it as a person's name. Retain a few meaningful patterns rather than constructing a dictionary before anyone can play. Language/lore is **1/Compose**; translation and recognition behavior would be **2/Extend**.

## Eight people to meet

The cast is **Play, High, 1/Compose** as authored biography. Each has an ordinary pleasure, a present aim and a relationship that can change. Behavioral follow-through—selective memory, work, refusal and commitments—is **2/Extend** and must be grounded in actual events. These are starting tendencies, not predetermined outcomes.

### Nella Reed, 42 — the millwright who keeps the bad parts

Nella grew up in a cheerful boat-building household and learned to diagnose machines by sound before she could read ledgers. She keeps failed bearings on a shelf because each taught her something. The river's move ruined her first independent mill, and she now wants a smaller repairable one instead of a monument to her ambition. She is generous with knowledge but defensive about estimates. She plays a poor flute with enthusiasm. Oren's neat accounts irritate her until they reveal where her fuel actually goes; Tavin is the apprentice she hopes will eventually surpass her.

### Oren Venn, 29 — the clerk who wants a song with his name in it

Oren's parents ran an orderly inn, and he associates accurate records with making strangers feel safe. He joined the Compact's toll office believing standard measures would prevent cheating. Discovering an unfair exemption shook that confidence without making him hate institutions. He wants to repair the crossing ledger and secretly writes comic songs about official forms. He can be fussy when embarrassed. Sela trusts his arithmetic but questions his loyalties; Nella could give his administrative skill a practical home if he leaves the office.

### Sela Tern, 51 — the ferry keeper who dislikes being called brave

During the south-river flood, Sela organized ropes and cooking while a more visible rescuer became famous. She does not want a statue; she wants the landing repaired and the women named on the plaque. She grew up loving fishing games with cousins and still cheats outrageously when teaching children to play them. She mistrusts grand promises but readily offers small help. Her adult son lives upstream by choice, not estrangement. She might support Oren's reform if he first listens to the people his records omitted.

### Tavin Kel, 18 — the apprentice with too many gardens

Tavin was raised by affectionate grandparents in an upland hamlet and came to Bellwold to learn metalwork. He keeps attempting balcony gardens, mostly successfully, and sees no reason a forge cannot have flowers. He wants a foot-operated clamp that lets more people work comfortably. His eagerness leads him to borrow materials before asking. Nella admires his imagination and insists he finish one project. He exchanges seed notes with Iven and has never decided whether he wants to return home or establish a mixed craft-and-garden shop.

### Iven Marr, 34 — the orchard keeper tracing an ordinary family

Iven was displaced as a child during the Reed War and raised by a household that treated him as their own. He is grateful and curious at once. He wants to learn his birth family's names, not discover that they were nobles. A bundle of graft labels may connect an abandoned orchard to them. He is patient with trees and impatient with vague testimony. He enjoys elaborate breakfasts. Sela remembers a relevant ferry passenger but not enough to be certain; Oren can help search records without promising an answer.

### Darn Rusk, 63 — the retired patrol captain who loves roofs

Darn spent years guarding roads and learned that a dry sleeping place often prevented more violence than a threat. He now builds shelters and wants to replace an old toll post with a travelers' hall. He can be overprotective and still speaks as if every suggestion were an order, then apologizes awkwardly. He adores carved roof birds. His pension depends on the Compact, complicating his criticism of its officials. Tavin's clamp could help his stiff hands, if Darn admits he wants it.

### Mira Neris, 37 — the host learning to close the door

Mira inherited the public oven's hospitality role from a beloved aunt and genuinely enjoys feeding people. Years of saying yes have left her little time to teach her daughter pottery. She wants a rota and another oven keeper, while fearing others will call this selfish. Her private pleasure is making tiny absurd animal cups. She and Darn cooperate easily but disagree about whether newcomers should earn their meals. A player can help negotiate a dependable kitchen rather than simply bringing her more ingredients to process.

### Pell Ash, 46 — the ambitious trader with a workable idea

Pell proposes financing the new bridge through a temporary toll. He understands seasonal demand, tells excellent travel stories and has helped households through lean years. He also wants exclusive landing rights longer than the bridge needs to repay itself. His parents lost a business through informal promises, making him overvalue enforceable control. He is a potential ally, opponent or partner rather than a fixed villain. Oren can test his numbers, Sela can challenge his terms, and a player can propose shared ownership that preserves a reasonable return.

## A first playable season

Begin with Bellwold, the orchard, one upland path and one downriver landing. The player can choose a home, meet three residents and help with a practical project before encountering major danger. Introduce rain and a missing tool before a severe storm. Let success produce a meal, a usable crossing or a comfortable room that stays useful.

Use [the wedding bridge](../combinations.md#the-wedding-bridge), [soup before the summit](../combinations.md#soup-before-the-summit) or [the harvest that tastes wrong](../combinations.md#the-harvest-that-tastes-wrong). Each supports building, conversation, material improvisation and several useful roles. Wolves, raiders and disputes can be present without making every stranger hostile; dangerous groups need particular aims and alternatives to extermination.

**Anti-boredom and resilience:** solved meals and maintenance become routines; cold spells have warning and shelters; villages retain seed reserves; wildlife has refuges; conflict has retreat and repair paths. Model a few crop and weather relationships, not a complete agricultural science. Prevent self-feeding material loops, infinite training through damage and toll monopolies that remove every route. These are proposed world defaults; a harsher variant should declare its consequences clearly.

Open choices before adoption: severity of bodily needs, lethal versus forgiving injury, detailed versus coarse seasons, multiplayer property agreements, and whether distant settlements are authored snapshots or bounded aggregates. None is decided by this lore.
