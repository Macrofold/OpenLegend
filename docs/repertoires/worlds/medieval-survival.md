# MD proposal: Threewater March

[Library](../README.md) · [Labels](../README.md#reading-the-labels) · [Selection and scale](../selection-and-scale.md) · [Design foundation](../design-foundation.md)

**Optional setting proposal, not canonical world history or implemented content.** Threewater March is a medieval survival and adventure world about **winning a place in a beautiful, dangerous borderland**. Build a home, hunt, learn a trade, explore abandoned works, fight for pay or conviction, acquire better equipment, and choose who gains power over the river crossings. Knights want land and renown, raiders want plunder, merchants want profitable control, households want another good winter, and these purposes can collide.

Home, work and expeditions feed one another. At home, cultivate a garden, make a useful thing, trade, train, form a relationship or enjoy the fair. Expeditions follow **prepare → venture out → fight, bargain, explore or improvise → bring something consequential home → recover, improve and choose what comes next**. A finished room, successful harvest, discovered route and hard-won victory offer different satisfying returns. The [shared design foundation](../design-foundation.md) connects these systems.

The material palette takes a loose regional inspiration from medieval societies around 1000–1200: timber, stone, iron, sail, water power, swords, spears and bows. This is an invented region whose starting play omits firearms, not a claim that the entire historical world was then before gunpowder. Technology can advance through knowledge, resources and institutions. The starting period is an aesthetic and capability choice, not an immutable prohibition on invention.

**Estimate:** Play/Blend, High. Lore, geography and biographies are **1/Compose** authoring candidates. A bounded home/work/expedition loop is provisionally **2/Extend** only where its constituent actions exist; differentiated combat, injury/capture, faction response and tactical opponents need a current capability audit and may be **3/New**. Detailed hydrology, structural failure, ecology and regional war are **3/New**, not prerequisites for the first slice. These are scope estimates, not evidence that the proposed game is implemented.

## A place worth surviving in

Three rivers meet around a low wooded ridge. The upper river is cold and fast, the middle river powers mills, and the lower river spreads through reed marshes before reaching the sea. Flooding brings both danger and fertile soil. Ice sings under bridges, cranes gather in wet fields and autumn smoke lies between the hills.

King Torren Cairn rules the upland Holds from High Cairn. His writ is contested in the March; the Compact and Ternmere retain their own governments. A royal progress can bring him to a borrowed hall near Bellwold without making the capital part of the starting map. Coastal ports and quarry cities in the cast's histories begin as background locations, reachable later only if selected.

These are political communities, not fixed ethnic temperaments. A Ternmere person can hate boats; a Cairnward family can be hospitable or exploitative for reasons particular to them. Migration, marriage, apprenticeship and trade cross the map.

## History that leaves useful problems

**Seventy years ago: the Three Winters.** Consecutive difficult harvests led several villages to share seed stores. Some remember the pact as generosity, others as the moment large households gained leverage over smaller ones. The surviving ledgers can guide present reserves without settling the moral history.

**Twenty-six years ago: the Reed War.** Competing landing tolls escalated into a short, destructive conflict. Many people were displaced; others profited from repair contracts. The war matters through missing records, abandoned orchards and relationships; later marriages, new work and shared celebrations have also shaped the intervening years. Torren took the upland crown during this war; Darn's defense of the Stone Ford and Torren's execution of seven truce guests left sharply different public memories. Its peace agreement protects crossings but leaves maintenance responsibility vague.

**Eight years ago: the south river moved.** A flood made Bellwold's old mill nearly useless and created a new ferry opportunity. The celebrated rescue of trapped families involved a ferryman, several washerwomen and two rival soldiers; the public plaque names only the ferryman. Sela organized the crews, while Edda's warning helped households move before the flood; the warning, the rescue and the later religious claims are different parts of the event. The plaque's omission is one possible historical dispute.

**Now: the crossing without a master.** A bank crack threatens the old ford just before the harvest fair. An iron-and-seed convoy has been taken near the Reed Road; its stolen toll seal can make the thieves look like lawful collectors to travelers who cannot verify it. The Veyl Compact offers troops in exchange for control of the repaired crossing. A Cairnward knight claims the same duty under an older oath, while the raiders prepare to sell the cargo downriver. Bellwold has something to lose before anybody attacks: a working forge, seed for spring, a beloved fair and the chance to govern its own landing.

The harvest fair is an occasion to play as well as the backdrop to the convoy crisis. Sell a first well-made tool, compare Iven's fruit, enter an archery contest, hear Oren's new comic song, race a model boat with Tess, dance or arrange a private meeting. Mira's oven, Tavin's gardens and Nella's workshop support ongoing projects and relationships. These activities have their own pleasures and useful results; a player can pursue them, take up the convoy opportunity or move between the two.

Alternative history: replace the war with a failed grand canal and a generation of debt. The same displaced households, disputed records and infrastructure questions remain, with a less martial tone. Both versions are **1/Compose** lore; the canal variant can still include greed, armed toll-taking and rival claimants. Modeled long-term demographic or military consequences would be separate **3/New** work.

Their commitments and responses follow their means, interests and knowledge; a persuasive appeal still needs something its recipient values. Torren's coastal war left him owing money to Compact creditors and merchants including Pell. His seal can validate Jessa's royal claim but cannot erase those contracts or Ternmere's independence. Edda's following reaches beyond any one shrine, though its donations and sworn protectors remain finite. The Reed Knives' three illicit tolls make Maer a familiar name among river traders; that is a different kind of reach from a royal court or a public prophet.

Power is local. A victory at the ford changes passage, income and reputation around these places; it does not instantly unite every household behind the winner or require a simulated continental war.

Technology advances through projects: a better mill bearing, a foot-operated loom, a dependable kiln, a shared repair standard. A breakthrough should spread through teaching and demonstration rather than instantly granting every resident a new technology tier. Future firearms could be a separately selected development with material and social consequences; they are outside this starting proposal.

### Gear, skill and rewards worth venturing for

The initial equipment palette includes a hunting bow with finite arrows, spear, hand axe, shield, knife, padded coat, helmet, rope, torch, bedroll and repair kit. Their differences should affect range, commitment, protection, burden and useful work. A spear controls a narrow approach; a shield permits a different advance through arrows; a bow rewards a chosen firing position but does not hold a gate alone. A rare sword can be an exciting prize without making all other tools obsolete.

Progression connects **practice, access, knowledge, equipment and social position**. Train a technique with Darn or Halven, bring Nella suitable fittings, learn a marsh approach from Sela, earn a patron's horse, recover an old watergate key, or establish a workshop that supplies your own expeditions. Do not make every character master everything immediately, or reward repetitive self-injury as the best training method.

Loot can be practical, luxurious, informational or political: good arrowheads, coin, a captured mail shirt, a fine cup, a route sketch, letters revealing a paid informer, or a banner whose capture humiliates its owner. Taking a defeated raider's equipment is different from robbing Iven's adopted family. The world can recognize that difference without requiring the player to choose the honorable act.

A first dangerous trip should offer something tangible enough to plan around. Its reward need not be a permanent upgrade: returning with a friend, good venison, a boast worth telling or an unobstructed route can satisfy different ambitions. A player may equally seek material power, glory or profit.

## Customs, belief and names

The **Keepers of the Open Table** understand hospitality as leaving room for a traveler, but households contribute differently. The **River Witnesses** maintain flood marks and remembrance stones; some speak of river spirits, others regard the same practices as duties to future neighbors. Neither tradition has to be mechanically supernatural. A minority of residents practice neither and still participate in festivals. The shrines also host weddings, teach records and offer company in grief. An oath can recruit fighters, a shrine can protect a wanted person, and a keeper can bless a claimant others regard as a usurper. Devotion, charity, ambition and fanaticism can coexist within a tradition. The player can respect, exploit, defy or fight its worldly power; supernatural intervention is not assumed.

Bellwold names favor short stress-first forms and practical compounds: **Nella, Oren, Tavin; Reed, Venn, Bellwold, Ashford**. Downriver names allow open syllables and flowing sequences: **Sela, Iven, Neris; Ternmere, Velin**. Upland place names tend toward compact consonants and descriptive endings: **Darn, Kel, Rusk; Cairnward, Stonefold**. These are invented sound palettes, not claims to reproduce a real language. Personal, household and occupational names can differ; speakers use aliases rather than forcing one official form.

An old root **ter** can mean a bank or edge in one proposed local language, appearing in places now far from water. Newcomers might reinterpret it as a person's name. Retain a few meaningful patterns rather than constructing a dictionary before anyone can play. Language/lore is **1/Compose**; translation and recognition behavior would be **2/Extend**.

## Fifteen people to meet

Bellwold sits inside other people's already eventful lives. These fifteen are a selection of residents, visitors and powers reachable through the March; choose a few for an opening. **Torren, Sela, Darn and Edda already have reputations across the river countries.** Their biographies distinguish what they did from what people say about them. A king's writ, a famous name and a gang's threat have different practical reach, and none supplies knowledge of an unwitnessed event.

**Cast defaults:** Play, High, **1/Compose** for authored people and history. Executing their skills, households, followers and changing relationships requires the corresponding supported capabilities; biography alone does not implement them.

### King Torren Cairn, 57 — the king who pawned his roads

Torren won the Cairnward crown at thirty-one during the Reed War. He broke a siege by bringing a supply column over the winter ridge, married into a rival house and ordered seven men seized and executed after inviting them to a reconciliation feast. The last act ended one succession claim and made his hospitality suspect for a generation. His roads, common weights and rebuilt upland markets are real achievements. So are the villages he taxed to pay for them. The Compact's creditors now hold toll concessions he granted during a later failed coastal campaign.

He still hunts, remembers an astonishing number of veterans by name and becomes absorbed in a good argument about masonry. The popular song says he fought a winter giant on the ridge; survivors remember exhausted pack animals and a commander who kept walking. His crown commands upland retainers and judges, not the independent landing league or the Compact. Jessa carries his warrant, Darn knows how his wars were won, and Pell owns part of his debt.

Meet him on a royal progress, hearing petitions in a granary that smells of apples. He wants the ford's income back and an heir who can inherit a solvent crown. A useful map, a military victory, a credible accusation or an offer to buy his debt can secure attention; flattery alone cannot release the rights he has already sold.

### Sela Tern, 51 — the Witch of Nine Landings

Sela has carried wool, fugitives, wedding parties, stolen cavalry pay and a dethroned prince on the same broad-bottomed boat. At twenty-seven she led nine overloaded craft through a besieged estuary by following old fishing channels in darkness. The resulting ballad gives her a bargain with the river. She gives the credit to a deaf reed cutter, good poles and the crews who obeyed a frightening order. Years later she organized the south-river flood rescue; the local plaque still names only its most visible ferryman. Fame elsewhere has not corrected the omission at home.

She has kept one former spouse as a trading partner and another as a competitive enemy, raised a son who now lives upstream, and lost a boat gambling on an early thaw. She enjoys loud company and cheats at fishing games with children. Oren can make her laugh; Maer remembers the free passage she gave him before he became a killer. Neither relationship gives its holder free use of her landing.

Find her arguing that a rich passenger's chest must stay behind because the wedding musicians need the space. She wants a sound landing, recognition for the flood crews and one more profitable coastward voyage. Hire her, race her, cross her or bring news of an old passenger: she has contacts and river judgment, not control of every waterway.

### Darn Rusk, 63 — the man who held the Stone Ford

Darn held the Stone Ford for three days during the Reed War with eighty-two exhausted fighters and a barricade of overturned carts. Refugees crossed behind them; Torren's arriving force found a road it could still use. Officers teach the defense. Darn remembers the seven people he sent to hold a bank he already knew he could not reinforce. Later, as a Compact patrol captain, he cleared Pike Mill during a levy revolt and marched its surrendered defenders away in chains. Two never returned. That was his order, not a false charge awaiting a sympathetic explanation.

He has commanded border escorts, trained Jessa's first instructor, buried two wives and raised his nephew Halven through several irregular winters. Retirement brought a pension, stiff hands and a genuine enthusiasm for building travelers' roofs. He can demonstrate a spear correction precisely and cannot march all day afterward. Some strangers salute him; others leave the room.

At the fair he may be teaching the watch when a former prisoner interrupts. He wants the old toll post made into a hall, his pension kept and the next levy kept out of another pointless bloodletting. A player can train with him, use his introductions, contest his account of Pike Mill or recruit him for a limited defense. His presence can steady veterans and alienate people whose families remember the same uniform differently.

### Edda Vey, 31 — the River Mouth

At twenty-three Edda read the flood marks, dreamed of a roof full of fish and announced that the south river would leave its bed. Most keepers told her to stop frightening the market. She rang the burial bell until people moved their children and stores. The river changed course that night. Thousands now know her as the River Mouth; they credit the dream, the records or a god according to their faith. The flood and her warning are established facts. Divine instruction is not.

She subsequently walked the river shrines, wrestled challengers at fairs and defended a burial ground against armed collectors. She once predicted a winter famine that did not come; families who sold stock at a loss still attend her sermons. Shrine elders court her following and dislike her habit of answering questions they intended to settle privately. Her staff work and shield discipline are learned skills. She laughs easily with Mira and keeps the oven keeper's absurd animal cups on a much-visited shrine shelf.

Meet her washing mud from her feet while petitioners argue over whose request is urgent. She wants sanctuary honored and custody of the river records kept outside a king's toll office. Jessa wants her blessing; Torren wants her beside him in public; a captive raider needs her protection. She can be courageous, mistaken or stubbornly right, and her followers may act beyond what she intended.

### Ser Jessa Cairn, 29 — the king's lance at the broken crossing

Jessa earned royal spurs after the Red Gate campaign, when she led dismounted fighters through a drainage cutting and captured the gatehouse before the main assault. She has won two major tournaments, lost a public duel to a caravan guard and spent eleven months as a valuable prisoner after her commander abandoned a coastal fort. Her ransom consumed a family estate her elder sister expected to inherit. They still exchange gifts; they do not agree that it was a fair price.

A cousin from a minor branch of Torren's house, Jessa commands a small royal escort and carries an old warrant for the crossing. She wants land and income enough to keep those fighters without continually begging the king. She enjoys her own ballads, practices seriously and understands the practical cost of a horse better than the cost of a peasant's lost harvest. She has hanged convicted raiders and accepted ransom from an equally murderous noble. Halven notices the distinction. Darn respects her technique and questions her judgment.

Encounter her drilling in a muddy orchard, royal pennon tied to an ordinary cart. She can offer a commission, teach a mounted approach, challenge an insult or seize an opportunity before permission arrives. Her protection is valuable; the taxes and obedience she expects are part of the bargain.

### Halven Rusk, 33 — captain of the Ash Company

Halven has fought for three employers who later fought one another. He learned roads with his uncle Darn, guarded salt caravans, spent two seasons in the red-quarry cities and helped a mercenary company arrest its own commander over stolen pay. He took command after the commander escaped. The Ash Company earned its name walking out of a burned billet with its wounded on salvaged doors. Halven later broke a contract to recover two captured comrades, costing him a rich patron and winning the loyalty of people who still fight beside him.

He has also collected an employer's debt from tenants who did not owe it, knowing perfectly well why the landlord had hired soldiers. The company needs wages between glorious stories. He keeps a plain axe, a carefully maintained spear and an embroidered cloak won at dice; recruits discover that he is a patient teacher and a ruthless negotiator. He likes Nella's estimates because they include failure, and distrusts Jessa's tendency to spend other people's courage.

Meet him selling a patron's confiscated table silver to settle arrears. Scout for him, join a flank, purchase an escort or compete for the same contract. He wants a defensible hall and continued work worth choosing. His people can desert, die or disagree; their names are more than a commander’s resource counter.

### Maer Fen, 38 — master of the Reed Knives

Maer began moving untaxed cloth after a levy dispute destroyed his family's landing. He learned customers, channels and the price of frightened silence, then killed the gang leader who tried to take his route. Over seven years the Reed Knives have burned rival boats, sold captured travelers back to their kin and collected false tolls from three stretches of river. He personally killed a surrendered informer in front of the gang. His grievance against the Compact is true; his decision to profit from other people's fear is equally true.

He keeps accounts, pays informants promptly and has twice survived betrayals by lieutenants. His younger cousin thinks the stories of his generosity tell the whole man. Maer encourages that belief. He races shallow boats for pleasure, dresses splendidly on feast days and once paid for the funeral of a washerwoman who had hidden him. Sela's remembered kindness may protect her boat while doing nothing for the passenger he wants.

Players might meet him at a fishing supper where stolen convoy goods are being priced in the next room. He wants the winter storehouses, the cargo's silver and recognition as a power that must be bargained with. Work for him, ransom someone, expose his buyers, divide his crew or fight him. Understanding his history does not make him surrender his advantages.

### Cassa Sorn, 46 — the thief who sold her partner

Cassa has emptied a bishop's traveling treasury, replaced a guildmaster's jeweled cup with a convincing copy and spent six years in a stone quarry prison. The arrest followed a failed burglary of a royal counting room. She sold her partner's hiding place for a reduced sentence; he was caught and lost a hand. He is alive elsewhere, and she has never sent the compensation she tells herself she will send when she can afford it.

Since release she has fenced small valuables through market towns, repaired old disguises and lived comfortably between occasional spectacular mistakes. She can read a household's routines and judge a lock she has actually inspected; she cannot open every door or know every secret. Oren once wrote letters for her in prison. Pell bought stolen plate from her and now pretends they first met over honest cloth. Tess has seen her perform coin tricks and regards her as a possible entertainment for the boat club.

At Bellwold she is appraising a wedding coffer while asking very good questions about the absent family. She wants back a hidden cache, a respectable room over a shop and a profitable way past the Compact's inspections. Hire her, catch her, learn from her or become the person she is prepared to sell this time. Some of her stories are funny because they really happened.

### Nella Reed, 42 — builder of six mills and one disaster

Nella apprenticed among boat builders, spent fourteen years repairing wheels and mills between the uplands and the salt coast, and supervised six complete installations. One supplied a besieged town after she converted its waterwheel to drive a lifting hoist. Another broke apart when a patron insisted on opening before she had replaced a cracked support; she signed the acceptance anyway. Two workers were injured. She paid for their care and still owns the signed document.

She made money, lost it rebuilding, married a cheerful cooper and established Bellwold's mill with a crew of her own. The changed river then stranded a technically sound building. Those are different failures, and she dislikes clients treating either as proof that she should work cheaply. Failed bearings fill a shelf; a poor flute fills the workshop at closing time. Her smaller replacement design is ready. Iron, water rights and an acceptable backer are not.

Meet her testing an ugly salvaged component while a royal messenger waits for a quotation. She can build, teach, repair or decline a commission that consumes her whole season. Tavin is her apprentice; Halven is a repeat customer; Jessa's offer of exclusive service threatens the independent workshop Nella has already spent a life establishing.

### Pell Ash, 46 — the merchant who has been rich three times

Pell's first fortune came from moving winter grain over a neglected upland road; a partner's disappearance took it. His second came from army supply contracts and ended in a foreign debtor's prison after his patron lost a war. He came home with a working knowledge of three trading tongues and rebuilt his business around iron, timber and rights to collect tolls. He now owns shares in two warehouses and part of Torren's road debt. His stories of caravans, dust and prison food are worth hearing even when he is delaying a question about price.

During a shortage he withheld grain until the price rose, then donated a public oven to the same district. Both transactions remain in the books. He has also financed unprofitable rescues of people he liked. Neither generosity nor greed reliably predicts his next deal. His adult daughter runs the coastal accounts and has refused his preferred marriage arrangement.

Find him inspecting a supposedly worthless damaged cargo, or losing loudly at a game he understands very well. He wants the new crossing's revenue, better relations with his daughter and one undertaking that survives him. He can bankroll Jessa, pay Halven or underwrite a local coalition; Cassa and Oren know different parts of the history behind his respectable signature.

### Mira Neris, 37 — the oven keeper who fed both sides

Before inheriting her aunt's oven, Mira cooked for a coastal inn, a merchant caravan and the trailing kitchens of two opposing armies in successive campaigning seasons. She learned to feed a hundred people, bargain over spoiled flour and recognize the silence before soldiers start a fight. At the end of the second campaign she hid three deserters among returning kitchen workers. One later stole her savings. Another still sends fish every autumn. She did not discover a single rule that explains both men.

Back in Bellwold she rebuilt the public oven after a chimney fire, married a traveling potter, separated amicably and raised Tess between the oven yard and her father's seasonal visits. Her small animal cups are a serious family joke: customers deliberately ask for impossible beasts. She enjoys courtship, a full table and quiet work with her daughter. She also ejects guests who have exhausted her patience. Darn knows her old employers; Edda trusts her enough to ask for something she may refuse.

An encounter begins with two old soldiers recognizing one another at supper. Mira has already stopped one fight and wants to finish baking. A player can take a shift, mediate, recruit her provisioning skill or ask her to hide somebody whose pursuers she knows. A dependable rota would improve an established life, not finally give her one.

### Iven Marr, 34 — the orchard keeper with three harvest books

Displaced at eight during the Reed War, Iven grew up in a family of growers who kept teaching through poor years. He grafted his first surviving tree at twelve, took seasonal work in three valleys and returned with varieties his adoptive parents distrusted until they tasted the fruit. He married a basket maker, helped her raise a younger brother and was widowed after six good years. His three harvest books record weather, prices and the meals they cooked for pickers; one remains stained with the cider they spilled celebrating a profitable season.

He has put out an orchard fire, argued successfully against a lord's compulsory purchase and made one expensive mistake planting a fashionable variety that nobody locally wanted. He is a practiced grower with customers and an opinion about every breakfast. He still wants his birth family's names. Graft labels recovered near the raiders' refuge may offer evidence; they are not a promise of noble ancestry.

Meet him serving breakfast to buyers while declining a prestigious order that would strip his own nursery. He knows Tavin's grandparents, trusts Sela's memory within its limits and resents Pell's prices without denying that Pell once carried his crop to market. Help recover labels, buy good trees or simply spend an afternoon in a place somebody already knows how to love.

### Oren Venn, 29 — the toll clerk with a forger's hand

Oren left his parents' inn with a traveling play company at sixteen. He wrote comic verses, copied contracts and eventually forged a permit to get the troupe past a closed border. Paid requests followed. At twenty-two he was caught selling duplicate travel papers and spent eighteen months imprisoned, where Cassa taught him to recognize a buyer preparing to disappear. He obtained his present toll-office job through a clerk who valued his arithmetic and knew exactly what he had done.

He has since caught a counterfeit cargo bond that would have ruined several carriers and concealed an exemption for the family who sheltered him after release. Accurate records and selective dishonesty coexist in his career. His songs have reached fairs he has never attended; the most popular makes a pompous king recognizable without naming him. Torren knows the chorus. Oren would prefer not to learn whether he finds it amusing.

The player may first see him perform, then discover the singer is the official inspecting their baggage. He wants a secure post, a collected songbook and the stolen toll seal recovered before someone uses it to frame him. His eye is useful evidence, not infallible authentication. Sela trusts his sums, Nella needs his contracts and Cassa knows how to ask for a favor he should refuse.

### Tavin Kel, 18 — the apprentice who brought the wrong prize home

Tavin's grandparents raised him among upland sheep, gardens and tools that were repaired more often than replaced. At fourteen he restored a broken handcart for a neighbor and spent the payment on seedlings. At last year's fair he entered a forging contest, came second and won a foot-operated clamp because the winner preferred the silver. He has since altered it to help Darn's stiff hands and Nella's fitting work. The useful version is a joint achievement, despite the increasingly heroic story he tells at supper.

He has traveled as far as the coast once, disliked the lodging, loved the unfamiliar plants and returned with a debt for materials he had borrowed without asking. He is skilled at a few things, inexperienced at many and delighted by the possibility of changing that. The flowers around his fair stall are grown, not a personality label. Iven swaps seeds with him; Tess negotiates fiercely for his offcuts.

Halven wants clamps and fittings for the company and offers a place on its next journey. Nella needs her apprentice for the new mill. Tavin can finish his orders, disappoint somebody, stay or leave. He is one of the people whose larger life is beginning, in a cast containing plenty of people who can tell him what that has cost them.

### Tess Neris, 12 — captain of the Ditch Navy

Tess has lived in Bellwold all her life, spent three summers traveling to fairs with her potter father and learned the oven's busy hours well enough to arrange her own small enterprises around them. She and three friends race model boats along the drainage channels. Last spring they found a blocked culvert before a downpour flooded the schoolyard; Tess still produces the teacher's thank-you note when someone calls the club a nuisance. She once blamed a younger friend for a broken jug and has only partly admitted what happened.

She wants the biggest model sail at the harvest fair, a visit to the coast and permission to build a proper club shelter. She likes Cassa's coin tricks, steals useful wood from Tavin's scrap pile after asking too vaguely and knows which adults keep promises. Her knowledge is local and incomplete: a cart moved after dark is something she saw, not proof that she understands the convoy plot.

Meet her conducting a furious hearing about a missing boat while trying to conceal that she misses her father. Players can join a race, mend a sail, believe or investigate a report, or negotiate use of a corner of Mira's yard. She has school, friends, rivalries, chores and adults responsible for her; she is neither a miniature mercenary nor a hostage waiting to start somebody else's story.

## A first playable season

**Bound the first slice:** Bellwold and its orchard, the ford and damaged mill, one upland path, the Old Reed Road storehouses and one Ternmere landing. Use the fifteen named people selectively, a few authored encounter groups, a short equipment ladder and finite stocks. Distant powers can arrive through messages and bounded forces. Full hydrology, ecological simulation, dynasty modeling and army economies are later options, not conditions for making this map fun.

### Opening: the missing convoy and the harvest fair

1. **Arrive with a role and a small success.** Choose an initial kit and connection: newcomer with useful hands, bow-carrying hunter, apprentice, trader or trained fighter. Eat at Mira's oven, practice with Darn, take a modest job or pursue an immediate rumor. The player learns movement, inspection, talk and one meaningful tool or fighting action before being asked to save a region.
2. **See what is threatened.** The convoy's surviving driver reaches the fair preparations. Nella lacks iron, Iven needs seed and a captive is still missing. A recovered arrow, damaged cart and witness account reveal different pieces of the ambush. Oren identifies the suspect toll seal. The game does not tell everyone the raiders' secret position.
3. **Choose a purpose and preparation.** Recover the cargo for Bellwold, take Jessa's commission, sell scouting information, hire Halven, infiltrate to free the captive, or seek a deal with the raiders. Prepare food, arrows, a useful loadout and a route. A second character can remain home to repair equipment or organize supply without becoming irrelevant.
4. **Act before a stated opportunity closes.** Maer intends to move the cargo downriver after the fair's third evening. That deadline is an authored, discoverable fact, not a hidden timer; a world creator can tune its pace. Delay changes where the cargo and captives go and who occupies the ford. It does not require the whole world to end if one objective is missed.
5. **Return to a materially changed home.** Deliver, keep or sell actual recovered goods. Pay hired help or face the debt. Rest, improve a weapon or room, feast, argue over credit, visit a loved person or plan an expedition into the old storehouses. A victory can be enjoyed before the next pressure arrives.

The storm and bank crack create a second route to significance. Repairing a crossing can enable a flank or evacuation as well as commerce. Building, negotiation and skillful battle each offer a substantial way to pursue the crossing's future. Meanwhile a successful market stall or a completed household project remains an achievement of its own.

### The ford fight: skill and preparation both matter

A raider lookout watches the open approach, two spear fighters hold the narrow bank and the stolen carts obstruct a direct rush. The player can observe their routine, take a longer marsh route, lure a guard from support, approach under a recognized seal, arrange a distraction, use a bow from cover or fight through the choke. Those alternatives require actual capability and evidence; prose ingenuity does not guarantee success.

Committed attacks create openings. Reach, facing, cover, guard, finite ammunition and retreat routes should provide a readable small combat vocabulary before many weapon tiers are added. Distinct opponents test different choices: a shielded blocker, an exposed archer, a flanking knife fighter and a commander trying to escape with the payroll. Supported tactical orders or assistance can reduce execution demands without replacing all uncertainty with a guaranteed victory.

A cart set alight may force defenders out while destroying valuable cargo and threatening the captive. Cutting a tether may recover a boat or strand an ally. Taking a wounded enemy prisoner costs attention and transport; killing that enemy is a different possible outcome. Do not manufacture a universally best, painless resolution that gives every reward.

The existing [wedding bridge](../combinations.md#the-wedding-bridge), [soup before the summit](../combinations.md#soup-before-the-summit) and [harvest that tastes wrong](../combinations.md#the-harvest-that-tastes-wrong) remain useful domestic, diplomatic and environmental situations. They share people, routes and resources with the wider setting while providing different reasons to gather, explore and create.

### Pace, limits and variants

Solved meals and ordinary maintenance can become dependable routines. Initial preparations and rare emergencies still demand attention. Dangerous regions have evidence and reasons to visit; enemy strength is learnable enough that retreat, a better loadout or hiring help can be intelligent choices. Finite stocks and explicit recovery limits prevent farming the same raider, self-harm training and resource duplication from becoming dominant strategies.

Loss severity, time during absence, player-versus-player violence and shared-property damage are declared world/session choices. A gentler version can emphasize patrols, wildlife and recoverable defeat; a harsher version can permit lasting injury, death, burnt homes and conquest. Use the shared [world tone and scope](../design-foundation.md#world-tone-and-scope) guidance to establish these choices; the selected consequences follow actual events.

The first slice needs observable aftermath, not a model call for each background household or simulated water molecule. Record important commitments, completed projects, discoveries, deaths, ownership changes, known betrayals, cargo transfers and route changes; aggregate distant trade and political pressure. Expanding from a local toll conflict to regional war is a separate design decision with new costs.

Open choices before adoption: bodily-needs severity; real-time versus more assisted tactical execution; injury, capture and death/recovery rules; coarse versus detailed seasons; shared-player property terms; and whether distant settlements are authored snapshots or bounded aggregates. None becomes an engine-wide law through this proposal.
