# MO proposal: Linden Reach

[Library](../README.md) · [Labels](../README.md#reading-the-labels) · [Selection and scale](../selection-and-scale.md) · [Design foundation](../design-foundation.md)

**Optional setting proposal, not canonical world history or implemented content.** Linden Reach is a contemporary river-and-rail city where people pursue love, money, recognition, security, pleasure and power through work, households, friendships and rival institutions. Its central question is: _what life will you make, what will it cost, and what will you defend or compromise to keep it?_ Domestic life, romance, career competition, public achievement, investigation and crime are substantial possible directions. The selected opening gives the player a foothold and connects their ambitions to particular neighbors, opportunities and pressures.

**Estimate:** Play/Blend, High, **2/Extend** for one household, several workplaces and a small neighborhood; **3/New** for modeled city services, institutions and economy. This is a later and more demanding world family if those interactions must execute broadly. An attractive city description does not establish support for its interlocking systems. Setting and cast content are **1/Compose** candidates.

## A city with more than one good life

Linden Reach began around a freight harbor and grew along two rail lines. Its old industrial quarter now contains repair shops, apartments, studios and a public college. The river is cleaner than it was a generation ago, but access to its banks remains uneven. Some streets are lively, others quiet; neither is the correct way to live.

The wider country, **the Republic of Edrin**, is optional background. It can provide common law, currency and rail standards without requiring a simulated national government. Neighboring cities **Harbor Vale** and **Eastmere** are destinations for visits, trade and career changes, represented first as bounded locations.

## Recent history and overlapping communities

**Forty years ago**, harbor automation reduced some jobs while creating others. A repair cooperative formed when workers pooled severance money to keep a machine shop open. Its founding story emphasizes solidarity; surviving accounts also show arguments, mistakes and help from people later omitted.

**Seventeen years ago**, severe flooding displaced several blocks. A temporary housing complex became permanent through residents' organizing. Some people want it renovated, others want the promised replacement homes, and some fear losing the relationships built there.

**Eleven years ago**, the nine-day rail-and-depot stoppage known as the **Silent Week** won a safer shift system and restored dismissed workers, but its settlement closed the Willow maintenance spur. Idris Saye negotiated the agreement; some families still remember a victory that cost them their livelihood.

**Nine years ago**, a redevelopment scandal exposed by Ana Velin forced resignations and a new election. Iona Vey became mayor and has since won two further terms. The same year, Leon Vale consolidated rival protection crews into the Quay Circle. These changes affected different institutions; neither gave one person control of the whole city.

**Six years ago**, the public college opened an adult-learning program, bringing new students into the Tileworks. This created friendships and businesses alongside noise, rent pressures and resentment about whose labor makes the district fashionable.

**Now**, a disused tram shed may become a market, a performance venue, housing or a combined project. Its future is one optional center of play. Nobody must solve the city's entire housing economy before opening a café or making friends.

Different migration waves can contribute food, names, faith and professional networks. Write specific families and institutions rather than assigning a temperament to an ethnic group. Alternative setting: a smaller coastal town with the same social relationships and fewer modeled services. Both versions support contemporary technology without assuming identical national politics.

Phones, computers and social media can exist as fictional interfaces to admitted messages and services. They do not grant real-world network or account access. For the first slice, rent, wages and purchases can use a small understandable model; mortgages, derivatives, national macroeconomics and every tax form are unnecessary unless a creator specifically chooses that simulation question.

A **repair library**, a **shared cold pantry** and a **neighborhood shift board** remain useful inventions. So do a distinctive stage installation, a training device, a profitable product or a tool that reveals concealed evidence. Players can design services, start a business, make art, pursue status or choose a dependable modest job that leaves time for friends; none is the universally preferred life.

## Desires, power and opposition

Linden Reach connects domestic comfort, personal ambition and public life. A resident may want a lover, a championship, an admired house, revenge, artistic recognition, freedom from debt, a profitable business or authority over other people. Some conflicts can be negotiated; some involve deliberate exploitation, incompatible claims or a person who prefers winning to being fair. The city's pleasure includes wit, flirtation, music, taste, skill and the satisfaction of succeeding at something difficult.

These are fictional organizations with particular members, not claims about real cities or a universal model of politics. Organizations can create useful public goods as well as harm. Their plans advance through authored events and bounded activities, not an omniscient city simulator.

Progression means more than earning money: expertise, access, a reputation, trusted relationships, an audience, ownership and institutional influence each change what the player can attempt. Competing for a contract, winning a bout, discovering a fraud or composing a performance can provide a satisfying evening with a visible result. Use a few legible systems for the chosen role rather than requiring every player to become a city administrator.

## Culture, belief and naming

The **River Lights evening** celebrates flood recovery with floating lantern displays in safe designated basins, music and recollections. The **Open Bench Saturday** invites people to bring broken objects to the cooperative. Neither needs attendance rewards. Residents may follow several religions, attend a secular choir, observe family rituals or prefer private reflection. Proposed institutions include **the House of Welcome**, a faith community that operates a meal service, and **the Common Room**, a nonreligious mutual-aid space that sometimes partners with it.

Street names preserve industry and landscape—**Kiln Row, Willow Steps, Morrow Quay**—while newer developments adopt polished names residents may mock. Personal naming should reflect family choices and migrations. The proposed cast mixes short given names with varied surname rhythms; these are invented individuals, not representatives of real cultures. A later author could build specific fictional language histories around families without assigning all speakers the same values.

## Fifteen people to meet

**Cast defaults: Play, High, 1/Compose** for authored life histories; **2/Extend** for executing schedules, commitments, selective memory and relationship changes. These are people encountered partway through their lives. Four already shape Linden Reach well beyond their own households: Iona through office, Tomas through ownership, Leon through criminal power and Lian through a nationally recognized body of work. Other reputations have narrower but real constituencies. Their resources give them specific capabilities, not command over every institution.

The histories below are proposed authored facts; explicitly identified stories, boasts and rumors need not be true. An embodied player learns them through encounters, records, places and people who have different reasons to speak. Select a handful for an opening, with the others available through later relationships and locations. They do not all wait in one courtyard for the player to arrive.

### Iona Vey, 63 — the mayor who knows which promises survived

Seventeen years ago, Iona was the councilor who obtained keys to empty freight offices and opened them to flood-displaced households before the owners agreed. People still call her **the woman who opened the doors**. She did secure permanent homes for hundreds. She also signed the settlement that left Ana's block in supposedly temporary housing, then defended it as the agreement the city could afford. Both things happened; the heroic nickname leaves out the second.

Now in her third four-year term as mayor, she holds a seven-seat coalition on a thirteen-seat council. She can direct her office, negotiate a budget and nominate officials; she cannot spend an unapproved appropriation or order a court to close Sera's case. Idris's support helped elect her. Tomas's money helped restore her majority after she broke with a former ally. She remembers which favor each man thinks she owes.

Iona wants the tram shed open before a regional election that might carry her beyond Linden Reach. At her weekly market surgery she can be funny, impatient and startlingly attentive. Players can bring a proposal, organize against her or gain an introduction through actual work. On Sunday she dances badly with her husband, who has been teasing her about the same turn for thirty-six years.

### Leon Vale, 43 — the man who made the Quay kneel

Leon began at club doors in Harbor Vale, served two years for an assault, and returned to Linden Reach with savings, contacts and a talent for deciding who was frightened of whom. Nine years ago he broke three competing protection crews into the Quay Circle. He ordered the killing of his partner Anton Mire after a fight over missing money, blamed a rival and acquired Anton's warehouse leases. The rival's surviving brother still lives in Eastmere. This is authored history, although the public version is an unsolved disappearance.

Leon now owns two clubs, controls three storage leases and commands a dozen dependable operators. His reputation travels farther than his actual ability to enforce a promise. He cannot buy every officer or prevent a member from defecting. He is an excellent host, remembers musicians' first paid sets and delights in making a room laugh; he also enjoys watching someone who refused him ask permission to continue trading.

He wants the tram shed's nighttime concessions, Tomas's financial respectability and Kira's name over his door. Priya has refused him. Sera is tracing the Circle's assault on a stallholder. Players may meet him over supper, take his work, steal from him, negotiate or help destroy his position. Hospitality is real; so are the orders he gives after dinner.

### Lian Orrel, 61 — the woman whose blue belongs to everyone

At Open Bench, Lian is sometimes mistaken for the assistant. Then a visitor recognizes the hand behind Eastmere Central's blue concourse, Harbor Vale's sea-colored chapel and the Linden flood memorial's bowls. Three decades of commissions made her famous enough that visitors bring museum postcards for her to sign. The popular story says she invented her signature glaze in one miraculous overnight firing. She actually developed it with two studio colleagues over seven years, and names them whenever a journalist will leave the names in.

She apprenticed in a sanitary-ware factory, crossed the country restoring old tilework and returned to open the Tileworks studio. After the flood, she rebuilt its kiln and kept four apprentices employed by making restaurant crockery for eighteen months. Her work includes grand beauty and excellent everyday cups. She is comfortable with both, less comfortable admitting that an apprentice can now outthrow her.

A lucrative Kest commission pays current wages while implicating the studio in a redevelopment Ana opposes. Lian wants new contract terms and a permanent public workshop. Her national standing can attract donors; it cannot make Tomas surrender his land. Idris receives lessons, Vic receives irritatingly precise criticism, and city pigeons receive elaborate invented biographies. Players can commission, learn, collect, collaborate or simply sit beside her while she turns a lump of clay into something worth keeping.

### Tomas Kest, 45 — the man who bought the view

Tomas's first business converted a Harbor Vale boardinghouse into flats. It failed, cost his brother most of his savings and taught Tomas to make sure somebody else shared the next risk. He returned to Linden, assembled riverfront parcels and financed the footbridge that residents now use every day. He also cleared seventy-one households from the Glassbank estate to sell its uninterrupted river view. Some accepted useful compensation; others lost homes under deadlines he deliberately refused to extend.

Kest Development now owns six substantial sites, including the warehouse controlling the tram shed's easiest delivery approach. The shed itself remains publicly leased. Investors and lenders can force sales, and Iona can deny permissions; his reputation as the man who owns the river is an exaggeration he finds commercially useful.

He wants the shed project to become his admired masterpiece and his least patient investor to sell out. Lian's work gives the scheme beauty; Leon offers income Tomas would prefer not to examine closely. He sponsors chamber concerts, remembers every bad review of his buildings and genuinely loves good rooms. Players can tour one with him, secure enforceable terms, work for him or organize the people his plans inconvenience. He already knows how to make a city change. The argument is over who gets to live in the result.

### Idris Saye, 48 — the man who stopped the trains

Eleven years ago, Idris led the nine-day rail-and-depot stoppage remembered as **the Silent Week**. His crews won a safer shift system and reinstated dismissed drivers. He also accepted the closure of the Willow maintenance spur to get the agreement signed. Workers there lost jobs while the victory photograph put him at the center. Osei took that photograph; Idris keeps a print without cropping out the two men refusing to smile.

He later negotiated schedules in Eastmere, survived a bitter union election and came back to depot work when the regional office offered him another term. His members trust his preparation, not every judgment. He can call a meeting and mobilize substantial support; he cannot promise how people will vote. Iona wants his endorsement, Mara wants him to stop treating her competence as spare capacity, and a long-time ally has concealed an unsafe shortcut to save a job.

Idris wants a workshop in the tram shed and one evening a week when nobody needs his judgment. Lian is teaching him ceramics; his disastrous first bowl has pride of place at home. Encounter him over lunch, bargain across his constituency or work a shift beside him. He loves an argument, but a quiet person saying “you already decided” can still stop him short.

### Mara Venn, 33 — the electrician who brought the lights back

Mara spent her twenties following repair contracts from Eastmere's salt-corroded tram lines to Harbor Vale's storm-damaged docks. Four years ago she joined Rafe's crew for a three-week emergency restoration outside Eastmere. When the contractor withdrew from the final district, she stayed with local technicians and finished its clinic supply. They did the work together. The newspaper photographed only her, and she still sends interviewers to the people it omitted.

Her current depot colleagues know both the excellent electrician and the woman who once walked off a shift after a manager called her band a hobby. The band has released an album, played a disastrous coastal tour and returned with one devoted following and no money. Jo designs its posters. Mara wants the next record finished, mornings back and an apprentice able to replace her without being exploited in turn.

She trains with Kira, argues with Idris about overtime and mistrusts Rafe's claim that every contract is simply a job. Meet her under a tram or after a loud set. She can teach, repair, travel or join a practical scheme, but competence does not make her automatically available. A perfectly ordinary day off is something she has already earned.

### Rafe Calder, 54 — the contractor who always came back

Rafe has been an army sergeant, a storm surveyor, a convoy escort and the man hired when a distant worksite stops answering. At the Red Stair dam collapse he brought fourteen stranded workers through the flooded service tunnels; at the Kerran oil concession he commanded a paid security crew that forcibly cleared a village. Nine people died in that clearance. He signed the report calling it a successful operation and still argues that the client, not the contractor, chose the objective.

The adventurers' story is that Rafe never lost anybody. His own notebooks contain names that disprove it. What remains true is his experience: he can read a deteriorating expedition, keep equipment functioning and make people act while frightened. His present business has two trucks, a riverboat and four crew members, not a private army. A lawsuit and an aging engine keep its finances precarious.

He wants the contract to retrieve survey records from an abandoned upriver installation before a rival reaches it. Mara once worked alongside him; Osei photographed the returning Kerran crew; Tomas occasionally buys his services. Players meet him unloading battered gear, can hire or accompany him, dispute his account or refuse his price. He expects his crew at dinner when they are in port and cooks enough for whoever they bring. He tells the Red Stair story freely; Kerran requires a more persistent question.

### Sera Holt, 38 — the investigator who broke the Northglass case

Sera spent fourteen months proving that two deaths at the Northglass packing works were deliberate. The convictions made her career and left her with colleagues who remember how much work she insisted they redo. Earlier, on a Harbor Vale assignment, she helped find a missing child living under another name; that now-grown person sends her an awkward birthday card each year. Her office keeps the packing-works verdict on display; the card goes in a drawer with her family's letters.

She also pursued an innocent freight broker for months after a witness identification began to fail. She eventually disclosed the contradiction and dropped the theory. The broker remembers the lost business; she remembers doing the right thing too late. Her marriage ended during those years. Her friendships, tennis games and new relationship have lives beyond the police station.

Now she wants a defensible case against the Quay Circle's ordered assault and the promotion that would let her choose future investigations. An impatient superior and a frightened witness want different things. Ana can find records, Osei remembers buildings and Vic knows how people conceal a stolen object, but none is an unlimited source. Meet Sera revisiting a shop others forgot. Players can assist, challenge, evade or expose her through what actually happened.

### Kira Dane, 29 — the champion fighting her way back

Kira won the Eastmere regional belt at twenty-five after twenty-two professional bouts. Her victory was a hometown celebration, and children still copy the little shoulder roll she made before the final round. She lost the belt in her next defense, fought twice while hiding a hand injury and spent two years driving deliveries while recovering. She remembers the loss as clearly as the win and dislikes fans who insist she was robbed.

A supportive father taught her to enjoy training and take opponents seriously. Her old training partner is now the champion she wants to face again. Kira can be generous with a promising beginner and cruelly impatient with someone who mistakes enthusiasm for preparation. Mara trains with her, Priya feeds the early gym crowd and Vic's flirtation competes with his need for the same sponsor.

She needs backing before a qualifying comeback bout. Leon offers enough money to matter and a venue relationship that would matter afterward. Kira wants another title, an independent training gym and the simple exhilaration of being very good in front of a crowd. Players can spar, support, manage, compete or attend. Losing again is possible; so is watching her earn something neither pity nor a charming biography could provide.

### Ana Velin, 39 — the librarian who brought down a mayor

At twenty-two, Ana carried her family's ruined documents out of the flood housing. At thirty, as a local reporter, she traced the sale of its promised replacement site to a company secretly owned by the deputy mayor. Publication forced resignations and a new election. Iona won that election, and the replacement homes still did not appear. Ana learned that exposing a lie and building a house are different accomplishments.

She left reporting after the paper closed its local desk, qualified as a librarian and stayed in Linden rather than take a distant newsroom job. She has a profession she likes, friends she trusts and no desire to spend every meal explaining institutional failure. Her desk is gloriously untidy. Her defense of a melodramatic radio serial can become more heated than a council argument.

Ana wants the housing history preserved and the library's cooling room funded. She values Lian's influence while mistrusting closed-door bargains; Sera sometimes needs a record Ana is entitled to provide and sometimes asks for more than that. Players can encounter her in a reading group before discovering the old front page. Research, gossip and public action may follow, but she also appreciates someone returning a book and asking how the ridiculous serial ends.

### Osei Rill, 72 — the driver who kept everybody's old city

Osei will politely ask a stranger to move out of his photograph, then talk to them for an hour after the light has gone. For thirty-nine years he delivered parcels through Linden, Harbor Vale and the towns between. He married a railway cook, helped raise two daughters and carried a camera because shop windows amused him. His pictures accumulated weddings, strikes, changing signs and people who thought they would only be in town for a month. During the flood he used his delivery van to move neighbors' furniture until the engine failed. He still calls that his most expensive good idea.

His wife died several years ago. He has friends, flirtations, routines and a life that is not waiting for rescue from loneliness. He wants an exhibition that looks like the city he remembers and digital-editing lessons from Jo. Ana keeps challenging captions where recollection has become certainty. His Silent Week photograph includes Idris and the men the agreement disappointed; another shows a property favor that benefited an old friend at somebody else's expense.

Meet him patiently waiting for reflected light on a window. Players can help mount the show, identify a face, accompany a former route or disagree about a caption. Most of his pictures contain no secret. A few contain something somebody now wishes had never been ordinary enough to photograph.

### Priya Dalen, 31 — the cook who has fed three different cities

Priya has cooked for a governor's reception in Harbor Vale, a packed overnight canteen in Eastmere and a Linden wedding where the newlyweds reconciled a month after canceling it. She remembers which food people actually finished. She co-owned an ambitious restaurant at twenty-seven; when her partner expanded before the first site could pay its bills, she sold her share, settled her staff's wages and returned home with considerably less money than her family imagined.

She now wants a lunch counter she can close at five, a shared cold pantry and time with her eleven-year-old nephew Niko. His mother works changing rail-service shifts and shares the school pickups with Priya. Priya has already run kitchens and built a business. This smaller one is a deliberate choice about the life her competence should buy.

Leon offered money and influence. She refused after seeing what his help cost another proprietor. Idris can help with staffing if he stops making every lunch a meeting; Mara and Kira are likely regulars. Players meet her catching a supplier's short delivery while telling an excellent story. Away from the stove she repairs old board games, keeps score mercilessly and enjoys a long afternoon that has nothing to do with work.

### Vic Orrel, 27 — the showman with other people's watches

Vic, Lian's nephew, has worked fairgrounds, hotel lounges and street festivals in six cities. His best act makes a roomful of strangers believe they have individually outwitted him, then reveals they have been helping build the same illusion. He learned the craft from an aging touring magician and learned theft from a partner who used the audience as cover. At twenty-two he was convicted of stealing watches from wedding guests in Harbor Vale. He served his sentence and paid restitution; one victim still calls Lian whenever his name appears on a poster.

He continues taking occasional illicit jobs. He takes pride in both the entertainment and the theft, while expecting very different reactions when somebody notices his skill. Leon knows both businesses. Sera knows the conviction but not every later act. Lian will lend him equipment, never her name as a guarantee.

Vic wants the River Lights centerpiece commission, then a tram-shed venue with his name above it. His staged musical act really could beat Jo's installation. He is attracted to Kira and competing for her sponsor. Meet him returning a watch after a dazzling trick, then negotiate the boundary between performance and theft. Players can admire, employ, collaborate, expose or outplay him; charm does not settle which promises he will keep.

### Jo Mercer, 24 — the designer who put a parade on the wrong street

Jo once built an enormous illuminated river creature from discarded signs for a student parade. When the permitted route abruptly ended at a sponsor's private party, they led the creature and half the musicians down Kiln Row instead. The unauthorized detour became a neighborhood favorite, cost the organizers a deposit and produced Jo's first paid design commissions. Their work has already delighted people and inconvenienced people who had a reasonable claim to their street.

Jo grew up in a happy crowded household. Their apartment became a rehearsal room, game night and guest room before they realized hospitality was consuming the space needed to work. Mara's band posters paid some rent; a failed Harbor Vale commission taught them what missing a deadline costs. They want the River Lights centerpiece and a shared tram-shed venue with doors that actually close.

Their installation and local band compete with Vic's polished act. They envy his ease with patrons and privately admire his timing. Osei wants editing lessons; Niko wants the old parade creature. Players encounter Jo's model occupying the kitchen table. Help can mean making something wonderful, meeting a deadline, setting a household boundary or honestly judging a rival better. This is an early career, but it already leaves footprints.

### Niko Dalen, 11 — the founder of the missing parcel club

Niko has lived above two different shops, spent school holidays in Harbor Vale with his mother and developed firm opinions about which Linden bus drivers brake too hard. Last winter, after returning a parcel left at the wrong door, he founded the **Missing Parcel Club** with three school friends. They reunited a neighbor with her wedding shoes, lost somebody's birthday present for two days and charged lifetime membership fees they had no plan to honor. The club is currently arguing about refunds.

He also broke Osei's old camera by dropping it, denied touching it and eventually confessed after Priya recognized a piece in his marble tin. Osei let him help build an exhibition frame; neither adult pretends that makes a repair free. Niko wants a bicycle, the captaincy of his school team and Jo's retired parade creature as a clubhouse roof. He is funny, bossy, loyal to particular friends and still frightened by a dark stairwell he insists is fine.

Meet him disputing marble rules or sorting parcels beside a very unofficial sign. Players can join a neighborhood mystery, play, help him make restitution or take his small project seriously. His knowledge covers routes, classmates and things he actually saw. At home he draws comics in which the club has a submarine, a ridiculous number of dogs and considerably fewer problems with refunds.

## A first month of play

Start with one apartment building, the courtyard, library, depot, a boxing gym and one venue. Choose a few of the fifteen people whose routines intersect the selected opening; senior figures can be reached through public occasions, work or introductions, and the upriver expedition remains an optional later direction. Let the player choose a foothold: a livelihood, an adult relationship, an artistic or sporting ambition, or an investigation. A job has consequential tasks and summarized routine time. Progress is visible in a better home, new competence, an audience, a won contract, a trusted partner or a changed balance of power.

The **River Lights opening** connects these pursuits without requiring everyone to follow one plot. Jo and Vic compete for the single commission to produce its main-stage centerpiece, offering an installation with a band and a staged musical act respectively; Kira needs a sponsor before her qualifying comeback bout; Priya wants a profitable launch; the tram shed's lease will be awarded after the festival. An assaulted stallholder can identify the Quay Circle's pressure, while an apparently generous sponsorship gives its recipient an immediate advantage. The case begins with a real authored perpetrator and evidence, not a culprit retroactively chosen to fit the player's guess.

| Build a shared place             | Survey, finance and organize a viable use of the shed, then actually enjoy it                                                                                | A coalition splits or loses the lease; a smaller project can persist without pretending it won the original contest                             |

Keep [the midnight rehearsal](../combinations.md#the-midnight-rehearsal), [missing parcel club](../combinations.md#the-missing-parcel-club) and [a good job becomes smaller](../combinations.md#a-good-job-becomes-smaller). Add [the festival contract](../combinations.md#the-festival-contract) and [the witness-and-the-promotion](../combinations.md#the-witness-and-the-promotion) when the selected premise includes competition or crime. A settled relationship, a successful exhibition or a defeated racketeer should change later play; neither happy outcomes nor serious harms are reset to keep every quest available.

**Anti-boredom and resilience:** routine bills and chores can be bundled; the selected scenario declares the stakes of debt, eviction and recovery; schedules are adjustable; no mandatory daily login; settled relationships do not decay automatically because the player takes a break. Start with a few services and named stakeholders. Distant neighborhoods can remain authored context or explicit aggregates. Injury, bankruptcy, separation or a death can be lasting when included in the chosen premise. Their occurrence should follow authored conditions and actual events. Constant random disasters and unexplained offscreen losses are not substitutes for active rivals or coherent consequences.

Open choices before adoption: time compression, household control versus one embodied player, housing-loss severity, scope of fictional law and crime, how much of the economy runs dynamically, and whether wider politics is authored context or a selected model. Full-city simulation is not assumed.
