# MO proposal: Linden Reach

**Current-game selection: 5 Specialist, as a separate primary experience.** Retain its home/career/social/urban possibilities without steering the current survival game toward shift planning or civic administration.

[Library](../README.md) · [Labels](../README.md#reading-the-labels) · [Selection and scale](../selection-and-scale.md) · [Design foundation](../design-foundation.md)

**Optional setting proposal, not canonical world history or implemented content.** Linden Reach is a contemporary river-and-rail city where people pursue love, money, recognition, security, pleasure and power through work, households, friendships and rival institutions. Its central question is: _what life will you make, what will it cost, and what will you defend or compromise to keep it?_ Domestic life, romance, career competition, public achievement, investigation and crime are substantial possible directions. The selected opening gives the player a foothold and connects their ambitions to particular neighbors, opportunities and pressures.

**Estimate:** Play/Blend, **Moderate/Extend** for one household, several workplaces and a small neighborhood; **Large/New** for modeled city services, institutions and economy. This is a later and more demanding world family if those interactions must execute broadly. An attractive city description does not establish support for its interlocking systems. Setting and cast content are **Small/Compose** candidates.

## A city with more than one good life

Linden Reach began around a freight harbor and grew along two rail lines. Its old industrial quarter now contains repair shops, apartments, studios and a public college. The river is cleaner than it was a generation ago, but access to its banks remains uneven. Some streets are lively, others quiet; neither is the correct way to live.

| District or institution | Proposed possibility and tension                                                                                            | Build path                                                             |
| ----------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Morrow Quay**         | Small apartments above shops, a courtyard and a bus depot; rents rise as old warehouses become desirable studios            | Moderate/Extend: household schedules, local rents and shared-space rules      |
| **Northglass**          | College, clinic and library around a large park; services attract people from districts with fewer resources                | Moderate/Extend: bounded service workflows and access                         |

| **Reach Council**       | A municipal institution inside a larger fictional republic, with real but limited budgets and authority                     | Large/New: political procedures if modeled; Small/Compose for authored context |

The wider country, **the Republic of Edrin**, is optional background. It can provide common law, currency and rail standards without requiring a simulated national government. Neighboring cities **Harbor Vale** and **Eastmere** are destinations for visits, trade and career changes, represented first as bounded locations.

## Recent history and overlapping communities

**Forty years ago**, harbor automation reduced some jobs while creating others. A repair cooperative formed when workers pooled severance money to keep a machine shop open. Its founding story emphasizes solidarity; surviving accounts also show arguments, mistakes and help from people later omitted.

**Seventeen years ago**, severe flooding displaced several blocks. A temporary housing complex became permanent through residents' organizing. Some people want it renovated, others want the promised replacement homes, and some fear losing the relationships built there.

**Six years ago**, the public college opened an adult-learning program, bringing new students into the Tileworks. This created friendships and businesses alongside noise, rent pressures and resentment about whose labor makes the district fashionable.

**Now**, a disused tram shed may become a market, a performance venue, housing or a combined project. Its future is one optional center of play. Nobody must solve the city's entire housing economy before opening a café or making friends.

Different migration waves can contribute food, names, faith and professional networks. Write specific families and institutions rather than assigning a temperament to an ethnic group. Alternative setting: a smaller coastal town with the same social relationships and fewer modeled services. Both versions support contemporary technology without assuming identical national politics.

Phones, computers and social media can exist as fictional interfaces to admitted messages and services. They do not grant real-world network or account access. For the first slice, rent, wages and purchases can use a small understandable model; mortgages, derivatives, national macroeconomics and every tax form are unnecessary unless a creator specifically chooses that simulation question.

A **repair library**, a **shared cold pantry** and a **neighborhood shift board** remain useful inventions. So do a distinctive stage installation, a training device, a profitable product or a tool that reveals concealed evidence. Players can design services, start a business, make art, pursue status or choose a dependable modest job that leaves time for friends; none is the universally preferred life.

## Desires, power and opposition

Linden Reach connects domestic comfort, personal ambition and public life. A resident may want a lover, a championship, an admired house, revenge, artistic recognition, freedom from debt, a profitable business or authority over other people. Some conflicts can be negotiated; some involve deliberate exploitation, incompatible claims or a person who prefers winning to being fair. The city's pleasure includes wit, flirtation, music, taste, skill and the satisfaction of succeeding at something difficult.

| Power or network                         | Wants and means                                                                                                         | Internal difference and potential opposition                                                                                                                                       |
| ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Reach Council and the Renewal Office** | Redevelop the tram shed, balance a budget and retain office; controls leases, contracts, permits and public information | Officials disagree about costs, patronage and public benefit. A protected contractor can lose a vote and still own the crucial site                                                |
| **The Tileworks Cooperative**            | Keep workshops, apprenticeships and affordable space; has skilled labor, member savings and neighborhood legitimacy     | Members can divide over selling land, excluding newcomers or accepting suspect money. Solidarity does not erase self-interest                                                      |
| **Kest Development and its investors**   | Earn returns and create a prestigious district; owns property and can finance construction or withhold it               | Tomas wants admiration and profit; some investors prefer a quick sale. Negotiation can work, but displacement and broken promises can also be deliberate decisions                 |
| **The Quay Circle**                      | Control late-night venues and disputed storage space; Leon Vale trades favors, money, employment and intimidation       | Some businesses welcome its custom or protection; others are being coerced. Its members can betray each other. Affection inside the circle does not make threats outside it benign |
| **The Reach police and local courts**    | Investigate violence and enforce the city's authored laws; possess coercive authority, records and finite resources     | Honest work, ambition, selective enforcement, corruption and professional loyalty can collide. A solved case need not repair the institution                                       |
| **The Lantern Circuit**                  | Run performances, sport and the River Lights events; controls stages, selection, sponsorship and prestige               | Artists, athletes, patrons and audiences want different things. These are fictional organizations with particular members, not claims about real cities or a universal model of politics. Organizations can create useful public goods as well as harm. Their plans advance through authored events and bounded activities, not an omniscient city simulator.

Progression means more than earning money: expertise, access, a reputation, trusted relationships, an audience, ownership and institutional influence each change what the player can attempt. Competing for a contract, winning a bout, discovering a fraud or composing a performance can provide a satisfying evening with a visible result. Use a few legible systems for the chosen role rather than requiring every player to become a city administrator.

## Culture, belief and naming

The **River Lights evening** celebrates flood recovery with floating lantern displays in safe designated basins, music and recollections. The **Open Bench Saturday** invites people to bring broken objects to the cooperative. Neither needs attendance rewards. Residents may follow several religions, attend a secular choir, observe family rituals or prefer private reflection. Proposed institutions include **the House of Welcome**, a faith community that operates a meal service, and **the Common Room**, a nonreligious mutual-aid space that sometimes partners with it.

Street names preserve industry and landscape—**Kiln Row, Willow Steps, Morrow Quay**—while newer developments adopt polished names residents may mock. Personal naming should reflect family choices and migrations. The proposed cast mixes short given names with varied surname rhythms; these are invented individuals, not representatives of real cultures. A later author could build specific fictional language histories around families without assigning all speakers the same values.

## Eight people to meet

**Cast defaults: Play, High, Small/Compose** for authored life histories; **Moderate/Extend** for executing schedules, commitments, selective memory and relationship changes. The twelve people across the two groups below share workplaces, creative projects, loyalties and rivalries. Their histories and ordinary pleasures give context to what they want and how they treat one another.

### Mara Venn, 33 — the transit electrician who wants mornings back

Mara grew up riding buses with a parent who worked split shifts, and still finds comfort in the first depot lights before dawn. She likes diagnosing faults because a good repair has an observable result. She wants a stable schedule to rehearse with her band and resents being praised as “indispensable” when it means endless overtime. She is patient with apprentices and brusque with vague managers. Jo designs her band's posters; Idris can help her negotiate coverage, though she worries accepting help makes her seem less competent.

### Idris Saye, 48 — the organizer who misses being a beginner

Idris helped establish a fair shift rota at the depot and became the person everyone calls about disputes. He enjoys people and believes institutions can improve, but his calendar leaves little room to learn ceramics. Raised in a warm, argumentative household, he sees disagreement as a sign of trust and occasionally overlooks quieter people's discomfort. He wants the tram shed to include a workshop, not only meeting rooms. Lian is teaching him to make cups; his first lopsided bowl is displayed at home with pride. A long-time depot ally has concealed an unsafe shortcut to protect a job. Idris must decide whether loyalty means defending the person, exposing the conduct or demanding a remedy his own supporters oppose.

### Lian Orrel, 61 — the ceramicist with a profitable compromise

Lian built a small studio through reliable commissions and careful savings, then took a lucrative contract for a redevelopment company. She likes its architect and dislikes the company's treatment of tenants. She wants to keep her apprentices employed while changing the contract's terms. Her hobby is cataloguing city pigeons by improvised names. She can be too certain that patience solves every problem. Idris respects her practical knowledge; Ana wants her to use her influence more publicly, creating a disagreement that need not end their friendship.

### Jo Mercer, 24 — the designer whose home is also everyone's venue

Jo grew up in a happy crowded household and equates welcome with leaving the door open. Their apartment became a rehearsal room, game night and temporary guest room before they realized they needed privacy. They want to design a shared venue in the tram shed and learn to set limits without withdrawing affection. Their work is imaginative but deadlines slip when friends ask for help. Mara loves their posters and worries about their sleep. A player can help make a workable household agreement instead of merely buying a larger sofa. Jo also wants the commission to produce the River Lights centerpiece: their bid combines a light installation, stage design and a local band. They envy the polished work of the better-connected Vic Orrel, whose rival bid is a staged musical act. Their generosity does not prevent a cutting joke, a jealous misreading or a choice to prioritize their own career.

### Ana Velin, 39 — the librarian who keeps the wrong folders

Ana's family lived in temporary flood housing that never received its promised renovation. She became a librarian partly because accurate public records can protect people, but her personal desk is gloriously untidy. She wants the housing history preserved and the library's cooling room funded. She loves melodramatic radio serials and dislikes being cast as the neighborhood's permanently serious conscience. Osei brings her archival photographs; Lian can help secure resources, though Ana distrusts quiet negotiations that leave affected residents outside the room.

### Osei Rill, 72 — the photographer learning the new city

Osei worked as a delivery driver and photographed shop windows for pleasure, accidentally creating a valuable record of changing streets. Widowed several years ago, he has strong friendships and a satisfying routine; his life is not waiting for the player to cure loneliness. He wants to mount a small exhibit and learn digital editing from Jo. He sometimes mistakes familiarity for authority and must listen when younger residents describe a different city. He and Ana disagree about which captions need uncertainty rather than a confident anecdote. An old photograph may expose a favor that benefited a friend while costing another household its shop. His affection and his duty to the record now point in different directions.

### Priya Dalen, 31 — the cook building a business she can leave at five

Priya trained in demanding restaurant kitchens and discovered she preferred feeding familiar people to chasing prestige. She wants a lunch counter with a shared cold pantry and hours compatible with caring for her nephew. Her family supports the idea but keeps offering unsolicited advice. She is inventive with leftovers and stubborn about accepting free labor. Her quiet pleasure is repairing old board games. Mara could become a regular customer; Idris can help design fair staffing, provided he does not turn every conversation into a committee meeting.

### Tomas Kest, 45 — the developer who wants to be liked for the building

Tomas grew up in an apartment over a shop and sincerely admires mixed neighborhoods. He also has investors expecting returns from the tram shed project and too readily treats displacement as a scheduling issue. He wants to build something admired, partly because an earlier project was ugly and profitable. He is a generous amateur music patron and a poor listener under pressure. He can become a credible partner if residents gain enforceable influence, or an antagonist if he keeps substituting goodwill for changed terms. His taste does not excuse his decisions.

## Four more people with competing futures

### Kira Dane, 29 — the boxer who intends to win

Kira is a delivery driver and a skilled amateur boxer competing for a paid place on the regional circuit. A supportive father taught her to enjoy training and take an opponent seriously; she loves the contest, the crowd and the pleasure of a cleanly executed combination. She wants a championship and a professional career. Her impatience with weaker training partners can become arrogance. Leon offers sponsorship that would solve a real financial problem and make her useful to his venues. She trains with Mara after shifts, admires Priya's discipline and has an opponent she respects but fully intends to beat. A player can train, compete, sponsor or oppose her without becoming her automatic best friend.

### Leon Vale, 43 — the patron whose hospitality has teeth

Leon owns a late-night club and coordinates the Quay Circle's intimidation of businesses that resist its terms. He grew up among proprietors who prized personal favors over unreliable formal protection, then learned to make that dependence profitable. He is funny at dinner, generous to his sister and proud of the bands he helped discover. He also knowingly orders violence and enjoys making a defiant owner submit. Those acts are not misunderstandings erased by hearing his childhood. He wants the tram shed's nighttime concessions and uses a helpful intermediary to approach Tomas. Priya has refused his offer; Jo is tempted by his stage. He can be outmaneuvered, prosecuted, displaced, served or fought in an appropriate scenario; no single sympathetic conversation guarantees reform.

### Sera Holt, 38 — the investigator with a case and a career

Sera earned her reputation by patiently linking testimony to physical evidence. Her mother ran a small shop, and she knows how a threat can turn a familiar street into a place someone fears. She wants to prove the Quay Circle ordered an assault, protect her witness and win a promotion that would let her choose better cases. Those goals can conflict when a superior wants an immediate arrest and an uncertain witness wants to withdraw. She can be brave, perceptive and professionally vain; under pressure she may defend a premature theory. Ana can locate public records and Osei can identify a changed building, but neither owes her every private confidence. Players can assist, challenge, evade or expose her conduct through actual evidence.

### Vic Orrel, 27 — the performer who will not stay an apprentice

Vic is Lian's nephew, a charismatic performer and event promoter who grew up helping set up exhibitions while better-connected guests received the praise. He wants his staged musical act to win the single River Lights centerpiece commission, then turn the tram shed into a venue with his name on it. He genuinely loves an audience and works hard at an act that Jo privately admires. He also flatters patrons, takes disproportionate credit and keeps two collaborators uncertain about promises he cannot honor together. His relationship with Kira combines attraction and competition for the same sponsor. He may choose honesty at a real cost, double down on a lie or win despite a rival's dislike. His performances give players reasons to admire his skill while deciding how far to trust his promises.

## A first month of play

Start with one apartment building, the courtyard, library, depot, a boxing gym and one venue. Let the player choose a foothold: a livelihood, an adult relationship, an artistic or sporting ambition, or an investigation. A job has consequential tasks and summarized routine time. Progress is visible in a better home, new competence, an audience, a won contract, a trusted partner or a changed balance of power.

The **River Lights opening** connects these pursuits without requiring everyone to follow one plot. Jo and Vic compete for the single commission to produce its main-stage centerpiece, offering an installation with a band and a staged musical act respectively; Kira needs a sponsor before her qualifying bout; Priya wants a profitable launch; the tram shed's lease will be awarded after the festival. An assaulted stallholder can identify the Quay Circle's pressure, while an apparently generous sponsorship gives its recipient an immediate advantage. The case begins with a real authored perpetrator and evidence, not a culprit retroactively chosen to fit the player's guess.

| Direction                        | Playable decisions and rewards                                                                                                                               | Failure and continuation                                                                                                                        |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Establish a home and business    | Furnish, cook, price an offer, negotiate staffing, attract customers, make a friend or date                                                                  | A rival gets the best site, a poor launch creates debt or a relationship changes; useful work and ordinary pleasures remain available           |
| Compete for recognition          | Train for the bout, rehearse, assemble a team, choose a sponsor, perform or fight under readable rules                                                       | Lose a contest, injure a hand, expose a lie or win with an obligation attached; rivals remember actual conduct                                  |
| Investigate or resist the Circle | Compare accounts, protect a witness, obtain evidence through interviews, records or supported infiltration, confront an intermediary or support a legal case | A suspect escapes, a witness withdraws, violence escalates or an ally is compromised; the investigation has costs and several possible outcomes |
| Pursue money and influence       | Bargain with Tomas, lobby, invest, cooperate with a faction or take a knowingly harmful deal                                                                 | Profit can coexist with eviction, a feud or damaged trust; public success is not a guarantee of innocence                                       |
| Build a shared place             | Survey, finance and organize a viable use of the shed, then actually enjoy it                                                                                | A coalition splits or loses the lease; a smaller project can persist without pretending it won the original contest                             |

Keep [the midnight rehearsal](../combinations.md#the-midnight-rehearsal), [missing parcel club](../combinations.md#the-missing-parcel-club) and [a good job becomes smaller](../combinations.md#a-good-job-becomes-smaller). Add [the festival contract](../combinations.md#the-festival-contract) and [the witness-and-the-promotion](../combinations.md#the-witness-and-the-promotion) when the selected premise includes competition or crime. A settled relationship, a successful exhibition or a defeated racketeer should change later play; neither happy outcomes nor serious harms are reset to keep every quest available.

**Anti-boredom and resilience:** routine bills and chores can be bundled; the selected scenario declares the stakes of debt, eviction and recovery; schedules are adjustable; no mandatory daily login; settled relationships do not decay automatically because the player takes a break. Start with a few services and named stakeholders. Distant neighborhoods can remain authored context or explicit aggregates. Injury, bankruptcy, separation or a death can be lasting when included in the chosen premise. Their occurrence should follow authored conditions and actual events. Constant random disasters and unexplained offscreen losses are not substitutes for active rivals or coherent consequences.

Open choices before adoption: time compression, household control versus one embodied player, housing-loss severity, scope of fictional law and crime, how much of the economy runs dynamically, and whether wider politics is authored context or a selected model. Full-city simulation is not assumed.
