# MO proposal: Linden Reach

[Library](../README.md) · [Labels](../README.md#reading-the-labels) · [Selection and scale](../selection-and-scale.md)

**Optional setting proposal, not canonical world history or implemented content.** Linden Reach is a contemporary river-and-rail city where an ordinary life can become interesting through work, friendship, taste and collective projects. Its central question is: _what would make this neighborhood a place you want to stay?_ The city can contain ambition, injustice and investigation without making crime or saving the world compulsory.

**Estimate:** Play/Blend, High, **2/Extend** for one household, several workplaces and a small neighborhood; **3/New** for modeled city services, institutions and economy. This is a later and more demanding world family if those interactions must execute broadly. An attractive city description does not establish support for its interlocking systems. Setting and cast content are **1/Compose** candidates.

## A city with more than one good life

Linden Reach began around a freight harbor and grew along two rail lines. Its old industrial quarter now contains repair shops, apartments, studios and a public college. The river is cleaner than it was a generation ago, but access to its banks remains uneven. Some streets are lively, others quiet; neither is the correct way to live.

The wider country, **the Republic of Edrin**, is optional background. It can provide common law, currency and rail standards without requiring a simulated national government. Neighboring cities **Harbor Vale** and **Eastmere** are destinations for visits, trade and career changes, represented first as bounded locations.

## Recent history and overlapping communities

**Forty years ago**, harbor automation reduced some jobs while creating others. A repair cooperative formed when workers pooled severance money to keep a machine shop open. Its founding story emphasizes solidarity; surviving accounts also show arguments, mistakes and help from people later omitted.

**Seventeen years ago**, severe flooding displaced several blocks. A temporary housing complex became permanent through residents' organizing. Some people want it renovated, others want the promised replacement homes, and some fear losing the relationships built there.

**Six years ago**, the public college opened an adult-learning program, bringing new students into the Tileworks. This created friendships and businesses alongside noise, rent pressures and resentment about whose labor makes the district fashionable.

**Now**, a disused tram shed may become a market, a performance venue, housing or a combined project. Its future is one optional center of play. Nobody must solve the city's entire housing economy before opening a café or making friends.

Different migration waves can contribute food, names, faith and professional networks. Write specific families and institutions rather than assigning a temperament to an ethnic group. Alternative setting: a smaller coastal town with the same social relationships and fewer modeled services. Both versions support contemporary technology without assuming identical national politics.

Phones, computers and social media can exist as fictional interfaces to admitted messages and services. They do not grant real-world network or account access. For the first slice, rent, wages and purchases can use a small understandable model; mortgages, derivatives, national macroeconomics and every tax form are unnecessary unless a creator specifically chooses that simulation question.

A **repair library**, a **shared cold pantry** and a **neighborhood shift board** are more immediately useful inventions than futuristic gadgets. Players can design practical services, start a business, make art or choose a dependable modest job that leaves time for friends.

## Culture, belief and naming

The **River Lights evening** celebrates flood recovery with floating lantern displays in safe designated basins, music and recollections. The **Open Bench Saturday** invites people to bring broken objects to the cooperative. Neither needs attendance rewards. Residents may follow several religions, attend a secular choir, observe family rituals or prefer private reflection. Proposed institutions include **the House of Welcome**, a faith community that operates a meal service, and **the Common Room**, a nonreligious mutual-aid space that sometimes partners with it.

Street names preserve industry and landscape—**Kiln Row, Willow Steps, Morrow Quay**—while newer developments adopt polished names residents may mock. Personal naming should reflect family choices and migrations. The proposed cast mixes short given names with varied surname rhythms; these are invented individuals, not representatives of real cultures. A later author could build specific fictional language histories around families without assigning all speakers the same values.

## Eight people to meet

**Cast defaults: Play, High, 1/Compose** for authored life histories; **2/Extend** for executing schedules, commitments, selective memory and relationship changes. Everyday pleasures and competence are intentional, not padding around hidden trauma.

### Mara Venn, 33 — the transit electrician who wants mornings back

Mara grew up riding buses with a parent who worked split shifts, and still finds comfort in the first depot lights before dawn. She likes diagnosing faults because a good repair has an observable result. She wants a stable schedule to rehearse with her band and resents being praised as “indispensable” when it means endless overtime. She is patient with apprentices and brusque with vague managers. Jo designs her band's posters; Idris can help her negotiate coverage, though she worries accepting help makes her seem less competent.

### Idris Saye, 48 — the organizer who misses being a beginner

Idris helped establish a fair shift rota at the depot and became the person everyone calls about disputes. He enjoys people and believes institutions can improve, but his calendar leaves little room to learn ceramics. Raised in a warm, argumentative household, he sees disagreement as a sign of trust and occasionally overlooks quieter people's discomfort. He wants the tram shed to include a workshop, not only meeting rooms. Lian is teaching him to make cups; his first lopsided bowl is displayed at home with pride.

### Lian Orrel, 61 — the ceramicist with a profitable compromise

Lian built a small studio through reliable commissions and careful savings, then took a lucrative contract for a redevelopment company. She likes its architect and dislikes the company's treatment of tenants. She wants to keep her apprentices employed while changing the contract's terms. Her hobby is cataloguing city pigeons by improvised names. She can be too certain that patience solves every problem. Idris respects her practical knowledge; Ana wants her to use her influence more publicly, creating a disagreement that need not end their friendship.

### Jo Mercer, 24 — the designer whose home is also everyone's venue

Jo grew up in a happy crowded household and equates welcome with leaving the door open. Their apartment became a rehearsal room, game night and temporary guest room before they realized they needed privacy. They want to design a shared venue in the tram shed and learn to set limits without withdrawing affection. Their work is imaginative but deadlines slip when friends ask for help. Mara loves their posters and worries about their sleep. A player can help make a workable household agreement instead of merely buying a larger sofa.

### Ana Velin, 39 — the librarian who keeps the wrong folders

Ana's family lived in temporary flood housing that never received its promised renovation. She became a librarian partly because accurate public records can protect people, but her personal desk is gloriously untidy. She wants the housing history preserved and the library's cooling room funded. She loves melodramatic radio serials and dislikes being cast as the neighborhood's permanently serious conscience. Osei brings her archival photographs; Lian can help secure resources, though Ana distrusts quiet negotiations that leave affected residents outside the room.

### Osei Rill, 72 — the photographer learning the new city

Osei worked as a delivery driver and photographed shop windows for pleasure, accidentally creating a valuable record of changing streets. Widowed several years ago, he has strong friendships and a satisfying routine; his life is not waiting for the player to cure loneliness. He wants to mount a small exhibit and learn digital editing from Jo. He sometimes mistakes familiarity for authority and must listen when younger residents describe a different city. He and Ana disagree about which captions need uncertainty rather than a confident anecdote.

### Priya Dalen, 31 — the cook building a business she can leave at five

Priya trained in demanding restaurant kitchens and discovered she preferred feeding familiar people to chasing prestige. She wants a lunch counter with a shared cold pantry and hours compatible with caring for her nephew. Her family supports the idea but keeps offering unsolicited advice. She is inventive with leftovers and stubborn about accepting free labor. Her quiet pleasure is repairing old board games. Mara could become a regular customer; Idris can help design fair staffing, provided he does not turn every conversation into a committee meeting.

### Tomas Kest, 45 — the developer who wants to be liked for the building

Tomas grew up in an apartment over a shop and sincerely admires mixed neighborhoods. He also has investors expecting returns from the tram shed project and too readily treats displacement as a scheduling issue. He wants to build something admired, partly because an earlier project was ugly and profitable. He is a generous amateur music patron and a poor listener under pressure. He can become a credible partner if residents gain enforceable influence, or an antagonist if he keeps substituting goodwill for changed terms. His taste does not excuse his decisions.

## A first month of play

Start with one apartment building, the courtyard, library, depot and cooperative. Offer three small directions: make a home, establish a livelihood, or help organize an event. A job can have a few consequential tasks and summarized routine time. Relationships emerge from shared activities, remembered contributions and boundaries, not repeated gift transactions.

Use [the midnight rehearsal](../combinations.md#the-midnight-rehearsal), [missing parcel club](../combinations.md#the-missing-parcel-club) and [a good job becomes smaller](../combinations.md#a-good-job-becomes-smaller). The tram shed supplies a longer optional arc: survey the building, gather needs, prototype activities, choose a viable plan and enjoy the result. The city should also support someone who declines public leadership and prefers gardening, work and friends.

**Anti-boredom and resilience:** routine bills and chores can be bundled; late payments have proportionate recovery; schedules are adjustable; no mandatory daily login; settled relationships do not decay automatically because the player takes a break. Start with a few services and named stakeholders. Distant neighborhoods can remain authored context or explicit aggregates. A crisis should reveal a new decision, not continually punish successful organization.

Open choices before adoption: time compression, household control versus one embodied player, housing-loss severity, scope of fictional law and crime, how much of the economy runs dynamically, and whether wider politics is authored context or a selected model. Full-city simulation is not assumed.
