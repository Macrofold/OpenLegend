# Narrative, character and humor

Open Legend's stories should give players people to care about, situations worth entering and consequences they want to see. Use this guide when developing characters, authored situations, dialogue examples, lore and the telling of actual play. Each world chooses its own [tone and scope](../repertoires/design-foundation.md#world-tone-and-scope).

The [characters and play workflow](../production-workflows/characters-and-play.md) owns turning an idea into playable content. [Player-facing language](../ui-ux/player-facing-language.md) owns clear, appropriate expression; [narration's truth boundaries](../narration-and-conversations.md#2-three-kinds-of-truth) own what a telling may establish.

[Authored worlds and personal discovery](authored-worlds-and-personal-discovery.md) develops the complementary direction for emotional weight across a whole world: nonlinear lore discovery, gods guiding personal journeys, region access, dangerous frontiers, underground exploration and meaningful needs.

- [ROCKBEASTS](case-studies/rockbeasts.md) — character, dark humor, acting and a coherent fictional world.
- [Trailblazers: Into the March](case-studies/trailblazers-into-the-march.md) — a home in the wilderness, mysterious people, practical care and an emotional journey.

## Give someone a specific want

Begin with a person who wants something now. Connect that immediate desire to a recognizable history, relationship, pleasure or self-image. Three questions help develop the situation:

- What do they want from this encounter?
- What complicates that desire: another commitment, an awkward dependency, a mistaken belief or a contradiction in how they see themselves?
- What could success or failure mean to them and to the player?

Specificity supplies material for both feeling and comedy. Someone wanting respect is broad; a veteran wanting the village children to stop using their ceremonial helmet as a fruit bowl gives the player something concrete to notice. That premise can suggest irritation, affection, pride or an unexpected practical solution. Choose the person and circumstances before choosing a punchline.

Contradictions can be affectionate, troubling or funny. A generous person can crave credit; a ruthless rival can take excellent care of an apprentice. Preserve the full person beyond the contrast, including what they enjoy when nobody needs them.

## Let motives and status collide

Put people together who want different things from the same interaction. One wants an apology, another wants the work finished, and a third wants everyone to notice that they predicted the problem. Consider who believes they are in charge, who has the useful skill and whose cooperation is actually necessary.

Status can shift through a small discovery or response. The imposing visitor may need the unimpressed apprentice's help. The apparent fool may understand one crucial detail. Let their competence, dependence and reactions carry the scene; explain only what the player needs to follow it.

Such collisions can produce negotiation, embarrassment, tenderness, hostility or a practical agreement. Give participants room to surprise one another. [People and factions have their own purposes](../repertoires/design-foundation.md#people-and-factions-have-their-own-purposes) remains the underlying design principle.

## Give ordinary tasks human meaning

Delivering supplies, repairing an object or finding a place to sleep can expose pride, loyalty, taste, rivalry or an old disappointment. Ask why this particular person cares about this particular result. Who will use the repaired object? Why does the gift matter? What would accepting help mean to its recipient?

Let that meaning affect the available choices and later encounter. The player might preserve a cherished feature, negotiate a compromise or choose a reliable practical solution. A modest task can resolve satisfyingly. Avoid requiring a hidden tragedy, grand conspiracy or escalating emergency to justify attention.

## Make humor arise from the situation

Useful techniques include an earnest response to an absurd circumstance, understated acknowledgment of a disaster, a revealing correction, an awkward pause, or a mismatch between someone's grand self-image and immediate practical concern. Subtext lets a person request one thing while revealing another need through their conduct.

Timing includes leaving space for the player to notice. A reaction, object or short silence may carry more than another explanatory sentence. Repetition can establish an expectation; a changed circumstance can make a later callback rewarding. Keep callbacks specific to remembered shared experience.

Dark humor can draw on mortality, failure, vanity, precarious work or someone maintaining ordinary priorities in a dreadful situation. Preserve the consequence and the person experiencing it. Genuine care can coexist with an outrageous remark, and a character can make a joke that another person dislikes. The joke's reception belongs to the participants.

The existing [mischief example](../repertoires/arts-leisure.md#make-a-place-for-mischief) connects occasion, audience, available props and independent reactions. [Stories, poetry and comedy](../repertoires/arts-leisure.md#stories-poetry-and-comedy) supplies varied forms without requiring every interaction to become a performance.

## Give voices room to differ

Build voice from attention, vocabulary, rhythm, confidence, evasions and what the speaker considers worth saying. One person answers directly; another changes the subject when embarrassed; another observes a practical detail everyone else missed. Use these as tendencies that circumstances can change.

Let wit coexist with sincerity, clumsiness, brevity and silence. A companion can be funny without producing a polished quip at every opportunity. Give a serious answer room to land, especially when someone risks revealing a need or asks a consequential question. [Speaker and tone guidance](../ui-ux/player-facing-language.md#8-fit-the-speaker-situation-and-tone) preserves that variation.

## Make choices lead somewhere

Offer understandable possibilities with consequences that matter to the activity or people involved. Follow an actual result into another opportunity: an object is useful, a favor remains unresolved, an earlier joke changes meaning, or someone has a reason to approach the player again.

Surprise works when the outcome is unexpected yet understandable from the people and world. It is optional. Let successful plans succeed, quiet company remain quiet and satisfying endings remain settled. Warmth, competence, beauty and relief give danger and absurdity contrast.

## Example seeds to explore

These are hypothetical authored situations, not current gameplay claims or prescribed character responses. Their required objects, actions and consequences must be supported before use.

- **The memorial carpenter.** A carpenter orders excellent timber for a solemn commission, then admits the deceased would have preferred a hideous design. The player can explore the tension between craftsmanship, affection and the family's wishes. Humor may emerge from specific memories while the loss remains real.
- **The retired herald's breakfast.** Adapt [CB-221](../repertoires/characters-backstories.md#absurd-uncanny-and-impossible-lives): a former apocalypse herald wants customers to appreciate the rolls. A request for an ordinary recommendation keeps being interpreted as prophecy. Give the baker an actual preference and a life beyond the misunderstanding.
- **The unexpectedly successful expedition.** Adapt RSH-242 from [everyday friendship](../repertoires/relationships.md#everyday-friendship): friends who celebrate spectacular failed ventures finally succeed. Their familiar ritual needs a new meaning; they can take genuine pleasure in both the victory and their ridiculous history.

## Preserve agency and factual grounding

Author people, circumstances and opportunities under the [character-authorship contract](../projects/compelling-characters-feature-spec.md#character-authorship-and-changing-personality). Illustrative dialogue helps develop a possibility; autonomous participants retain their own choices, including refusal or silence. Explicitly authored performances and scenario setup remain identifiable as such.

Narration may shape rhythm, imagery and tone around permitted facts. It cannot invent a motive, off-screen event or consequence to complete a dramatic beat. Attributed speech can be mistaken, exaggerated or deceptive without replacing the actual event. Follow actual consequences through the [existing production workflow](../production-workflows/characters-and-play.md#6-carry-actual-consequences-into-another-opportunity).

## Review the experience

- What does each important person want, and what makes this encounter particular?
- What can the player choose, understand or affect?
- Does the humor reveal something about people or circumstances?
- Do reactions, pauses and sincere moments have room alongside dialogue?
- Can the scene remain worthwhile if someone refuses, the joke fails or the expected surprise never happens?
- What actual consequence makes a later encounter different?

Judge the whole encounter and its aftermath. A good line is useful when it contributes to an experience the player wants to have.
