# Authored worlds and personal discovery

**Creative direction and candidate game ideas — October 22, 2026.** Mike wants Open Legend's generative, procedural play to carry emotional weight and enough authored substance that players can follow a compelling story while choosing their own paths. The [Trailblazers reference study](case-studies/trailblazers-into-the-march.md) prompted this note. The goals below are endorsed preferences; the proposed gods, gates and procedural sites still need selection and design before becoming gameplay claims.

This note owns the combined narrative idea. [Character and humor guidance](README.md) owns scene craft; [gods, death and personal mythology](gods-death-and-personal-mythology.md) develops divine relationships and a possible personal realm beyond the shared world. The linked repertoires and existing designs retain their respective mechanics and examples. These possibilities belong to selected worlds, with different worlds free to choose different histories, supernatural laws and progression.

## The experience Mike wants

- A world with a compelling authored history, lore and larger mysteries, discovered through play at different times and in different orders.
- Enough direction and emotional continuity to follow a story without requiring every player to enact the same sequence.
- Gods that can address thousands of players personally because their existence need not be confined to one physical body. At meaningful milestones they might offer goals, reveal information or open access to places.
- Regions held back until sufficient progress, a level, a skill or another relevant qualification, alongside wilderness whose threats themselves make travel difficult.
- The feeling that anyone might be out there: mysterious strangers, unusual companions and lost souls with histories worth discovering.
- Procedural cave systems and ancient dungeons that add depth beneath familiar territory without requiring endless outward expansion of the land map.
- Needs that make caring for people and preparing for a journey meaningful, drawing on the attraction of RimWorld-like simulation.

## Author a history; let people discover and change the world

Give a world a coherent past: what happened, who was involved, what survives, and why it still matters. Procedural generation can select or arrange authored variants during world creation, and generative characters can interpret evidence and respond to new events. Once a history is established in that shared world, finding its clues in another order should not silently change what happened.

Keep five things distinct when authoring a mystery:

| Element                  | Example in a hypothetical world                                                           |
| ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| What happened            | A settlement deliberately flooded its lower district to stop something spreading          |
| What survives            | A sealed waterway, a damaged record, household objects and a commemorative song           |
| What people believe      | Some call it a rescue; others remember an expulsion; a witness knows only their part      |
| How someone discovers it | One player follows a water problem, another translates the song, another meets a survivor |
| What they can do with it | Locate an entrance, seek a missing witness, repair a mechanism or reconsider an agreement |

Each route should provide a satisfying discovery and understandable further questions. A clue needs enough context to matter when found first. A player who already knows part of the answer should receive acknowledgment or new significance instead of being forced through an ignorant version of the scene. Important understanding can have several surviving sources, so one missed conversation need not make the world's story impossible to follow.

This supports a shared world with different personal journeys. The same ruin can matter to a historian, an explorer, someone trying to help a friend, and a player who simply needs shelter. Their present actions create new history. Use [inspectable ruins](../repertoires/history-myth.md#a-ruin-older-than-the-explanation), [competing historical accounts](../repertoires/history-myth.md#the-flood-with-three-anniversaries) and [mysteries with consistent evidence](../repertoires/adventure-discovery.md#mysteries-and-investigations) as starting material.

The [worked history-authoring guide](authoring-discoverable-history.md) develops this flood example into useful partial discoveries, three possible entry sequences, a present undertaking and its lasting aftermath. It supplies a method for developing selected content without making the example a chosen world or storyline.

## Gods can connect personal journeys to a larger story

Mike especially likes gods as a believable way to provide personal guidance in a world with many simultaneous players. A deity could speak through dreams, shrines, voices, visions or several manifestations. Different people can encounter the same being at different moments without requiring copies of one ordinary person standing in incompatible places.

A god could recognize a level, a learned skill, an expedition, a fulfilled commitment, a discovered truth or a significant choice. Its response might supply a next goal, reveal another part of the world's history, teach something useful, grant a blessing, or open a previously inaccessible region. Levels remain a valid option for worlds that use them; this idea does not require all worlds to share one advancement system.

Make the god a particular being with interests, relationships and a perspective on the world's past. Its invitation can mean something beyond an automatic reward notification. A player can bargain, hesitate, refuse, ask a different question or pursue another concern; a world may still reserve particular revelations or places for those who satisfy their conditions. The amount and timing of guidance should leave room to explore.

The existing [shared-campaign direction](../product-scalability/shared-campaigns.md#5-manifestations-aspects-and-distributed-embodiment) already considers connected manifestations of one being. It is a useful design foundation, not proof that thousands of simultaneous personal conversations are implemented or qualified. A world's divine rules must settle what the being knows, what its manifestations share and what it can actually change. Fictional divinity does not grant access to human-private notes or messages. See also [divinity with a jurisdiction](../repertoires/faith-ritual.md#divinity-with-a-jurisdiction) and [pilgrimage by useful stages](../repertoires/faith-ritual.md#pilgrimage-by-useful-stages).

Mike also wants different gods to give different buffs and to change their relationships with the player according to how that person plays. The [focused divine narrative note](gods-death-and-personal-mythology.md) owns this expansion, including original personalities, unscripted reactions, encounters after death, religions, discovering gods and personal late-game outcomes in the Beyond.

## Bards carry player legends and world news

Mike wants bards to spread legends in both directions: players can inspire songs about their own deeds, and they can hear about notable world events through songs, stories and gossip. An evolving mythology can emerge from people retelling what happened as the shared world changes. Authored old lore and newly created player history can reach someone through the same enjoyable encounter.

A rescue, expedition, discovery, public failure, act of kindness or extraordinary piece of work could give a bard material. Recognition need not be reserved for combat victories or the most powerful players. A song heard far from home might reveal that someone has told the player's story; another verse might introduce an unfamiliar person, ruined place or event worth investigating. News becomes part of social life, with a teller and a reason to listen.

Keep the actual event, the account reaching the bard and the performed version distinct. Bards can hear testimony, encounter witnesses, reinterpret an old song or add an embellishment. A song can be funny, partisan, mistaken or exaggerated without changing what really happened. Local versions and delayed news can give information geography. Hearing a song establishes knowledge of that account, not personal experience of the original event or automatic knowledge of a private divine encounter.

A hypothetical example: a player holds a crossing long enough for travelers to escape a flood. A witness tells a performer, who later sings about the rescue in another settlement. The player hears their deed attributed to an exaggerated heroic name, while another listener learns that the crossing has been damaged. The song provides recognition, imperfect news and a new lead at once. These are possible outcomes rather than prescribed bard decisions.

Related ideas were already present as proposals: [a play about recent shared history](../repertoires/arts-leisure.md#the-play-about-last-winter), ARL047's tavern tall tale and ARL051's competing storytellers in [stories, poetry and comedy](../repertoires/arts-leisure.md#stories-poetry-and-comedy), HMY087's combined heroic identity in [famous lives](../repertoires/history-myth.md#famous-lives), HMY139's changing travelers' legend in [folk explanations](../repertoires/history-myth.md#legends-and-folk-explanations), and [rumor traveling through real sources](../repertoires/languages-knowledge.md#rumor-with-a-route). This section records the complete player-deed, bard-performance and world-news connection explicitly; it does not claim the loop is implemented.

## Make different kinds of access feel meaningful

Keep Mike's proposed level, skill and milestone locks available alongside natural danger. Each can serve a different purpose:

| Kind of progress         | What changes for the player                                                              | Useful tradeoff                                                                       |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Discovery                | A rumor, map, revelation or investigation reveals a place or route                       | Rewards curiosity; learning the location alone need not make the journey safe         |
| Readiness for danger     | Better preparation, companions, knowledge or equipment make a hostile region survivable  | Allows daring early attempts and creative preparation, while preserving real risk     |
| A useful capability      | Climbing, translation, a breathing method or another supported skill enables a route     | Makes development concrete; different routes may reward different capabilities        |
| A personal milestone     | A level, blessing, initiation or completed goal satisfies a region's authored entry rule | Gives a clear progression beat, with an understandable reason for the restriction     |
| A shared physical change | Players repair a bridge, drain a passage or open a sealed entrance                       | Makes effort visible in the common world and changes opportunities for later visitors |

A revelation, a new technique and an open entrance are different rewards. A god announcing readiness does not by itself establish that a character learned a method or that a barrier changed. Link each narrative moment to the actual selected effect and explain the relevant condition to the player.

For shared play, decide whether an unlock belongs to a person, a party or the world. A repaired physical bridge normally becomes part of the shared landscape; a personal blessing can remain personal. Decide how an experienced friend can help, whether an escort can cross a danger boundary, and whether arriving together satisfies each person's revelation or initiation. Keep these questions visible when selecting a design rather than quietly assuming private copies of the world.

Existing [advancement examples](../repertoires/abilities-progression.md#teaching-recognition-and-advancement) and [a treasure that is a route](../repertoires/adventure-discovery.md#a-treasure-that-is-a-route) provide concrete starting points.

## Make the frontier inviting and consequential

Leaving a familiar settlement should feel like crossing into greater uncertainty. A distant silhouette, a missing expedition, unfamiliar tracks or an ordinary person's incomplete account can make the wilderness intriguing before the player knows exactly what is there. Let its beauty, silence, useful resources and inhabitants matter alongside its threats.

Mike's proposed difficult regions can create natural progression through hostile creatures, terrain, weather, isolation and supply demands. Give players observable reasons to prepare, investigate, retreat or seek help. Different preparations can change which places are difficult; combat strength need not explain every frontier. A return journey with a useful discovery can be worthwhile before the deepest objective is reached.

Caves and ancient dungeons offer another direction of expansion. A cellar, ravine or sinkhole can lead to a connected system with depth, alternate approaches and later routes beneath a familiar region. Procedural arrangement can vary passages, inhabitants and discoveries around authored landmarks and historical relationships. Avoid turning depth into a sequence of interchangeable rooms: ventilation, water, habitation and successive uses can explain what players find.

Generation also needs an eventual choice about persistence. In a shared world, an explored chamber, moved object or opened door should have an understandable continuing existence for later visitors. Personal instances would require a deliberate world design. The current spatial owner supports [ground, structures and levels](../spatial-world.md#ground-structures-and-levels); that does not establish a delivered procedural dungeon generator. Reuse [ruins and inhabited dungeons](../repertoires/adventure-discovery.md#ruins-and-inhabited-dungeons) and [cave terrain](../repertoires/ecology-weather.md#ground-caves-and-difficult-terrain) for the content possibilities.

## Let a lost soul have a future as well as a past

Preserve the openness Mike likes in the question about whom the player might find. A lost soul could be a displaced living person, a spirit, a strange creature, a former adversary, a traveler seeking a new life, or someone content with a life the player does not yet understand.

Give the person a present activity, preference, competence or concern before revealing their history. A peculiar habit can invite curiosity. Familiarity, shared work, a discovered object or a place they recognize can create occasions for further understanding. They can decline to explain themselves, and their future need not be limited to resolving an old tragedy. Some encounters can offer friendship, humor, practical help or pleasant company without a hidden quest.

The existing [cairn conversation](../worlds/base/extraordinary-minds-and-afterlife.md#a-conversation-at-the-cairn) is a narrow proposed encounter with a known deceased person. Mike's idea intentionally leaves room for a much wider range of unknown people and beings.

## Use needs to make care and preparation matter

Needs can turn the expedition into a story about particular people: who requires rest, who gives up a dry bed, who notices missing provisions, or who finally feels at home. Food, shelter, recovery, company and privacy can produce different decisions in different circumstances. Let what the player does create observable consequences and opportunities for later interactions, while leaving characters free to interpret the gesture in their own way.

The attraction is the connection between practical care and emotional life. A dependable meal, repaired shelter or reliable friend should sometimes resolve a concern and free attention for adventure. The [needs repertoire](../repertoires/needs.md#selection-guidance) distinguishes bodily requirements, desires and lasting concerns; a richer simulation does not require every concern to become a permanently draining meter. The selected [survival behavior](../worlds/base/survival.md#bodily-conditions-and-chosen-responses) is a smaller existing foundation.

## Additional ideas to explore

Mike explicitly endorsed the first three ideas as creative direction. Their exact mechanics remain unselected; the remaining ideas are suggestions to explore:

1. **A history with several entrances.** A funeral song, damaged observatory and disputed family name lead toward the same authored event. Each offers a useful smaller discovery. Comparing them changes the player's understanding without rewriting the past to match their theory.
2. **Different divine invitations to the same frontier.** An explorer is asked to find a lost sanctuary, a craftsperson to understand its failing mechanism, and a mediator to meet its inhabitants. They can travel together while having different reasons to care and different personal revelations. Different blessings and changing divine relationships can follow how each person plays; the [divine narrative note](gods-death-and-personal-mythology.md#relationships-follow-how-the-player-lives) develops that direction.
3. **A familiar place acquires new meaning.** A learned language or way of perceiving sound reveals that the wind in a familiar ruin carries a damaged message. Another route to the same discovery might involve repair, an old record or a willing teacher.
4. **Waystations make progress tangible.** Restoring a shelter, well or crossing makes a distant journey practical. Players gradually create a route into the wilderness; its usefulness persists and gives other people a reason to visit or contribute.
5. **A stranger's small habit becomes a clue.** Someone quietly repairs broken bells with an unusual technique. A much older record uses the same method. Their identity becomes an intriguing possibility, while what they want now can be quite different from what the player wants to learn.
6. **Needs reveal how a place was lived in.** A dry sleeping shelf, warm draft, old cooking area or exhausted supply cache explains an earlier expedition. Following a practical clue may reveal another passage or a human story embedded in a generated cave.
7. **A shared revelation has a lasting aftermath.** Contributions in several regions restore an ancient network and enable a major opening or divine revelation. Later arrivals encounter the changed world, surviving evidence and further opportunities; they do not need a contradictory replay of the same unique event.
8. **Expeditions leave personal keepsakes.** A group portrait, repaired cup or ordinary blanket can carry meaning because of who was there and what happened. Returning home provides space to remember, change plans or tell another person about the trip. This connects the trailer's photograph to Mike's interest in meaningful collected objects; any recorded memories should follow actual experiences.

## Questions for a selected design

Before turning a candidate into a project, choose the world's authored history and emotional themes, which mysteries can resolve, and which experiences can stand on their own. Then decide what each god may know and promise; how progress is recognized; who gains access when a region opens; what happens when players travel together at different stages; and how later visitors experience permanent changes.

Also choose the useful scope of procedural generation, which sites persist, and how needs can be meaningfully satisfied. None of those choices is settled by admiration for the reference. Existing [world-creation follow-up](../maintainers/needs-design.md#nd01--assemble-a-new-world-from-a-creators-premise), [shared campaigns](../product-scalability/shared-campaigns.md) and [knowledge](../knowledge.md) remain the relevant design owners.

For any future example, keep [world facts, personal understanding and narration](../narration-and-conversations.md#2-three-kinds-of-truth) distinct. Reading a disputed account establishes that it was read, not that its claims are true or that the reader witnessed the original event. The [memory contract](../memory-architecture.md#personal-perspective-and-acquisition) owns that distinction.
