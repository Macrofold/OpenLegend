# Gods, death and personal mythology

**Creative direction and candidate mechanics — October 22, 2026.** Mike wants original gods with deeply authored personalities, changing relationships with players, and unscripted reactions to their lives. Death could bring an encounter, gift, complication or revelation, while a personal realm beyond the shared world could support major authored narrative outcomes. These ideas extend [authored worlds and personal discovery](authored-worlds-and-personal-discovery.md); they are not an implemented pantheon, selected progression specification or universal rule for every Open Legend world.

The immediate reference is [Curse of Resthaven](case-studies/curse-of-resthaven.md), with Hades as a related example of recurring encounters and story continuing across attempts. The reference study distinguishes those games' actual claims from the Open Legend proposals below.

## Persistent characters worth getting to know

Author our own gods. Greek mythology can inspire their recognizable temperaments, competing interests, intimate disputes, generosity, cruelty and vanity, without importing the Greek pantheon or merely renaming its members. Mike wants some helpful gods, some harmful or evil ones, and others whose help is complicated. They can argue, interfere with one another and disagree about the same player.

Their persistence makes deeper authorship worthwhile. A player can gradually discover a god's history, voice, humor, grudges, affections, contradictions and relationships, then see those qualities tested by new circumstances. A god need not disappear after delivering one quest or explanation. A possible late confrontation or death would become meaningful because that relationship has had time to develop.

Give each god concerns beyond combat and the player's advancement. Craft, hospitality, curiosity, survival, promises, forgetting, ownership, weather or the treatment of strangers can connect a deity to ordinary life. These are possible interests to develop, not a chosen list of gods. A divine encounter can be funny, tender, threatening, petty or unexpectedly sincere, following the [character and humor guidance](README.md).

## Relationships follow how the player lives

Mike explicitly endorses different divine invitations to the same frontier and extends the idea to different buffs and changing relationships. How someone plays, the choices they make and the character they become can change which gods take an interest, offer help, distrust them or feel betrayed. A relationship can develop through several encounters and remembered events rather than one declared allegiance.

The same act can mean different things to different gods. In a hypothetical world, rescuing an enemy could be merciful to one deity and a broken oath to another. Those interpretations need not be equally fair or correct. Gods can have incomplete beliefs, prejudices and personal stakes; power does not make their moral judgment authoritative.

Blessings, special items, invitations and access to discoveries can express those relationships. Benefits might help travel, making things, conversation, investigation or recovery as well as fighting. A gift can reveal the giver's priorities, or provoke another god. The player should be able to understand what an accepted gift actually does while still being surprised by the person offering it. Exact durations, stacking, reversibility and tradeoffs remain choices for a selected design.

Mike mentioned Divinity: Original Sin as a loose thematic comparison for divine involvement, while wanting more open reactions here. This is an inspiration note, not a claim that its particular mechanics have been adopted or verified in this study.

## Death can open another encounter

Mike expects frequent deaths and wants returning to feel interesting rather than merely irritating. The bundled world already gives each death a random, removable bodily scar; continuing restores ordinary bodily conditions while the scars remain until treated. [Player death](../worlds/base/player-death.md#death-scars) owns those implemented rules. Frequent death is an intended experience here, not a measured playtest result.

The new proposal adds possible divine involvement: a random buff, a special item, a warning, a new goal, a piece of lore, a changed relationship or a meeting with an unfamiliar god. A deity might help, hurt, bargain, mock, show concern or argue with another deity about the player. The manner of dying, what the player attempted, whom they helped and the relationship already built can all supply meaningful material. Randomness can vary the encounter or gift without making the god's personality arbitrary.

A hypothetical return could involve a god remembering that the player died while getting someone else to safety. Another might object that the rescued person was an enemy. A useful gift could help the next rescue, while the conversation reveals a dispute between the gods. This illustrates the attraction; it does not prescribe those reactions or select a guaranteed reward rule.

Preserve room for quick returns and substantial encounters. A player dying repeatedly should not have to sit through the same speech. Surviving, retreating and recovering should remain worthwhile too. Before choosing rewards, examine whether deliberately dying becomes the most efficient way to gain them and whether the existing scar burden plus a divine penalty makes recovery tedious. These are design questions, not a decision to remove danger, forbid surprising gifts or automatically cancel every injury with a buff.

## Authored personalities with unscripted cognition

Mike wants gods to feel as though they are watching: noticing notable things the player does and responding through open-ended AI cognition, including situations the author did not anticipate. Because these characters persist, their authored background and accumulated relationship can give a spontaneous reaction specificity and continuity.

Author the being's history, outlook, interests, relationships and circumstances. Let its cognition interpret what it learns and choose a response, following [character authorship](../projects/compelling-characters-feature-spec.md#character-authorship-and-changing-personality). A deeply authored personality need not be a fixed sequence of dialogue or a table that forces one reaction to each act. Authored revelations and encounters can coexist with room to question, refuse, bargain or pursue an unexpected subject.

The desired fictional awareness does not settle the technical mechanism. A selected world must decide what each god can notice, what counts as a notable event, what it remembers, and how often it intervenes. Divine awareness may be much broader than ordinary sight or hearing when the world supports it. It still needs actual gameplay evidence; generated speech cannot silently invent a witnessed deed, issue an unsupported gift or change the shared world. Fictional gods also do not gain access to human-private messages or notes. Cost and scale remain unqualified until implemented and measured.

## The Beyond: personal outcomes outside the shared world

Mike proposes a realm **Beyond** the world everyone shares. It could host death encounters, divine relationships, authored revelations and late-game confrontations. Near an endgame, the player might fight and kill a god, and other gods would react to that deed. This can be a real, consequential part of that player's story without necessarily killing the god for every other player.

The attraction is two kinds of continuity working together:

| Where something happens         | What it can mean                                                                                                                      |
| ------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| The shared world                | People, places and events evolve together through players' unpredictable actions; a common physical event has a coherent history.     |
| A player's encounters Beyond it | Relationships, revelations and major divine outcomes can follow that person's journey at their own pace.                              |
| A consequence brought back      | A memory, blessing, item or changed relationship may accompany the player, with its permitted shared-world effects chosen explicitly. |

This extends the earlier shared-campaign idea with a possible personal narrative space. The existing [shared climax](../product-scalability/shared-campaigns.md#7-the-climax-and-irreversible-history) still concerns a genuinely shared adversary and lasting common consequences. The new proposal does not require resetting the shared world or declaring a personal victory to be merely a dream or a noncanonical replay.

The cosmology remains deliberately open: what exactly has died in a personal confrontation, how that relates to the being other players still meet, and what the other gods know about it. [Connected manifestations](../product-scalability/shared-campaigns.md#5-manifestations-aspects-and-distributed-embodiment) offer one source of possibilities, not a selected explanation. Party participation and which consequences can cross back also need decisions before implementation. The existing [cairn encounter](../worlds/base/extraordinary-minds-and-afterlife.md#a-conversation-at-the-cairn) is a much narrower proposal and does not define the scope of this realm.

## Religion is another path into the mystery

Different religions could change how players approach and interact with gods. Rituals, institutions, interpretations and relationships can differ even when worshippers refer to the same being. A player might discover a previously unknown god, an overlooked form of worship, or a disagreement between religious teaching and the god they actually encounter.

Keep room for devotion, skepticism, changing belief, competing allegiances and discovery through practice. Religion can connect divine encounters to people and places in the shared world rather than making every relationship a private menu. The [faith repertoire](../repertoires/faith-ritual.md#gods-as-participants) already includes jealous gods, divine disagreements, newly awakened gods, refused patrons and worshippers holding a deity to an earlier promise. Those seeds can inform an original pantheon without becoming mandatory reactions.

## Less developed alternative: voices within a science-fiction character

Mike's tentative science-fiction variation replaces an external divine voice with a conscience or several aspects of the psyche that speak to the player, loosely inspired by Disco Elysium. Different voices could interpret the same experience differently, making inner conflict a source of personality and narrative commentary.

This is an early idea, not a selected science-fiction world rule or a requirement that every world disguise gods as another system. It remains open whether the voices are literary presentation, distinct minds, technology or something else; whether they grant any effects; and how the player responds to them. Their confidence should not make their interpretations automatically true.

## Connections and next design questions

The [bard and gossip direction](authored-worlds-and-personal-discovery.md#bards-carry-player-legends-and-world-news) can let public deeds enter songs and local religion while personal divine encounters remain known only where they have been experienced or reported. [Relics across official worlds](../game-ideas/official-world-relics.md) offers a possible connection for distinctive divine gifts, without making every gift transferable or adopting account-wide powers here.

Before selecting a project, choose a world and a small original cast; what they care about and can know; how relationships and gifts change; the rhythm and consequences of death encounters; the meaning of killing a god Beyond the shared world; and which effects are personal, shared or transferable. The purpose of this note is to preserve Mike's full direction while those choices remain open.
