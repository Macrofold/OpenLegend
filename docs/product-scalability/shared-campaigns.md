# Shared campaigns, local sieges, and canonical adversaries

**Status: accepted campaign direction with proposed mechanics and unresolved tuning.** This page owns product participation and causality across communities, not cross-server deployment or networking. Read [worlds and belonging](worlds-and-belonging.md), [participation](participation-and-protection.md), and [capacity](capacity-and-economics.md) together with it.

## 1. The participation promise

Players across many communities should have meaningful ways to oppose a shared adversary without all needing to attack one body in one room. Their participation should remain close enough to their homes, relationships, and institutions that the conflict feels like part of their world rather than a detached matchmaking lobby.

One canonical villain can have distributed influence, finite sources of power, servants, logistics, and explicit manifestations. These are actual parts of the same conflict, not thousands of contradictory copies of its final defeat. A single mortal embodiment remains subject to travel, time, and local capacity unless the world explicitly supports a different embodiment law.

The point of cooperation is causal interdependence: participants can see how another community's action enabled theirs. A contribution counter can help presentation, but it should not replace meaningful changes in the world.

## 2. Distributed sources of power

A God of Darkness might depend on shrines, gates, imprisoned spirits, controlled territory, a supply network, or loyalty from mortal factions. Each source has an actual location, identity, effects, vulnerabilities, and lifecycle. Removing one changes something specific.

For example, destroying the western shrine disables a reinforcement route to Greyharbor. Freeing a spirit removes a particular protection from a manifestation. Negotiating with a supporting faction reduces its supply of siege equipment. These are illustrative mechanics, not universal required fantasy content or hidden canned recipes.

Keep the network bounded and legible. Unlimited regenerated anchors with no enduring effect would make local victories feel cosmetic. Replenishment can exist, but it must be an actual costly/world-supported process with visible consequences rather than an administrative undo to preserve a boss schedule.

Some information can be hidden or uncertain. Players may discover which source matters through exploration and relationships. The villain likewise acts on the information its agents and supported senses acquire, not an omniscient feed of all player intentions.

## 3. Local fronts and forecast sieges

The preferred campaign includes large events with understandable approximate timing. An invasion can advance city by city, threaten transport routes, besiege settlements, and bring meaningful encounters to the players who live there. Ordinary play continues outside these windows.

Publish an approximate local arrival/operation window, its uncertainty, the reason for the forecast, and how updates will be communicated. Show real-world local time as well as a fictional date where useful. An approximate forecast is not a guarantee that every participant will see the villain at an exact minute.

A siege can have several meaningful phases: preparation, scouting, defense of approaches, the main assault, evacuation or counterattack, and aftermath. Players can contribute before and after the peak battle through building, diplomacy, discovery, supplies, rescue, and rebuilding. Do not make every valuable contribution require attendance during one narrow global hour.

Rotate or distribute substantial opportunities across time zones and communities. Provide local progress and warnings without requiring a player to monitor the game continuously. Privacy and communication permissions apply to notifications and reported locations. This document requests an in-game product capability, not an external automation task.

### Forecasts are constrained by causality

Player actions can delay, redirect, weaken, or prevent an invasion. Update the forecast rather than force the scheduled attack to occur anyway. If a bridge is destroyed or the villain loses a gate, the route and arrival window should reflect the admitted consequences.

Conversely, routine scheduling should not be so volatile that players cannot reasonably plan. Use bounded commitments once an operation has been announced, explicit uncertainty before commitment, and understandable reasons for revisions. Exact notice periods, protected participation windows, and revision rules need playtesting; do not silently promise an appointment the simulation cannot keep.

The director can create pressures and opportunities under world authority. It cannot rewrite a completed defeat, conjure impossible forces, or ignore a successful preventative action merely to ensure that everyone receives the same content.

## 4. A city-by-city example

The campaign announces evidence that the adversary's host is moving toward Ashvale, with a likely local siege window. Ashvale's players build defenses, seek allies, investigate its source of reinforcements, and prepare evacuation routes. Nearby communities can send real supplies or participants within their permissions.

An expedition severs a power source. The campaign records that the upcoming host lacks its previous reinforcement capability. Ashvale's defenders see that consequence during their local assault. Whether they hold the city affects which routes and resources remain available to the adversary.

The host later threatens Greyharbor under a revised forecast. This is the same continuing campaign, not a reset of Ashvale's battle in another shard. Ashvale's outcome changes Greyharbor's conditions. Defeated defenders can still contribute through refugees, knowledge, repair, or a later counteroffensive rather than being permanently excluded from meaningful play.

A unique physical villain must actually travel between appearances. Simultaneous local confrontations require distinct lieutenants or explicitly supported manifestations, not a silent duplicate canonical body. Protect local continuity: the siege does not replace the town with a disposable instance, and its aftermath persists under the world's agreed property and protection policies.

## 5. Manifestations, aspects, and distributed embodiment

A supernatural antagonist can have several genuine manifestations with distinct identity, local knowledge, powers, and consequences while remaining connected to one canonical being. Destroying an aspect could remove sight in a region, weaken a shared ability, or consume a finite strategic resource.

Define what the global mind shares with its aspects, how quickly information moves, which resources are shared, and what damage transfers. Do not infer global omniscience or instant consensus from a common name. A local manifestation cannot make mutually incompatible global commitments concurrently.

Worlds may permit possession, projection, or reincarnation through admitted capabilities. The lore is not permission to bypass player privacy, domain access, physical admission, or compute budgets. If local victories never limit anything, call the encounters expendable forces rather than imply a meaningful shared embodiment.

## 6. Cooperation with complementary roles

Offer multiple useful approaches: combat, reconnaissance, construction, logistics, diplomacy, evacuation, invention, sabotage, and recovery where supported. Players should discover methods through the world's actual mechanics rather than merely filling mandatory class slots or completing a fixed checklist disguised as emergence.

Make dependencies visible enough to coordinate: which gate needs defense, why a material delivery matters, what an invention enables, and which community is waiting for support. Preserve uncertain intelligence and misinformation as such; an in-world report is not automatically objective campaign truth.

Recognize contributions specifically. The historical account might name a settlement that severed an anchor, the people who carried a vital warning, and the defenders of a bridge. Do not reduce recognition to the last damaging hit, raw damage totals, or paid access. Support participants who made a small but real contribution without manufacturing personal credit for events they never joined.

Campaign tasks must not grant one group indefinite veto over everyone else's participation. Provide alternative routes or recovery methods when appropriate, while preserving consequences rather than nullifying a failed objective. Optional side fronts and wider windows can absorb crowding without cloning the same victory.

## 7. The climax and irreversible history

A final operation can span several connected fronts: disabling anchors, holding routes, defending a ritual, evacuating civilians, and confronting a core embodiment. These are concurrent actual events. Only the parts requiring tight physical interaction need share that local capacity envelope.

There is an irreducible product choice: one unique final death, permanent history, and a guarantee that every current and future player personally delivers the decisive blow cannot all hold. Prefer broad meaningful participation and specific historical recognition over duplicate endings.

Later players can encounter consequences, surviving factions, successors, new conflicts, archives, or explicitly noncanonical reenactments. A historical retelling or optional dream is not a second canonical killing of the same person. Such modes are secondary possibilities, not the primary shared-world model.

The game should not become inert after a major victory. New pressures can emerge from actual aftermath, other adversaries, ecological change, institutions, or newly available capabilities. Do not automatically resurrect the same villain just because a content treadmill requires it.

## 8. Bounded strategic coordination

Keep local detailed actions and most NPC cognition local. A future campaign owner maintains the relatively small set of shared strategic facts: active fronts, surviving sources, resources, territorial effects, canonical objectives, and phase changes. It receives validated meaningful outcomes, not every footstep or every private thought.

Each accepted contribution needs stable identity and provenance so retries cannot destroy the same source twice or award duplicate effects. Conflicting outcomes need one explicit authority and resolution. Local information can propagate with disclosed or fictional delay where valid; instantaneous global coordination should be reserved for mechanics that actually require it.

The villain's strategic cognition can use aggregated legitimate reports and decide broad intentions; local agents execute within their knowledge and delegated authority. A more capable strategic model can amortize across many players, but its workflow and reports remain bounded and funded. Shared infrastructure does not require a global mind to process all participants' sensory histories.

Operational transfer, receipt durability, and recovery belong to the existing data/distribution owners. The product invariant is that accepted local consequences affecting the shared campaign cannot later vanish independently through a quiet world's rollback.

## 9. Opt-in worlds and compatible powers

Campaign participation requires compatible world rules, import policies, authority, and outcome validation. A creator who can manufacture unlimited weapons or rewrite local outcomes cannot automatically submit equivalent competitive victories. Offer explicit independent or noncompetitive modes rather than pretending those worlds share identical stakes.

Private domains may opt out, allow visitors only, accept preparatory activity, or join broader risks under their permissions. The God of Darkness cannot override a private domain's access controls because a narrative says it is a god. Human-private state and real spending remain protected regardless of fiction.

Clock differences need a clear real-time schedule mapping and production/contribution policy. A fast calendar is not unlimited preparation advantage. Forecasts must not expose unpublished operations or private locations beyond the world's information rules.

## 10. Capacity and fairness during major events

Forecasting demand helps preparation but does not abolish local density limits. Publish capacity conditions, reserve appropriate resident/reconnect access, and provide useful nearby fronts or actual broadcasts. Do not advertise unrestricted personal access to a unique body when only a bounded encounter is supported.

Avoid requiring every community to teleport elsewhere for all meaningful action. Local sieges, lieutenants, power sources, and distinct manifestations distribute participation while the antagonist remains canonical. Queues may still be necessary at a popular local encounter; be honest early rather than let people travel into a broken event.

A scheduled event should not covertly revoke accepted property protection, enable attacks in a safe venue, or alter hostile AI quality to shed load. Use agreed campaign risk policies and capacity admission. Event rewards and competitive fairness cannot depend on a hidden low-power boundary exploit.

## 11. Acceptance and open questions

Demonstrate a small multi-community campaign in which one local action changes another front, an invasion is forecast, players delay or prevent it, and the forecast updates coherently. Show distinct local encounters with no duplicated body, contribution, or final death. Include a private opted-out domain, an incompatible creative world, an absent player, and a capacity-constrained peak.

Open tuning includes notice windows, forecast confidence vocabulary, revision commitments, community rotation, participation reservations, contribution recognition, and embodiment costs. These must be decided from playability and sustainable demand, not inferred from the examples. The [limits register](../limits/product-scalability.md) owns these decisions.
