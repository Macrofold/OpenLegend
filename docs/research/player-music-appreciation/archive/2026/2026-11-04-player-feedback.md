# Why players love game music

**Study completed: November 4, 2026. Scope: public player reception and research synthesis.**

[Current synthesis](../../README.md) · [Source ledger](2026-11-04-sources.md) · [Research index](../../../README.md)

This study asks which soundtracks attract strong player affection, what players value about them, and when those same qualities become unwelcome. It does not rank composition, recording quality, production spending or composers. It makes no Open Legend musical, artistic, technical or procurement decision.

**Spoilers:** the comparisons discuss musical reveals, late-game progression, endings and changes during boss encounters. They generally omit plot outcomes, but the linked discussions often do not.

## Main findings

Players in these sources value music as part of a remembered experience: a place they inhabit, an encounter they overcome, a character they recognize, a routine they enjoy, or a moment that makes their progress meaningful. Melody, rhythm, texture and voices matter, but players frequently explain their attachment by describing what happened while the music played. This pattern appears across narrative adventures, action games, persistent worlds and ordinary daily play. [E01], [E05], [P05], [P13], [H03], [X10]

There is no defensible universal winner in this evidence. Different questions produce different favorites: best to hear while exploring, most emotionally affecting, funniest, most energizing, most distinctive, or best to replay outside the game. The games below form a deliberately varied comparison set, not a population ranking.

Three distinctions are especially useful:

- **A great song, a great soundtrack and a great musical moment are different successes.** A listener can love an album while disliking its repetition in play, or treasure a contextual ending without choosing it for everyday listening. [H14], [H17], [E09]
- **Effective music need not produce a pleasant emotion.** Players can value grief, unease, guilt, loneliness or bittersweet nostalgia alongside delight, reassurance and excitement. [E04], [P11], [H19], [X19]
- **Affection and tolerance change with exposure and circumstances.** A welcome routine can become an exhausting loop. Muting can reflect a household situation, a podcast, concentration or accumulated exposure rather than rejection of the score. [P09], [P17], [X15], [X21]

The most informative testimony describes a voluntary action: stopping to listen, delaying a fight, returning to a location, seeking a track name, singing later, choosing the music for another activity, or replaying a game partly to hear a moment again. These reports give concrete meaning to appreciation. They remain anecdotes, not telemetry or proof that music increases retention.

## Method and evidence limits

### Collection and inclusion

The investigation used purposive English-language web research, moving from broad favorite-soundtrack and outside-listening discussions into focused searches for particular games, tracks, situations and complaints. Example query families included “favorite game soundtrack,” “soundtrack listen outside game,” “stop to listen,” “music makes me feel,” “music repetitive,” “music off,” and game-specific combinations with Steam, Reddit, forums and YouTube.

The comparison deliberately includes different player activities and emotional functions: repeated survival or management play; quiet exploration; seasonal and hourly routines; difficult action; narrative payoffs; comedy; horror; and music carried into ordinary life. Negative and mixed accounts were sought beside praise. This improves the explanation of limits but does not remove selection bias.

The ledger retains **84 source records**: 62 focused records in the E, P and H groups, plus 22 discovery, complementary-case, audience-vote and research records in the X group. A record is a thread, review, related source family or study—not a respondent. Multiple pages of the same Expedition 33 thread count once; a Zelda cross-post is kept in one record; the two papers are supporting research rather than newly collected player testimony. The same Steam reviewer wrote E10 and E19. No unique-player count, sentiment percentage or formal ranking was computed.

Evidence was retained when it offered a specific appreciation, aversion, use, comparison or relevant audience vote. Bare nominations helped discover candidates but received less interpretive weight. Professional criticism, awards decided by juries, composer intentions, production anecdotes, sales claims and listening totals were not treated as substitutes for player reception.

### What was actually accessible

| Evidence class | Use in this study | Limit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Returned page text | Inspectable Steam reviews/discussions, forums, selected Reddit pages and published voting results | Only the returned text or displayed page was inspected; a retrieved first page does not establish review of a whole thread. |
| Indexed excerpts | Substantive post/comment text exposed by search when direct opening failed | Supports only the visible account. Replies, edits, sarcasm, chronology and surrounding disagreement may be missing. |
| Audience-vote results | Separate evidence of preference within a named community and year | Self-selection, eligible games, platform reach and voting rules prevent a universal comparison. |
| YouTube creator description | One fan's stated attachment and performance project | The video was not watched and its comments/transcript were not inspected. |
| Published research | A YouTube-comment analysis and a small controlled study | The former was read through its abstract and author-group summary; the latter through its paper. Neither is this study's own dataset. |

Steam **user reviews** and **discussion posts** are labeled separately. Whole-game recommendation scores are not music ratings. YouTube searches did not yield a directly inspectable comment corpus; this study therefore does not claim an original YouTube-comment analysis. X07 supplies relevant published analysis of such comments, while X09 supplies only an accessible creator description.

### Confidence and transfer

The strongest conclusions concern recurring *kinds* of appreciation, because related accounts appear across games and communities. Specific anecdotes explain possibilities; they cannot establish their frequency. The controlled study can address a narrow causal question, but it cannot validate the whole synthesis.

The sample overrepresents internet-active enthusiasts, memorable games, people motivated to discuss music, and English-language communities. Search ranking, thread prompts, survivorship, nostalgia, recency, fandom and platform access all affect what appears. There was no random sample, systematic review protocol, complete comment census, independent coding agreement or comparison of soundtrack budgets. Public accounts also cannot isolate music from visuals, gameplay, story and personal history.

This is a qualitative study of appreciation and its limits. It does not establish that a technique causes commercial success, that a majority prefers a particular style, or that these responses transfer unchanged to Open Legend.

## Which soundtracks are useful examples of strong player affection?

### Broad discovery and actual community votes

Cross-game favorite and outside-listening discussions repeatedly bring forward games such as NieR, Final Fantasy, Chrono Trigger, Skyrim, Hollow Knight, Supergiant's games and DOOM. Those threads are useful discovery routes, but their prompts and visible replies do not provide a controlled nomination count. This study follows the detailed reasons into the cases below instead of converting a few threads into a “top ten.” [X01], [X02], [X03]

There is stronger evidence for a narrower claim when an actual audience vote is available:

| Community and scope | Observed result | What the result establishes |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ResetEra, game soundtracks of 2024 | Final Fantasy VII Rebirth placed first; the indexed results report 849 points, 90 votes and 65 top placements. | It led that community's published 2024 ballot. Ninety is the title's reported vote count, not a count of all participants. [X04] |
| ResetEra, game soundtracks of 2025 | Clair Obscur: Expedition 33 placed first; the indexed results report 823 points, 91 votes and 53 top placements. | It led that community's published 2025 ballot. Points and title votes cannot be compared directly with unrelated polls. [X05] |
| PlayStation Blog, player-voted 2025 awards | Expedition 33 won Best Soundtrack, followed by Ghost of Yōtei, Death Stranding 2 and Hollow Knight: Silksong. | A second, platform-associated voting audience favored Expedition 33. The announced total of more than 1.5 million votes covers 19 categories, not soundtrack voters alone. [X06] |

The polls make Rebirth and Expedition 33 credible recent affection examples. They do not establish why a particular player cares, how older games would fare in the same ballot, or a ranking across all games. Ghost of Yōtei, Death Stranding 2 and Silksong are vote signals here; they did not receive the detailed case research below.

## What makes the music effective in these accounts?

### 1. It gives players something musically attractive to return to

Catchy melodies, compelling rhythms, striking voices, satisfying textures and stylistic variety appear in the reports. Players seek names, hum tunes and choose to listen again. Those are genuine musical pleasures, not merely narrative effects. Katamari and Outer Wilds accounts also show music attracting a listener before a completed playthrough; a separate forum discusses favorite music from games the posters have never personally played. [H01], [H15], [E02], [X22]

The available comments are better evidence of attraction than of a technical recipe. A fan calling something catchy does not specify the intervals, arrangement, recording process or production budget that caused the response. The evidence cannot select a universal instrumentation, genre, level of complexity or spending threshold.

### 2. It acquires meaning through an experienced situation

A cue can become attached to reaching a place, recognizing a character, surviving a challenge or understanding an ending. Later listening then recalls more than sound. Recurrent musical material can make the present moment feel connected with earlier ones; the player recognizes both the similarity and the change. Undertale, To the Moon and FFXIV accounts make this relationship explicit. [E05], [E12], [E19]

This is an interpretation of reported experience, not proof that a recurring motif independently creates attachment. The story or place may already matter, and music may help recall that value. The practical research distinction is between liking an isolated track and caring about what hearing it means.

### 3. It helps a place or routine develop a recognizable emotional character

Place themes and recurring daily music can make ordinary play feel worth inhabiting. The attachment need not depend on a climactic scene: farming, working, waiting, looking at rain or returning to a familiar zone may be the experience a player values. The Stardew Valley, Animal Crossing, Skyrim and World of Warcraft accounts are particularly useful comparisons. [P03], [P05], [P07], [P10]

A familiar musical identity can also become an expectation against which new material is judged. A person may like a new composition but feel that it belongs to a different world. That is a question of learned fit and continuity, not automatically evidence of poor music or mere resistance to change. [P02], [X16]

### 4. It supports the player's changing effort and attention

The action cases describe bodily involvement, confidence, persistence and attention. Musical changes can make effort feel as if it is going somewhere. A climax gains meaning from what precedes it; a constantly intense presentation cannot supply the same contrast merely by remaining intense. Metal Gear Rising and Celeste offer specific accounts of these different relationships. [H04], [X10]

The relationship is not uniformly beneficial. A busy presentation can compete with attention, and a player trying to survive may miss a musical detail. Some prefer another activity's audio; others want the game's beat to hold their attention. These are differences in context and listening needs, not a simple division between attentive and inattentive players. [H08], [H11], [H12], [X21]

### 5. It appears to acknowledge events, characters and consequences

Players notice when music changes with what is happening. FTL's reported exploration/combat relationship and Hades II's reported performance changes are examples of music feeling connected to a situation. Hades II also shows that an adaptive detail can be appreciated by one person and unnoticed by another. [P18], [H09]

The evidence supports perceived correspondence. It does not establish that a more complex music system is better, that every change must be consciously noticed, or that a particular middleware approach produced the effect. Musical implementation was not audited in this study.

### 6. It uses contrast, quiet and relief as meaningful experiences

Silence or reduced music can leave room for a place, produce relief after pressure, or change the interpretation of an action. Breath of the Wild, Hollow Knight, Resident Evil and Hotline Miami show different responses to space and contrast. These are not interchangeable uses: quiet exploration, temporary refuge and disturbing aftermath do different emotional work. [X12], [P13], [X19], [H19]

There is also real demand for prominent musical identity. A sparse score may support one player's attention while leaving another without the themes they expected. The evidence supports matching a musical presence to an experience and audience, not an abstract rule that less music is better. [X13]

### 7. It lets humor belong to a person or situation

The Hades II and Portal examples make humor more specific than “use a silly tune.” Players respond to a performer, a rivalry, an unexpected continuation of an encounter, or a character's peculiar perspective. The joke can include what the game allows the player to do while listening. [H07], [H17], [H18]

A comic song must still survive taste and exposure. A Hades II discussion of the first encounter's song separates enjoyment of the fight from enjoyment of its lyrics; it does not assess the later diss-song reveal. An initially funny Portal ending can also become tiresome through widespread repetition. A memorable joke is therefore evidence of a successful moment for some players, not proof of enduring replay value for everyone. [H10], [H18]

### 8. It offers emotions with different purposes, including mixed ones

The vocabulary in the evidence is broader than happy, sad and epic. Players report safety, curiosity, loneliness, tenderness, resolve, nostalgia, unease, relief, surprise, absurdity, grief and closure. Some want a soundtrack to support an existing feeling; others enjoy a shift or complication. A horror save room can soothe without eliminating threat; a familiar zone can be comforting and painful at once. [E04], [P11], [X19]

The most moving track also need not be the most replayable. Distinguishing peak emotion from everyday preference helps explain why two sincere “favorite soundtrack” answers may be addressing different needs. [E09]

### 9. It continues into personal life and shared activity

Outside listening is a strong sign that a player values the music beyond its immediate use in a game. The accounts include work, study, exercise, driving, decompression, singing, fan analysis and returning to memories when there is no time to play. These uses demonstrate several forms of affection rather than one uniform measure. [P03], [P04], [E06], [E15], [H06], [H16], [H20]

They also complicate nostalgia. A track may recall childhood, a recent playthrough, a person, a family habit or a difficult period. A song can attract a listener before the game, and watching someone else play can provide context without firsthand play. Musical attraction and autobiographical association can reinforce each other. [P01], [H15], [X22]

### 10. It remains welcome across the player's actual exposure

Repeated listening can create familiarity or fatigue. Exposure is shaped by how long a player stays, how often they fail, how much they revisit, whether the same words demand attention, and what else they want to hear. The evidence contains requests to replace an iconic Undertale cue, expand a fitting RimWorld repertoire, mute beloved Animal Crossing music, or escape a repeated Expedition 33 cue. [E22], [P17], [P09], [X15]

Consequently, an album's appeal is insufficient evidence of its suitability for a long gameplay loop. Conversely, muting alone is an ambiguous signal: a player may love the music and still prefer household awareness, an audiobook, environmental sounds or a different mood on that occasion. [X21]

## What published research adds

**YouTube reception:** Laato and Rauti's HICSS 2022 study analyzed 1,893 comments on 20 early-game and final-boss songs. Its abstract and author-group summary report that comments often concerned game or life associations; early-game selections attracted home, nostalgia and sadness, while final-boss selections more often evoked gameplay. This supports the value of studying remembered situations. It is a selected adventure-game sample, not a census of all game music or evidence that nostalgia explains every preference. [X07]

**Causal limits:** Roberts and colleagues' 2024 experiment recruited 36 participants and analyzed 32 in one firefighting game with two contrasting musical treatments. Powerful music increased reported tension and participants completed the task faster. Enjoyment and several other experience measures did not differ significantly; direct risk-taking measures also did not show a significant difference. The small, mostly young-student sample and two tracks limit transfer. It supports the possibility of specific experiential effects, not “more intense music makes games more enjoyable.” [X08]

Together these sources strengthen a careful claim: music can be associated with meaning and can affect particular aspects of play. They do not justify treating every favorable anecdote as a causal effect, or every emotional response as improved enjoyment.

## Failure modes and disagreements

| Tempting conclusion | Counterevidence | Better-supported interpretation |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A beloved track will always improve the scene. | FFXIV placement criticism; Persona's standalone-versus-loop disagreement. [E13], [H14] | Composition, placement and duration require separate judgments. |
| Familiarity is always comforting. | Painful WoW nostalgia; Undertale repetition fatigue; Portal social overplay. [P11], [E22], [H18] | Familiarity can deepen attachment, boredom or aversion. |
| More music or more variety is always better. | Zelda's valued restraint; RimWorld additions perceived as a poor fit. [X12], [P17] | Amount and variety do not replace contextual fit. |
| A sparse soundtrack is necessarily more immersive. | A Zelda player misses prominent musical identity. [X13] | Different listeners value different relationships between music and environment. |
| An appreciated dynamic detail will be noticed by every player. | Hades II participants disagree about noticing a reported instrumental change. [H09] | The evidence establishes appreciation by some, not a universal attention response. |
| If a player mutes music, the soundtrack failed. | Household constraints, personal audio, ambience preference and exposure appear in muting accounts. [X21], [P09] | The reason for muting matters. |
| Liking the soundtrack means liking the game. | Outer Wilds, Journey, Celeste and To the Moon contain split judgments. [E03], [E18], [X11], [E20] | Score appreciation is a distinct outcome. |
| Emotional effectiveness means happiness or tears. | Determination, temporary safety, guilt and humor are also described. [E04], [X19], [H19], [H07] | Several emotional functions can be valued, sometimes within one game. |
| Success can be inferred from views, budgets or game review scores. | The sampled evidence does not isolate any of those variables as a music-appreciation measure. | Use explicit reception and behavioral descriptions; keep population and causal claims limited. |

## Connections to Open Legend: questions, not direction

The repository describes an authored-realities engine and a shared survival/adventure world with useful invention, independent people, exploration, persistent consequences and ordinary life. The [gameplay priorities](../../../../repertoires/gameplay-priorities.md), [design foundation](../../../../repertoires/design-foundation.md), [base-world overview](../../../../worlds/base/README.md) and [narrative model](../../../../narrative/README.md) supply that context. They make the place, routine, character, effort and emotional-contrast cases relevant comparisons.

The research raises several questions that could help interpret future player feedback without selecting a musical answer:

- **Home and ordinary life:** Do players describe music as contributing to belonging, or do they prefer the sounds of activity and environment in that situation? Stardew Valley, Skyrim and Animal Crossing show both possibilities.
- **Long and variable sessions:** Does affection survive repeated construction, travel, recovery and return visits? RimWorld and the fatigue cases show why an impressive first hearing does not settle that question.
- **Independent characters and humor:** When a musical encounter is meaningful, are players responding to the person and situation, the song itself, or both? Hades and Portal provide comparisons without requiring that Open Legend adopt musical characters or comic songs.
- **Consequence and progress:** What experienced change makes a musical moment meaningful to a player? The narrative and mastery cases suggest looking at actual play rather than assuming an intended emotional label was achieved.
- **Different player purposes:** Are players seeking concentration, companionship, excitement, relaxation, environmental awareness or their own audio at that moment? The evidence supports asking the reason behind listening and muting behavior.

The existing [audio and feedback workflow](../../../../production-workflows/audio-and-feedback.md) remains the procedural owner, and the [art, audio and game-feel synthesis](../../../../../archive/02-research/game-inspiration/synthesis/art-audio-and-game-feel.md) remains the broader presentation-research owner. Soundtrack presentation does not establish physical facts, character knowledge or permission in the simulation.

**No music direction is selected here.** The study does not choose genres, instruments, composers, sonic palettes, themes, motif assignments, cue lists, adaptive architecture, asset sources, budgets or production priorities. The questions above are interpretive connections, not approved requirements or new implementation tasks.

## What remains unknown

This study cannot determine a representative all-time popularity ranking, preference percentages, demographic differences, an optimal amount of repetition, a required production budget, or effects on retention and sales. It also cannot establish which approach Open Legend's eventual players would value.

The most useful extensions would answer a specific unresolved question: multilingual reception; music appreciation among less vocal or habitual-muting players; direct, systematically sampled YouTube comments; distinctions between first hearing and long-term exposure; and situations where players want music versus environmental sound. These are possible future research scopes, not unfinished obligations within this completed public-source study.

The durable result is a set of explained cases and bounded conclusions: players can value music for attraction, meaning, place, effort, responsiveness, contrast, character, emotional range and personal association, while rejecting the same material when timing, repetition, fit or attention costs change.
