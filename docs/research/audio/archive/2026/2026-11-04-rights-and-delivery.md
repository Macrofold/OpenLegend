# Audio rights, release scope and browser delivery

Research checked November 4, 2026. This record connects licensing and production research to Open Legend's existing contracts. Recommendations are procurement and implementation inputs, not a claim that audio playback, rights clearance or player qualification is finished. No audio was generated, purchased, downloaded or auditioned in this investigation.

## Separate the questions before buying anything

The composition and its recorded performance are separate works. Owning or licensing a recording does not automatically clear the melody and lyrics beneath it; owning a composition does not clear somebody else's performance. This matters for a licensed song, an arrangement of an old tune and a commissioned soundtrack. The U.S. Copyright Office explains the distinction in [Circular 56A](https://copyright.gov/circs/circ56a.pdf) and its [music recording guide](https://copyright.gov/engage/docs/recording.pdf).

For Open Legend, ask four independent questions:

1. **Permission:** can this exact recording be incorporated into the sold or hosted game, edited as needed, and delivered to players on the intended platforms?
2. **Control:** can we revise, extend and re-record its themes, appoint another contributor later, and keep shipping if a subscription or collaboration ends?
3. **Distinctiveness:** can competitors license the same recording, or obtain similar generated output? A useful nonexclusive effect and a signature theme have different needs.
4. **Release reach:** do trailers, player videos, soundtracks, self-hosting and creator exports have the necessary permissions too?

Commercial-use permission, copyright ownership, exclusivity and indemnity are different answers. A provider's statement that its model is licensed is relevant evidence about its production route; it is not a warranty that every output is unique, registrable, or cleared for every downstream use. Preserve the exact plan and contract, not just a pricing-page badge.

## Specify the actual uses

This is the proposed rights brief to use when comparing a license or requesting a quote. It does not assert that any candidate currently grants all of these rights.

| Intended use | What must be established | Why it matters here |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Commercial browser game | Incorporation, hosting, delivery/cache copies and commercial play on the official service | A social-video subscription is not automatically a game license. |
| Future ports and stores | Platforms, territories, term, distribution limits and any additional fees | Do not pay for console rights now if inappropriate, but understand the extension path. |
| Demo, trailer, ads and crowdfunding | Promotional synchronization, paid advertising and publication after subscription expiry | A game license can leave marketing outside its scope. |
| Player streaming and videos | Monetized gameplay, clips, reviews, VODs and a practical claims policy | Players should be able to show the game without routinely losing audio or revenue. |
| Soundtrack album | Standalone audio distribution, sales, streaming, artwork/credits and revenue allocation | Permission to embed a song does not imply permission to sell the song itself. |
| Editing and adaptive playback | Cuts, loops, fades, stems, arrangement changes and derived versions | Necessary edits should be anticipated in the license, not improvised after acquisition. |
| Collaborators | Contractor access, seats, project transfer, secure source handoff and deletion obligations | Paying one seat may not authorize sharing the entire library with a team. |
| Creator worlds and self-hosts | Exact sublicensing and distribution rights for official world packs and third-party operators | Open Legend has ambitions beyond one closed executable. |
| Raw source or asset export | Whether source audio may leave our licensed production team at all | Publicly distributing a sample library is different from shipping a mixed effect in a game. |
| Future operations | Successors, publisher/distributor grants, continued sales, patches, termination and replacement | A soundtrack should not become unusable because the project changes business arrangements. |

Do not demand the same expensive rights bundle from every supplier. For signature music, broad lasting control is valuable. For routine licensed effects, a sufficiently broad game-use license with no raw redistribution may be entirely adequate. Keep optional OST and creator-export rights visible so they cannot be assumed accidentally.

## Commissioning: control without a needlessly hostile deal

An invoice alone is not a reliable copyright transfer. Under the U.S. framework, work made for hire depends on the employment circumstances or a qualifying commissioned category and signed agreement. A label in an email does not resolve every case. Use an appropriate written assignment or sufficiently broad exclusive license, with legal review of the final arrangement where needed. [Copyright Office, Circular 30](https://copyright.gov/circs/circ30.pdf).

Our recommended negotiation brief:

- Identify both composition and master-recording rights, the actual contracting entity, credited contributors and any publisher or performing-rights affiliation.
- Prefer ownership or lasting exclusive control over the signature themes and commissioned masters. A narrower exclusive game/franchise license can be a sensible price tradeoff if adaptations, continued shipping and replacement-contributor rights remain adequate.
- State how paid sketches, rejected ideas, accepted cues, revisions and cancellation work. Do not require unlimited revisions or unpaid speculative composition from a shortlist.
- Set the deliverables and approval milestones before quoting. Minutes of new composition, alternate mixes, stems, transitions and implementation are separate workload drivers.
- Identify samples, session performers, vocal permissions, outside arrangements and any generative AI. Agree whether uploads to AI services and their onward licenses are permitted before uploading unreleased material.
- Reserve the ability to edit and maintain shipped assets. Obtain rendered stems as a practical preservation layer even when a composer retains their DAW project or uses plugins we cannot redistribute.
- Set soundtrack release, royalty splits, credit and promotional use explicitly. A fair OST share can improve a collaboration without tying compensation to an ambiguous share of the entire game's revenue.
- Decide who controls distributor fingerprinting and resolves claims. Avoid an automatic music-distributor setting silently overriding the game's creator-video policy.

These are proposed commercial terms to negotiate, not a universal contract or a claim that every composer will accept a buyout at the same rate.

## AI output and copyright

The U.S. Copyright Office's January 2025 report concludes that human-authored expression can be protected when AI is used, but prompting alone does not ordinarily supply sufficient human control over the generated expressive elements. Human composition, arrangement or modifications are assessed case by case. [Official release](https://www.copyright.gov/newsnet/2025/1060.html) · [full report](https://www.copyright.gov/ai/Copyright-and-Artificial-Intelligence-Part-2-Copyrightability-Report.pdf).

Practical implications: a commercially permitted AI track can still be useful in a sold game. Conversely, a contractual ownership statement does not guarantee that we can stop someone else using unprotected material. Merely cleaning a mix, changing its format or having a musician replay it should not be treated as automatic copyright clearance or ownership of the underlying generated composition. Preserve evidence of actual human contributions and disclose generated material accurately when registration is sought. Rules outside the United States may differ; the cited baseline is not a worldwide clearance.

The route-specific research distinguishes provider permission from these copyright questions. It also distinguishes editing generated audio from independently composing a new work using an abstract mood brief. Re-recording an infringing melody would not solve the underlying problem.

## Protect players' ability to share the game

YouTube requires appropriate exclusive rights for Content ID references and excludes nonexclusive licensed material, Creative Commons/public-domain material, sound effects and production loops from eligible references in the ways described by its policy. Game OST references have publisher-specific requirements. This is an eligibility system, not proof that a track is lawful or unlawful to use. [Content ID policy](https://support.google.com/youtube/answer/2605065?hl=en).

Recommended release behavior:

- Choose a rights arrangement that allows ordinary monetized gameplay videos and streams; publish that permission in plain language when the game ships.
- Make no promises for third-party music beyond the rights actually obtained. Resolve exclusions before accepting a signature cue.
- Coordinate the composer, label and distributor so authorized gameplay is not automatically claimed. Keep one named contact and a practical release process for mistaken claims.
- If a remaining track cannot be covered, provide a clear replacement/mute route for it. Prefer resolving the rights once so creators hear the intended score, rather than making most players discover a diminished soundtrack mode.
- Do not enroll common stock effects or a nonexclusive generated/library recording in fingerprinting merely because we paid for it.

A private upload can reveal some claims at that moment; it does not prove future claim-free use. A contractual creator policy and a working claims process remain more useful than a single test upload.

## Open-source code and separately licensed audio

[Open Legend's licensing policy](../../../../../LICENSING.md) applies AGPL-3.0-only to first-party material without a separate notice, while third-party works retain their rights and independent content packages declare their own terms. Do not apply the repository's license to a purchased song or promise a redistributable official soundtrack without the necessary grant.

The [asset workflow](../../../../asset-workflow.md) already keeps media and editable source payloads outside Git. Audio should follow that same owner, including immutable accepted exports, provenance and access boundaries. A private composer source project, public runtime asset and public download pack can require different permissions. No second audio-specific storage platform is proposed.

Creator-world export and third-party hosting are particularly consequential: the official service's right to play a recording is not automatically a right for every world creator to copy, sell or host it. Initially keep restricted licensed audio out of creator-export packs unless the license expressly covers that use. A separately cleared official music package can be considered when the product actually needs it. This is a procurement dependency, not a new restriction on engine code or a completed creator-marketplace design.

## Keep the browser delivery practical

PlayCanvas supplies sound slots, positional and nonpositional playback, loops, gain and pitch controls; Web Audio can supply further routing/effects. That supports investigating a small authored soundtrack without first buying a new middleware system. This is an inference from [PlayCanvas's documented capabilities](https://developer.playcanvas.com/user-manual/editor/scenes/components/sound/), not evidence that Open Legend already integrates them.

The first implementation brief should cover:

- User-gesture audio activation and recovery after browser/tab suspension. Autoplay rules affect both Web Audio and media elements. [MDN autoplay guidance](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/Media/Guides/Autoplay).
- Separate music, effects, ambience and optional voice controls; comfortable default levels; no loss of essential information when muted.
- Loading only the needed music and nearby sound families, predictable failure behavior, and stopping owned loops when their source or scene ends.
- Clean musical entry/exit and sparse transitions. Synchronized multitrack playback is an additional requirement to prove if selected, not a consequence of possessing stems.
- Actual supported-browser and device checks for codec decoding, loop seams, memory, initial transfer, long sessions and overlapping sounds. Source masters and delivery encodings serve different purposes.

**Illustrative calculation, not a measurement:** 30 minutes of stereo audio at 160 kbit/s is about 36 MB of compressed payload. Decoding it all into 48 kHz, stereo, 32-bit float buffers is about 691 MB before other overhead; four full-length stems approach 2.76 GB. The calculation is duration × sample rate × channels × four bytes, using the [documented AudioBuffer representation](https://developer.mozilla.org/en-US/docs/Web/API/AudioBuffer). Actual encoders, browser resampling, allocation and caching differ. The lesson is to load/stream deliberately, not to impose these illustrative numbers as fixed engine limits.

FMOD has an [HTML5 platform](https://www.fmod.com/docs/2.04/api/platforms-html5.html), so browser delivery is not an argument that middleware is impossible. Evaluate it only if concrete authoring, transition or mixing needs outweigh integration, licensing and delivery cost. Do not import a Unity tutorial as proof of a ready-made PlayCanvas integration. An engine switch is not an audio-sourcing requirement.
