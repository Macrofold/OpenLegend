# Character-production strategy research brief

Research request recorded November 11, 2026, with the five requested scope amendments. [Research report](2026-11-11-strategy.md) · [Topic and current owners](../../README.md). This record preserves the supplied research request and historical evidence locators; it does not independently authorize implementation or provider work.

Research and recommend the best overall strategy for creating visually convincing, continuously adjustable game characters from concept art.

Your primary assignment is to determine what approach we should adopt going forward. Evaluate the full landscape of established character tools, authored morph libraries, scan-derived models, anatomical datasets, image-to-3D workflows, template fitting, sculpting practices and AI-assisted production.

Treat our current implementation and failures below as a detailed case study. Use them to understand our artistic goals, expose weak assumptions and identify approaches likely to fail again. The research should remain independent of our current implementation choices.

We are willing to change our body foundation, authoring tools, target-generation method, transfer process or division of work between AI and human artists. MetaHuman and Meshy are candidates to evaluate. Existing investment in either does not establish that it is the best long-term choice.

The central questions are:

1. What is the most reliable, practical way to obtain excellent anatomical body and face geometry that matches our artistic references?
2. How should we obtain convincing corresponding shapes for continuous variation in build, fat distribution, muscularity, definition, proportions and age?
3. Which existing tools, datasets, morph libraries and deformation systems already solve substantial parts of this?
4. Where can AI perform the work effectively, what tools and references does it need, and where does skilled human intervention offer better results?
5. How can the resulting characters support reusable clothing and animation without repeatedly rebuilding the system?

First evaluate viable strategies on their own merits. Then assess how our existing work could fit the recommended strategy.

The implementation details, numerical test matrix and tool suggestions below describe our current experiment and intended capabilities. You may recommend a different authoring approach or a smaller, more decisive initial experiment. Explain how it would establish the capabilities we ultimately need.

Avoid spending the report on adjusting our existing coordinates, smoothing coefficients or individual mesh defects. We want to know which production method deserves further investment.

Research broadly and deeply. Ground recommendations in actual documentation, implementations, demonstrated results and practitioner experience. Identify unsupported assumptions and practical limitations.

## 1. Our project and intended character system

We are building OpenLegend, a game with an existing browser renderer and an existing playable world.

We want reusable character bodies with continuous controls for:

- Masculine and feminine body foundations.
- Apparent age.
- Height.
- Structural frame, including shoulder span, ribcage breadth/depth and pelvis breadth.
- Proportions, including leg-to-torso ratio, arm length, neck length and relevant regional widths.
- Muscle mass.
- Fullness, meaning fat volume and distribution.
- Muscle definition.
- Useful local anatomy adjustments.
- Face identity and facial features.
- Skin and eye appearance.
- Separate hair, beard and eyebrows.
- Modular clothing, armor and accessories.

Presets should store editable selections and control values. Named examples such as “runner,” “lifter” or “Peacock” should remain ordinary recipes within a shared system.

The game already has machinery for appearance recipes, corresponding body targets, interpolation, combination corrections, garment fitting, skeletons, animations and rendering. MetaHuman is an offline authoring source. Supported character creation and variation must run in OpenLegend without Unreal, MetaHuman or provider calls at runtime.

We are early in development. We want a practical route to convincing playable characters, with reasonable extensibility and performance. Avoid proposing a large speculative architecture.

## 2. The artistic target and why Meshy matters

Our first demanding target is a fantasy character called the Peacock Mercenary.

The approved artwork shows a fit, muscular adult man with a distinctive face and detailed hair, beard, clothing and armor. He is lean enough that his anatomical structure is readable. He should not become bulky or overweight simply because armor makes the clothed silhouette larger.

We generated additional body-reference artwork and a bald, clean-shaven head sheet to separate anatomy from costume and facial hair.

Meshy models generated directly from the reference images generally captured much more of the intended character than the AI’s manually scripted approximation:

- More convincing overall character and silhouette.
- Better placement and form of major muscle groups.
- More detailed surface relief.
- More faithful facial structure in some regions.
- Much richer clothing and armor detail, texture and fit.
- Hair and beard closer to the original design.

However, Meshy outputs have real problems:

- Damaged or polygonal facial geometry.
- Local anatomical and topology defects.
- Clothing fused into the apparent body.
- Uncertain anatomy beneath clothing.
- Inconsistent correspondence between generated characters.
- Rigging and animation suitability requiring further work.

We want to preserve what makes the generated model visually convincing while obtaining a reusable, controllable, rigged character.

Evaluate Meshy as a source, reference or intermediate. The objective is visual fidelity and a useful character system, rather than loyalty to a particular provider.

We have explored fitting the canonical body and head toward Meshy geometry, including MetaHuman’s From Custom Mesh workflows.

A central problem has been loss of convincing anatomy during fitting:

- Overall outlines became closer in some passes.
- Sculpted pecs, abs, obliques, shoulders, back and limbs became flattened or softened.
- Important muscle boundaries and volumes disappeared.
- Some corrections retained too much of the old recipient body.
- Other attempts reintroduced the donor’s overly narrow waist despite earlier artwork-based correction.
- Neck transitions developed bulges, protrusions or unnatural contours.
- The seat projected excessively.
- Different mesh detail levels sometimes failed to preserve the same dimensions or relief.

We need to understand which stages cause this loss and which established workflows can transfer broad shape, anatomical placement and surface relief while preserving useful canonical correspondence.

Do not assume that transferring geometry transfers MetaHuman’s entire native adjustment system.

## 4. How the current sliders were constructed

This is a crucial part of the failure.

The AI authored custom mathematical deformation fields and saved their outputs as shape targets. Examples include:

- Sideways torso expansion controlled by vertical coordinate.
- Forward and backward expansion curves.
- Radial expansion around limb centrelines.
- Local hip and seat expansion.
- Mesh smoothing to reduce definition.
- Regional masks to protect joints or avoid intersections.
- Additional local corrective displacements.

One current fullness generator uses prescribed coefficients reaching approximately:

- 50 mm of lateral expansion per side in the abdominal region.
- 72 mm of forward abdominal expansion.
- 37 mm of rear torso expansion.
- Additional local hip/seat movement.
- Radial arm and leg expansion.
- Regionally weighted smoothing and extra fullness/definition corrections.

These are coefficients within weighted fields, not uniform final measurements.

Some locations were anchored to mesh landmarks or previous measurements. The expansion amounts, distributions and many boundaries were AI-authored heuristics. We have no established derivation tying those particular values to a validated fuller-body reference or anatomical dataset.

The system then interpolates those targets and adds combination corrections.

The result can be technically valid and still look awful.

We need you to investigate better sources of deformation knowledge. Generic advice to “use blend shapes,” “adjust proportions” or “add corrective morphs” does not answer this problem. We already have those mechanisms.

The missing piece is how to obtain high-quality, anatomically appropriate target shapes and interactions reliably.

## 5. Specific visual failures

Our current fuller bodies can show:

- A rectangular torso with weak distinction between ribcage, abdomen, flanks and pelvis.
- An abrupt lower-abdominal ledge or shelf.
- A horizontal ledge across the back/flanks in earlier versions.
- Broadly blurred torso anatomy beside sharply defined limbs.
- Arms that read as enlarged muscular arms rather than plausibly fuller arms.
- Inflated or disconnected shoulders and upper arms.
- Exaggerated ribcages and abrupt chest-to-waist transitions at high muscle mass.
- Loss of muscle definition in obliques, shoulder blades and knees.
- Neck bulges and awkward jaw-to-neck transitions.
- Unconvincing seat projection and back curvature.

These are examples. The workflow must identify problems across the whole person without relying on the user to point out every defect.

A broad waist can be anatomically appropriate. We are asking for plausible tissue distribution, connected forms and convincing transitions at each selected build.

There have also been clothing and motion failures:

- Skin breaking through raised sleeves.
- Buckled or locally folded shirt surfaces.
- Inflated-looking shirt waists.
- Garments intensifying existing body defects.
- Backward-bending elbows from animation transfer.
- Arms intersecting fuller torsos.
- A clearance workaround that made the arm swing visibly too wide.

Body shape changes affect garment fitting and motion. We have spent too much effort repairing downstream fit while the underlying bodies still visibly failed.

## 6. The bounded proof we are trying to establish

These are our current experimental recipes and intended coverage. Use them to understand the range we need. Recommend the most effective staged validation plan for your preferred strategy, including a smaller initial experiment where appropriate.

The first body family is an adult masculine base, with the approved Peacock body as the reference recipe at:

- Muscle mass 50%.
- Fullness 0%.
- Definition 100%.

These percentages locate the source within an authored parameter range. They are not medical measurements.

The controls have distinct intended meanings:

- Muscle mass changes the volume of muscle groups.
- Fullness changes fat volume and its distribution.
- Definition changes the visibility and separation of muscular forms at the selected mass and fullness.

The required matrix contains 30 recipes:

- Mass at 0%, 50% and 100%.
- Fullness at 0% and 100%.
- Definition at 0%, 25%, 50%, 75% and 100%.

It also includes continuous sweeps through intermediate values:

- Definition at each mass/fullness combination.
- Fullness at each of the three mass settings with definition at 0%, 50% and 100%.
- Mass at fullness 0%, 50% and 100%, with definition at 0%, 50% and 100%.

The eventual bounded extension includes independent masculine and feminine adult bases at apparent ages 30 and 75, their midpoint at 52.5, and continuous age interpolation.

For each base and age, the intended sample combinations are:

- Mass 0, fullness 0, definition 50.
- Mass 100, fullness 0, definition 100.
- Mass 0, fullness 100, definition 0.
- Mass 100, fullness 100, definition 50.

This gives 24 age/base samples and eight age sweeps.

Age must affect appropriate body and facial geometry while retaining identity and fixed stature for the comparison. An older athletic person must remain visibly athletic. Wrinkles, grey hair, softened muscles or an automatic stoop alone do not establish whole-body ageing.

The age/base extension remains unqualified. Research how to approach it without propagating bad adult endpoints.

Evaluate generated-geometry-to-canonical transfer as one possible strategy. Establish when it is preferable to using an existing character generator, an authored morph library, a scan-based foundation or artist-created geometry. Investigate the following transfer methods where they support a competitive overall workflow.

Compare established workflows involving:

- Non-rigid registration and template fitting.
- Landmark-assisted correspondence.
- Wrap tools and retopology transfer.
- Cage deformation and surface deformation.
- Scan fitting.
- Multiresolution sculpt transfer.
- Projection of geometry, displacement and normal detail.
- Skeleton and skin-weight adjustment following shape transfer.

Investigate tools such as Wrap, Blender tools/add-ons, Maya workflows, ZBrush workflows and other relevant systems. Verify their current suitability and automation limits.

Explain how a good workflow handles:

- Different poses between source and recipient.
- Different arm positions across reference images.
- Stature and proportion alignment.
- Canonical vertices, connectivity and UV preservation.
- Muscle boundary alignment.
- Transfer of broad volumes and local relief.
- Fused shorts and concealed anatomy.
- Mesh defects around joints.
- Head/body scale and neck continuity.
- LOD reduction without flattening anatomy.
- Changes to joint positions, skin weights and bind data.

Would it be more effective to generate several reference-grounded bodies, then fit each to a common template? Could that produce more convincing slider targets than deriving every extreme mathematically from one lean source?

Identify the correspondence and interpolation risks in that approach. Do not assume independently generated bodies will blend successfully.

- Reference-driven Blender, ZBrush, Maya or Unreal workflows.

- Useful agent skills, prompts or instruction packages.
- Ways to preserve visual reference information throughout execution.
- Automated matched-camera comparisons.
- Landmark, silhouette and surface comparison workflows.
- Feedback loops that combine measurements with visual judgement.
- Methods for preventing an agent from repeatedly making plausible-sounding but anatomically poor edits.

For each promising workflow, establish what was actually demonstrated. Identify the human intervention required and whether the published result includes multiple views, intermediate shapes and animation.

Do not assume all procedural modelling is unreliable. Distinguish our improvised fields from established, validated procedural or statistical models.

## 13. Required output

Produce a strategic research report with these deliverables:

1. **A clear overall recommendation.**
   State which character-production strategy you recommend, why it is the strongest fit for our goals, and the material tradeoffs.

2. **A comparison of credible alternative strategies.**
   Consider established character generators and morph ecosystems, scan-based or statistical body models, generated geometry fitted to canonical topology, artist-authored targets, and hybrid approaches. Include other promising approaches discovered during research.

   Compare visual quality, artistic fidelity, controllability, clothing compatibility, animation, automation, manual effort, licensing, cost and maintainability.

3. **An explanation of the anatomical knowledge each strategy uses.**
   Identify where its proportions, tissue distribution and shape variation come from. Distinguish artist-authored shapes, measured data, learned body models, generated geometry and heuristic deformations. Explain the limitations of each source.

4. **An assessment of AI's useful role.**
   Find concrete demonstrations of effective AI-assisted character work. Explain which tasks can be automated reliably, which require visual supervision, and where a character artist would provide the greatest value.

   Include useful tools, plugins, repositories, agent skills, prompts, tutorials and practitioner discussions.

5. **A recommended end-to-end production workflow.**
   Cover concept/reference development, body and face creation, correspondence, adjustable targets, interactions, rigging, clothing fit, animation, export and visual acceptance.

   Explain exactly how convincing endpoint geometry would be obtained.

6. **A diagnosis of the recurring failures illustrated by our case study.**
   Identify the general causes and how the proposed workflow prevents them. Use specific examples where helpful, while keeping the focus on reusable production practices.

7. **A decisive initial proof.**
   Propose the smallest experiment that can meaningfully test the recommended strategy against our current results. Specify inputs, tools, expected outputs, visual comparisons, acceptance criteria, estimated effort and stop conditions.

   The experiment should establish visual quality early enough to prevent extensive work on a poor foundation.

8. **A realistic adoption plan.**
   Explain what existing work remains reusable, what needs replacement, what later refinements would invalidate, and what dependencies should be settled before wardrobe production.

9. **Reusable instructions for AI-assisted execution and review.**
   Provide practical prompts or workflow instructions grounded in the recommended tools. Require direct reference comparison, whole-character visual inspection and correction of obvious failures before downstream work.

10. **Evidence, uncertainty and costs.**
    Link sources supporting major recommendations. Distinguish documented capabilities, demonstrated workflows and your own proposals. Identify licensing constraints, unavailable evidence, likely manual work and realistic cost ranges.

Prioritize the overall strategy decision. A detailed repair plan for our current procedural body generator alone would not satisfy this assignment.
